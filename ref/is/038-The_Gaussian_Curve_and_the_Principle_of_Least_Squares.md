## The Gaussian Curve and the Principle of Least Squares

Laplace’s work remained the most influential book on mathematical probability until the end of the 19th century, when **Carl Friedrich Gauss** (1777–1855) advanced Laplace’s idea in explicitly probabilistic terms. The result of this work was eventually (and somewhat erroneously) named the “Gaussian curve”, since it had already been discovered by Laplace.

![image](./images/00076.jpeg)

I acknowledged my debt to Laplace and made use of the law of probability in my work on the theory of the motion of heavenly bodies. And I reached the principle of least squares in 1809\. But I had already discovered in 1805.

The principle of least squares, based on the theory of errors, had been devised in the early 19th century by such mathematicians and astronomers as Gauss, Laplace and Legendre to determine, for example, the shape of the Earth. It would find its greatest use in statistics at the end of the 19th century for interpreting statistical regression (see [here](./077-George_Udny_Yule_and_the_Method_of_Least_Squares.md#pg128)).
