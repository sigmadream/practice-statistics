## Ratio and Interval

The American psychologist **Stanley Smith Stevens** (1906–73) made a further sub-division with “continuous variables” in 1947 when he introduced _ratio_ and _interval_ scales of measurement (most of Pearson’s continuous variables were ratio). Stevens proposed the following:

1\. **Ratio scales**

These differ from interval variables (see [here](./066-Nominal_and_Ordinal_Variables.md#pg111)) in two ways: a) an absolute zero indicates the absence of the property being measured (i.e. height, weight and blood pressure) and b) ratio scales are additive.

![image](./images/00173.jpeg)

Thus, one can say that something is “twice as tall” or “three times the distance”. As metric and imperial tools are absolute measures, the difference between 3 feet and 6 feet is identical to a metric reading of the difference between 0.91 metres and 1.81 metres. Both are twice as long.

2\. **Interval scales**

The zero point is arbitrary and does not reflect the absence of an attribute (such as 0 Celsius and 0 Fahrenheit readings).

![image](./images/00174.jpeg)

Thus, one doesn’t say that it’s “twice as hot” if the temperature increases from 10°C to 20°C. Since temperature is a relative measure, and the different scales are arbitrary, they are not comparable. Converting Celsius to Fahrenheit would produce a reading of 50°F to 68F°, which is just a bit warmer, but not twice as hot.

![image](./images/00175.jpeg)

**Correlation** one of the most widely used statistical methods, indicates the extent to which two variables go together (e.g., height and weight). The most common type measures a linear relationship between two variables, and refers to how well they go together in a straight line.

But not every pair of characters or variables can be assessed by using a statistical correlation, and different methods of correlation are used within the biological, medical, behavioural, social and environmental sciences, as well as in industry, commerce, economics and education.

Different types of correlational methods are used for different types of variables, depending on the scale of measurement.

![image](./images/00176.jpeg)

Variables can be nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio. And data can be ranked and can take on categories such as binary (0,1) and dichotomous (two mutually exclusive values), which require the use of very specific methods of correlation.
