## Simple Correlation and Multiple Correlation

Pearson introduced the term **simple correlation** when measuring a linear relationship between _two_ continuous variables only, such as the relationship between stature of father and stature of son.

![image](./images/00208.jpeg)

When became interested in measuring the relationships between characters in more than two generations, I needed a different set of statistical procedures. I had already dealt with statistical correlations of three variables in 1892, which I expressed as “Galton’s functions”, named by Weldon in 1889.

![image](./images/00209.jpeg)

When I offered a mathematical resolution for Galton’s work, I devised the mathematical structure for multiple correlation, symbolized by R,

This work provided the basis for the development of **multiple regression.** Like simple regression, it involves a linear prediction, but rather than using only one variable to be “predicted”, a collection of variables can be used instead.

![image](./images/00210.jpeg)

To calculate the multiple correlation coefficient, Pearson introduced a higher form of mathematics. This played a pivotal role in the professionalization of mathematical statistics as an academic discipline at the end of the 19th century. Pearson learnt this type of mathematics at Cambridge from J.J. Sylvester and **Arthur Cayley** (1821–95), who had created matrix algebra out of their discovery of the theory of invariants during the mid-19th century.

![image](./images/00211.jpeg)

It wasn’t until the 1930s that Pearson’s higher form of mathematics was replaced by matrix algebra in mathematical statistics. Matrix algebra has remained central to multivariate statistics.

This higher level of mathematics enabled statisticians to find complex mathematical solutions for statistical problems in a multivariate (or _p-_dimensional) space when a bivariate (or two-dimensional) system was insufficient.
