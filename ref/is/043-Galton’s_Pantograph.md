## Galton’s Pantograph

Inspired by Quetelet, Galton became so committed to the idea of a ubiquitous normal curve that he created a mechanical device, a modified pantograph, to stretch or squeeze any figure in two directions.

![image](./images/00089.jpeg)

This meant that I could stretch any shaped curve to fit into the normal curve.

![image](./images/00090.jpeg)

This uncompromising belief in the normal curve would become divisive between the older school of vital statistics and the new, emergent one of mathematical statistics. Such was the tyranny of the normal curve that by the end of the 19th century most statisticians assumed that no other curve could be used to describe data. But this monolithic view would be challenged by Pearson in the last decade of the century.
