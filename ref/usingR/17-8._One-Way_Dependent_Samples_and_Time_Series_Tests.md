## Abstract

This chapter introduces fundamental statistical methods for analyzing change over time within the same group, emphasizing dependent measures. Researchers often measure repeated outcomes, such as student reading scores across a school year, to determine whether scores change over multiple time points. For comparisons between two time points, the dependent (paired) samples t-test provides a straightforward method, while repeated measures ANOVA extends this approach to three or more time points. Key assumptions (including independence of observations, normality of differences, and scale-level measurement) are discussed, along with strategies for checking these assumptions in R using histograms, Q-Q plots, and Shapiro-Wilk tests. If assumptions are violated, this chapter introduces nonparametric Wilcoxon _V_ for paired samples and Friedman’s test for repeated measures as well as non-parametric post-hoc and effect size tests.

## 8.1 Detecting Differences over Dependent Measures

The previous chapter covered instances when a researcher is interested in differences between two separate populations. For example, our astronaut wanted to know if there was a difference in ACT and HS GPA between Earthlings, Martians, and Venusians. In this instance, we are comparing values across different groups. Hence, we may use independent tests. For example, an independent samples t-test examined the difference in these scores between Earthlings and Martians, two samples that are independent from each other. One-way ANOVA does not have the word “independent” in its title, but it also assumes independent samples.

Researchers are frequently interested in change over time within the same group. For example, a classroom teacher might be interested to see if their students’ reading scores improved between fall and the spring. Moreover, they may be interested to see if they changed over the summer. Thus, that teacher has three different time periods: (a) the fall (pre), (b) the spring (post) and (c) the summer (postpost). If that teacher was interested in measuring the change between two of those time periods (say fall and spring) they could use a dependent samples t-test, often called a paired-samples t-test (Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)). If that teacher was interested in change over three periods, instead of running different combinations of paired samples t-tests (fall to spring, spring to summer, fall to summer) they would run a repeated measures ANOVA (Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)). The purpose of this chapter is to introduce you to these fundamental approaches to time-series analysis and teach you how to conduct them on R.

Before we continue, it is important to note that researchers are typically not interested in examining just one group over time. Experimental designs often focus on multiple groups across different interventions or treatments. In these cases, researchers look at within-group effects (comparing a group to itself over time), between-group effects (comparing different groups to one another), and how changes within groups vary across time. These statistical tests require more advanced models, such as mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) and analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), which later books will cover. For now, we will focus on examining single groups over time (Leech et al., [2015](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)).

## 8.2 Dependent (Paired) Samples t-Test

Our astronaut happens to be interested in how one class of fifth graders changed in their reading ability between the beginning of the school year and the end of the school year. For simplicity purposes, we have sampled 20 students for these analyses. Since there are two iterations of data, the researcher will first collect reading scores at the beginning of the school year (pretest) in August or early September, wait until the year ends, and collect reading scores at the end of the year (posttest). We will assume that the reading test is the same test at the beginning of the year as the end of the year and has strong evidence of validity, and that the students took the test seriously. Of course, in the “real world” the latter assumption might not be true.

After the posttest data were collected, the researcher compiled them in her database and conducted a dependent samples t-test (Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)). Her research question was: _Is there a difference in reading scores for these students at the end of the year as compared to the beginning of the year_? The null hypothesis was that there was **no change** in reading score. There are two alternative hypotheses. One is that the reading scores were higher. The other is that the reader scores were lower. The astronaut decided to run a two-tailed test because they were interested in any direction of change in reading score.

Equation ([8.1](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) shows how _t_ is calculated for a dependent (paired) samples t-tests. The equation begins by calculating the difference between each pair of scores. For example, you subtract pretest for student one from posttest for student one and you do that for all the students and then average them to produce D¯. Next, you calculate the standard error of the difference, or SD/n, which is the standard deviation of the difference divided by the square root of the sample size (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Mara & Cribbie, [2012](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)).

t\=D¯SD/n

(8.1)

### 8.2.1 Statistical Assumptions of Paired Samples t-Test

Our researcher had to first check some statistical assumptions of dependent samples t-test. The first of which is **independence of observations**, meaning that each pair of measurements comes from the same participant and observations were not influenced by other observations (so the pretest did not influence the posttest). The second assumption is **normality of differences**, which examines the skewness and kurtosis of the differences in scores between pretest and posttest. The third is a **large enough sample size for statistical** power. This can be checked on R or online. The fourth is that **both measurements are scale variables. If they are not scale, R will not run the statistical test because it will read the column as a character or a factor.** Therefore, we sometimes need to check the columns in R to make sure they are numeric (Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Mara & Cribbie, [2012](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Ross & Willson, [2017](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)).

## 8.3 Non-parametric Dependent Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test

If the assumption of normality is violated or of one or both observations have significant outliers, there is a non-parametric test that can be conducted instead. The Wilcoxon signed rank test calculates the difference between paired observations. Equation ([8.2](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) gives how Wilcoxon _V_ is calculated. _Y_ _i_ is the second observation given the sample; _X_ _i_ is the first observation given the sample. _D_ _i_ is _Y_ _i_ − _X_ _i_. _R_ _i_ is the rank of |_Di_|. The first step is to calculate the difference between _Y_ _i_ and _X_ _i_ (post minus pre). Next, convert each value (_Di_) to absolute values (|_Di_|), which represents the literal difference of each value from zero (for example, −1 = |1| and 1 = |1|). Next, rank the absolute values of the difference where the lowest absolute value = _R_1 \= 1, the second lowest _R_2 \= 2, etc. Third, you assign negative values to all the ranks that were originally negative numbers. Then you sum them to get _V_ (Bauer, [1972](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); R Core Team, [2020](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)). Wilcoxen’s _V_ does not use degrees of freedom. Instead, one uses the sample size with _V_ on a table to compare to critical alphas to determine statistical significance. Luckily, R determines this automatically and does all the calculations.

V\=∑D1\>1RI

(8.2)

## 8.4 Repeated Measures ANOVA

Frequently in experimental designs or other circumstances, it is desirable to measure the same value, or score given a sample across multiple iterations. It is also often desirable to measure three or more different scores with the same scale or standardized (converted to _Z_ scores) to the same scale. To achieve this, one could use repeated measures (or dependent samples) ANOVA. Simply put, this is similar to paired-samples t-test, only we add one or more measurements to the model (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Leech et al., [2015](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)).

For example, our astronaut is interested in adding another wave of data to her analysis using t-test. Instead of examining the longitudinal change of fifth grade student reading scores between pretest and posttest, she added another wave of post-posttest to see if there was a difference in score after the summer to answer the research question: _Was there a change in reading scores between the fall, end of year, and after the summer?_ To calculate this, she will employ repeated measures ANOVA, but first we need to examine how it is calculated.

### 8.4.1 Statistical Assumptions of Repeated Measures ANOVA

There are four statistical assumptions. First, **independence of observations** requires that each subject be measured only once per condition. This can be confirmed by ensuring that the data set represents independent observations, meaning that no participant is measured twice under the same condition. **Second, scale variables shared by the same sample** imply that each subject undergoes three or more repeated measures (conditions) on the same continuous scale. For example, in an educational study, students might be tested before, immediately after, and sometime after an intervention. Third, **normality** assumes that the dependent variable follows a normal distribution at each condition. This can be tested in R using the Shapiro-Wilk for each condition, or by inspecting Q-Q plots. Finally, **sphericity** assumes that the variances of the differences between all pairs of conditions are equal. In R, we test sphericity using Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity. If the test indicates a violation of sphericity, corrections such as the Greenhouse-Geisser adjustment can be applied. If statistical assumptions are severely violated, there is a non-parametric Friedman test that can be applied.

### 8.4.2 Calculating Repeated Measures ANOVA

Equation ([8.3](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) shows the steps in calculating repeated measures ANOVA. The numerator of the far left is the mean squares condition (MSC) and represents the sum of the differences between each time iteration divided by the number of iterations minus one. The denominator, mean square error (MSE), is a bit more complicated. It calculates the sum of squares for subjects (SSS), which subtracts the average of each subject’s measurements across all time conditions and subtracts that from the grand mean. The SSS is then subtracted from the sum of squares within (SSW), which accounts for the variability of the measurements within each subject across time conditions. This difference, known as the residual sum of squares, reflects the variation that cannot be attributed to the treatment or condition differences but is due to individual differences or random errors.

The within-subjects sum of squares (SSW) is computed by examining how each participant’s individual scores deviate from their condition mean across all time points. Each deviation is squared, and these squared deviations are summed across all participants and conditions. This captures the total within-subject variability. Next, the sum of squares for subjects (SSS), which represents the portion of that variability attributable to stable individual differences, is subtracted from SSW. The remaining variability reflects fluctuations not explained by consistent subject effects or by the model itself. This residual sum of squares is then divided by its corresponding degrees of freedom to yield the mean square error (MSE), which estimates the error variance within subjects.

Finally, the ratio of MSC to MSE produces the _F_\-statistic for the ANOVA test, allowing for the comparison of the variability due to the treatment or condition (MSC) against the residual variability (MSE). If the _F_\-statistic is large, it suggests that the time conditions or treatments have a significant effect on the outcome (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)).

## 8.5 Non-parametric Freidman Test for Repeated Measures

If the assumptions of normality and sphericity were violated, we could conduct the non-parametric Friedman test to see if the rank differences between the three waves of data are different. Keep in mind that much literature suggests that ANOVA tests are robust to violations of normality (Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)), and the statistician can address issues of normality and variance with data transformations (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)). These transformations may change the interpretation of the results a bit, but this can be converted after the analysis. Nevertheless, if violations of assumptions are strong in repeated measures, the Friedman test is an option.

Equation [8.5](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter) shows how Friedman’s _Q_ statistic is calculated where n is the number of individuals, _k_ is the number of data iterations and R is the total ranks for each column of data. _R_ reports the data as a Friedman chi-squared value. Degrees of freedom (_df_) are calculated as _k_ − 1\. Note, the _Q_ is sometimes reported as an _F_, _T_ or _M_ in some literature (Corder & Foreman, [2014](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Fadeyi, [2021](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Fahoome, [2002](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter); Pereira et al., [2015](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)).

Q\=12nkk+1∑j\=1kRj2−3nk+1

(8.5)

### 8.5.1 Effect Sizes and Post-hoc with Friedman

Friedman uses _Kendall’s W_ omnibus effect size. If desired, you could report an _η_2 with this equation: χ2χ2+n−1, which can easily be programmed in R. _Kendal’s W_ is a bit more complex, as shown in Eq. ([8.6](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) as it uses the ranks, group numbers, and sample sizes to compute. However, _rstatix_ has it programmed with the _friedman\_effsize_ command (Kassambara, [2023](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)). The similarity between Eqs. ([8.5](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) and ([8.6](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) is interesting as ([8.6](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)) takes the right half of Eq. ([8.5](A668427_1_En_8_Chapter)), places it in the denominator after squaring _k_ and then adjusts the denominator.

W\=12∑j\=1kRj2−3nk2+1nk2−kk−1

(8.6)

To conduct post-hoc tests use the Wilcox test and program a Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes can be calculated by computing or programming ∣V∣n, if _|V|_ \> 0\. If _|V|_ \= 0, then this effect size is not possible to calculate.

## 8.6 Upgrades to Time Series Tests

For outcomes measured over time or under repeated conditions, the next book in the series will extend one-way ANOVA to designs that capture within-subject effects and interactions across time. Mixed ANOVA combines repeated measures with between-subjects factors, enabling the analysis of temporal changes while accounting for group differences. Repeated measures MANOVA and mixed MANOVA extend this approach to multiple dependent variables, providing a framework for examining complex longitudinal or repeated-measures data. Together, these methods equip researchers to explore patterns that unfold across time or conditions, offering a richer understanding of change and stability in social science research.
