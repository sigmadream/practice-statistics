## Abstract

This chapter explores the historical evolution of statistical computing and the emergence of large language models (LLMs) within the context of R programming. It traces the development of statistical methods from early census records in ancient civilizations through advancements in the Enlightenment and the establishment of modern statistical societies, highlighting the exponential growth in computational capabilities following the invention of the printing press and early computers. The chapter situates LLMs, such as ChatGPT, Claude, and LLaMA, as both transformative and disruptive tools for researchers, emphasizing their potential to accelerate coding and analysis while raising ethical, reproducibility, and validity concerns. A framework for responsible and effective use of LLMs in statistical research is presented, focusing on four core competencies: mastery of underlying statistical mathematics, proficiency in coding languages, effective prompt engineering, and rigorous verification of AI-generated code. Practical applications for LLMs are illustrated, including debugging, writing functions, ensuring correct syntax, and automating repetitive tasks. The chapter concludes with guidance for maintaining researcher authenticity, integrity, and critical thinking while leveraging the efficiency and democratization afforded by LLMs, encouraging ongoing discourse in the integration of AI within statistical practice.

## 10.1 The Evolution of Knowledge and Tools for the Researcher

The first version of the printing press was completed in 1454 by Johannes Gutenberg (Treasures of the McDonald Collection—Special Collections & Archives Research Center, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Before the printing press, books were painstakingly hand-copied, often by monks working in monastery scriptoriums. These scribes devoted countless hours to producing manuscripts—most commonly Bibles or other religious texts—for a very limited literate audience, typically members of the Church or the aristocracy. Each book was a labor-intensive work of art, sometimes illuminated with intricate decorations, and could take months or even years to complete. Access to knowledge was therefore slow, localized, and tightly controlled, making books rare symbols of authority and learning (Manuscript Bibles from the Middle Ages | CSUN University Library, [2016](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)).

After the printing press, literacy rates began to steadily rise over the centuries because access to books increased. The printing press was also a key factor in the development of the Renaissance and the Enlightenment, ideas which inspired the American Revolution and the rise of Democratic ideals in the world (Eisenstein, [1983](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). It is reasonable to hypothesize that the expansion of access to information and rising literacy rates contributed directly to the conditions that made the American Revolution possible (Parkinson, [2015](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Just over three centuries after the invention of technology that dramatically broadened the availability of knowledge, a novel experiment in governance emerged. Since that time, the circulation and accessibility of information have been central to civil rights, the feminist movement, and broader political discourse—both in the United States and globally.

During my career as a researcher, the greatest barriers to statistical knowledge relate to mathematical literacy, understanding the underlying mechanics of statistical processes, and knowledge of how statistical tools generate results reported in articles, books, and dissertations. The recent rise of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Claude, LLaMA, and others (CLAUDE (Version 4.5), [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter); Meta, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter); OpenAI, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) has prompted many in data science and academia to reconsider how these tools should be approached. In data science, the response has been cautiously progressive, reluctant yet open, embracing their potential, while remaining anxious about the implications for human roles in data analysis and decision-making (Eigner & Händler, [2024](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Academia, by contrast, has exhibited a dual response: on one hand, suspicion, and avoidance; on the other, curiosity and adoption. Critics worry that reliance on LLMs could compromise the validity of outputs, potentially leading to a rise in AI-driven competence alongside a decline in human expertise and knowledge (Jung et al., [2024](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter); Stadler et al., [2024](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)).

Thus, LLMs exemplify a paradox that has accompanied every major technological innovation: They improve human existence, and they present dangers to human existence. The magnitude of the improvements and dangers are a function of the level of advancement of the individual technology. For example, the invention of the automobile and the utilization of the assembly line improved human mobility and thus many related areas, invented new jobs and economic opportunities, but also created new forms of pollution and decreased the employment of blacksmiths, farriers, and other related workers. The question for AI and LLMs is: What are the long-term benefits, and what are the long-term risks?

This chapter does not ponder these opportunities and challenges for AI and LLMs on a global scale but instead contextualizes it to the individual researcher using R statistical software. Many of these ponderings will cross into other areas and programming languages (e.g., Python, Java). One option is for the R programmer or the data scientist to deny themself any use of LLMs in their programming, insisting on prior methods of utilizing books online resources, and “the school of hard knocks” to develop their projects. This would ensure purity of their work. Unfortunately, it would also put them behind the rest of the culture, which is, even as I write this chapter, utilizing LLMs in R coding.

Another option is for the R programmer to fully embrace LLMs and use them to develop their statistical projects strictly and purely with complete faith that the code produced works perfectly, thereby copying, pasting, running, and reporting the results on blind faith. This would not be a good idea for a few reasons. First, at a practical level, LLMs are imperfect and have often been shown to “cheat” or hallucinate their code, rendering deceptive results that are inaccurate. If the community of data science and R programmers did this, we would live in a world of false statistical outputs and information, and we know the dangers of accumulated errors. Second, this is clearly inauthentic and unethical and comparable to plagiarism and misrepresentation of work. This is an issue many academic journals, publications, colleges, universities, and other entities are grappling with. Finally, and most importantly, this would be a fundamental betrayal of the researcher’s own dignity and self, an intrinsic violation where the R programmer no longer cares about their own originality, foundation of knowledge and substance, but is merely a human typing prompts to an AI machine to pump out substance-lacking material devoid of authentic representation of the programmer’s or scholar’s mind. A decision to rely solely on LLMs is a decision to surrender to an algorithm and declare it more valid and competent than the self, which means you remove all human critical thinking, judgement, and value. Somebody once asked if a room full of monkeys had a typewriter if the monkeys could write Hamlet. If a room full of humans had LLMs, could they run models to inform decisions to improve the world at large? I do not think so.

There is a third option, and though I am aware of the fallacy of the middle ground, the middle ground is not always fallacious. The only practical solution to LLMs and their interaction with statistical programming is to first acknowledge that LLMs exist, are faster than humans at coding, and are likely to increase in use. Denying this reality is worse than religious leaders fearing the printing press would result in heresy and rebellion, worse than Edison denying the superiority of alternating current, worse than Ken Olsen claiming that nobody would ever need a personal computer, worse than pretty much any denier of any technological advancement in history. Admittedly, LLMs carry a science-fiction-esque potential for future doom and gloom, and these concerns are valid and call for serious decision makers to take them seriously and build limits and parameters. In the meantime, however, individual R programmers and others need to develop roadmaps and guidelines for using LLMs in coding that ensure human authenticity but also capitalize on the opportunity to democratize statistics and data science and increase efficiency.

That is the purpose of the rest of this chapter—to provide the readers with a roadmap to capitalize on the efficiency and accessibility of LLMs in R programming while addressing the need for ethics and reproducibility of work so that the R programmer, data scientist, or statistician maintains their authenticity, knowledge, and honesty in their work. This chapter will proceed by providing an overview of the evolution of statistical computing and software, followed by an overview of the advent of LLMs and their interactions with coding and statistics, it will then provide practical guidelines and cases for using LLMs with R and conclude with an proposed ethical and pedagogical roadmap. Admittedly, I am writing these ideas while the technology is new to academia and data science. Therefore, I encourage my colleagues and others to expand upon and improve these ideas, even replacing some of them with better ones.

## 10.2 Evolution of Statistics and Software

Early statistical efforts began in ancient times where civilizations such as Egypt and Rome kept census records. The methods of doing this were very rudimentary, but can be considered the first descriptive statistical efforts in human history (Bagnall, [1991](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Like many disciplines, statistics experienced a boost with the Enlightenment when Johan Graunt and William Petty began expanding upon basic census records with mortality rates, population statistical details, and other complex figures (Connor, [2024](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). The mathematician De Moivre developed methods of least squares and probabilistic error laws (Plackett, [1972](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). The 18th and early nineteenth century also saw the development of the normal distribution, though it is often thought that Galileo originally conceptualized it (Stahl, [2006](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Later, the nineteenth century saw significant developments with the works of those such as Quetelet, Galton, and Karl Pearson (whose work we used in earlier chapters) (Jahoda, [2015](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). This is also the century when the Statistical Society of London was founded in 1834 (Hill, [1984](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)).

The above short summary illustrates the exponential development of statistical methodology, which remained static for thousands of years, but suddenly experienced an uptick in development around the Enlightenment, not long after Gutenber’s printing press. The twentieth century saw even more rapid growth in statistical methodologies with development of Fisher’s methods (which are used heavily in previous chapters), Neyman and Egon Pearson’s (not to be confused with Karl), Kolmogorov’s axiom contributions, and the growth of statistical programs and departments across global colleges and Universities (Lehmann, [1993](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)).

Up until the mid-twentieth century, most statics were calculated manually, which limited our capacity to analyze complex problems. For example, imagine hand-calculating an ANOVA for a sample of 1000\. Thus, the most significant development in statistical capabilities, following the growth of literacy due to the printing press, was the advent of computing.

Early computers were massive machines requiring punch cards for calculating statistics. IBM has a fascinating website ([LINK](./https___www.ibm.com_history_punched-card.md)) if you are interested in learning more and seeing one for yourself (The Punched Card | IBM, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Punch cards functioned like data tables, each card containing carefully arranged information that was fed into the machine manually. The computer would process the input according to the selected program and then print the results. The process was unforgiving: a single error or malfunction could undo hours of preparation. One of my professors recalled using such a system during his dissertation research. He described the meticulous effort required to sort the cards correctly, the necessity of scheduling time on the computer well in advance, and the pressure of completing everything within that narrow window. A single misstep meant starting over and rescheduling, jeopardizing his ability to finish the dissertation on time.

Take a moment to ponder the fact that one of the professors of the author of this book, a person who is still teaching and conducting research, used early computing systems for statistical calculations. That same professor now uses R statistical software as well as an IBM product called Statistical Software Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). In the academic journey of one person, which began in the late twentieth century and continued into the early twenty-first, evolved from card-reading room-sized machines to personal computers containing enough RAM to conduct complex statistical problems. Beyond SPSS, there are several other packages. Stat Crunch is a product of Pearson and allows the early statistical pupil access to analytics. Statistical Analysis System (SAS) requires specialized programming knowledge as do STATA (a combination of “stats” and “data”), MPlus (which specializes in psychometrics and latent variable modeling), Python and S (like R is open source and has multiple uses), as well as LISREL (also useful with latent variable analyses). Excel has a data pack that allows for statistical analyses.

Some packages like SPSS and Stat Crunch provided user interfaces (UIs) that allow the user to point, click, and drag variables to build models without any knowledge of programming. Other packages such as STATA, SAS, LISREL, MPlus, S and R (as we know) require the user to know programming language to calculate the statistics.

The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries saw the rise of open-source software tools like R, S, and Python, and the rise of data science, which included highly complex models such as machine learning using massive datasets (or “big data”). These tools have been available to anyone interested at no cost. However, the trade-off has been a difficult curve for the average person wanting to learn the programming languages. When I came to R around 2016 as an institutional researcher, I had the privilege of having already obtained a Ph.D. and I had the leadership and space to learn a new tool. Not to mention that I had some very good mentors in other colleges who helped me with my understanding of the language and with bugs in my coding. Nevertheless, it likely took me 3 years of practice to master R at a basic level, and even then, I faced steep learning curves with more complex features such as the dashboarding package Shiny (Sievert, [2020](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Within a few years of obtaining a level of competency with R that I was satisfied with, ChatGPT emerged and changed the game forever.

## 10.3 Emergence and Evolution of LLM

We cannot begin a discussion of large language models (LLMs) without revisiting the Turing test. In 1950, Alan Turing proposed that if a human could not distinguish between text written by a machine and that written by another person, the machine could be considered intelligent (Pinar Saygin et al., [2000](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter); Turing, [1950](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). This vision set the stage for decades of progress in natural language processing (NLP). Early systems such as ELIZA (Weizenbaum, [1966](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) relied on rule-based pattern matching to simulate conversation, while statistical approaches in the 1990s introduced probabilistic language models, such as n-grams, that captured word co-occurrence patterns. These estimated the probability of each word given the preceding one or more words in a sequence (P. F. Brown et al., [1992](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). These developments laid the groundwork for machine learning methods like decision trees and naïve Bayes, which advanced text classification and sentiment analysis (Jurafsky & Martin, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)).

The deep learning era transformed NLP through distributed word representations and sequence modeling. Distributed word representations organize words in a mathematical space so that those with similar contextual meanings appear closer to one another. Sequence modeling allows the computer to understand the order of words, so it can generate or predict text that makes sense in a sentence. Word embeddings such as Word2Vec (Mikolov et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) and GloVe (Pennington et al., [2014](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) captured semantic relationships between words, enabling context-aware understanding. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, [1997](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) extended this capacity by modeling long-range dependencies in text, allowing systems to generate coherent sequences of language rather than isolated predictions. RNNs and LSTM networks are types of computer models that can ‘remember’ what came earlier in a sentence. This helps the system understand how words relate to each other over longer stretches of text, so it can generate sentences that make sense, instead of just predicting one word at a time without context.

A major breakthrough came with the transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. ([2017](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Unlike older models that had to process words one at a time, transformers use a system called self-attention to look at all the words in a sentence at once. This allows the computer to understand context more efficiently and handle much longer passages of text. Transformers are the foundation for models like GPT (T. Brown et al., [2020](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) and open-source systems such as LLaMA (Touvron et al., [2023](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) and Mistral, which can generate human-like text, answer questions, or even help with coding and research tasks.

For researchers and practitioners in statistics and data science, LLMs represent both a democratizing and disruptive force by accelerating coding, analysis, and writing tasks while also raising concerns about overreliance, bias reproduction, and interpretability (Bommasani et al., [2021](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter); Marcus & Davis, [2019](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Therefore, I contend that R programmers bear responsibility for their work and for their use of LLMs and AI, ensuring that their contributions are both ethical and substantive.

## 10.4 Responsible Use of LLMs for Coding in Statistics

There has already been some discussion on the ethics of AI and LLMs in the literature. When drilling down to programming specifically, the literature becomes much thinner. This is because code generation is only one of several activities potentially affiliated with LLMs, which also include text generation, debugging assistance, optimization, and learning support (Lin, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Further, coding in R represents a very specific subset of research and data science, often focused on reproducible analyses, statistical modeling, and data visualization. Therefore, there is a clear need to develop guidelines and standards for using LLMs with R.

Existing frameworks in related domains provide a foundation for these guidelines. For example, the IEEE Robotics and Automation Society emphasizes transparency, disclosure, and human accountability when AI-generated content is included in research publications, including text, figures, or code (Guidelines for Generative AI Usage—IEEE Robotics and Automation Society, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). AI may be used for drafting, editing, or summarizing, but authors remain responsible for correctness and must avoid fabricating data or code. Similarly, Atemkeng et al. ([2024](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) highlight that ethical use of AI-generated code relies on transparency, accountability, and compliance with licensing or legal restrictions, noting that code reuse is common but must be properly acknowledged to maintain trust and integrity.

The literature also identifies practical risks in LLM-generated code, including biases embedded from training data, potential security vulnerabilities, and intellectual property concerns (Bistarelli et al., [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). These risks underscore the importance of human oversight, verification, and responsible documentation. In applied contexts, structured workflows for AI-assisted coding can enhance productivity while ensuring ethical and responsible use (Lin, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)). Integrating these principles into R programming practices will allow researchers to leverage LLMs effectively while maintaining scientific rigor, reproducibility, and ethical standards.

The essential foundation of using AI and LLMs for statistical research is for the researcher to know what they are doing. The biggest danger in these tools is the potential for them to act as a replacement for human authority. Imagine a world where all the major data decisions are derived from AI systems, and where few if any humans are educated enough in the source code, underlying mathematics, and underlying theory to determine if the results and recommendations are beneficial or harmful to human beings. LLMs and AI models indeed accelerate our ability to generate knowledge and information, but this comes with a Faustian risk—the risk of humans delegating all important research and analyses to these models. The only way to preserve our positionality within these systems is to KNOW the underlying structure so that we can be sure that any results or output that are produced truly represent our intentions and are truly tendered to benefit the organic world.

Little work has been done to set ethical and practical guidelines for data scientists, researchers, and other statistical coders. Therefore, to start this conversation, I propose four “Knows” of programming with R under the assistance of LLMs or AI.

### 10.4.1 Know #1: Know the Mathematics of Your Statistical Modeling

It is no secret that researchers have published or reported statistical studies with limited knowledge of the underlying mathematics of their models. Packages such as SPSS (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)), which offer point-and-click options for statistical computing, allow researchers to run statistical tests without modeling the mathematical processes. Nevertheless, the models in these packages are not generative—meaning they do not evolve and change. They are derived from pre-programmed algorithms that apply specific rules to loaded datasets and crank out _p_\-values and other statistics in output files. Arguably, assuming the author formulated the correct hypotheses, cleaned and screened their data correctly, and matched the correct model to their imported data set, the results were likely accurate and not problematic in the sense they did not fundamentally change the meaning of the data.

R and similar languages like Python are different. These packages, like SPSS, have commands with pre-programmed statistical formulas and process the data under the assumption the dataset is “tidy.” For example, the R command _anova()_ conducts Analysis of Variance given the identified variables using a pre-programmed statistical equation. However, unlike SPSS and similar packages, R also allows the programmer the freedom to customize models and change the fundamental mathematics of that model. An uninformed programmer may ask an LLM to conduct a specific statistical test only to have it change the code slightly enough to affect the results and thus the interpretation. The R programmer informed in the underlying algebra of the model should be able to detect these problems and train the LLM to correct them. However, the unskeptical user of an LLM could easily miss a small or large alteration in the model’s mathematical engine and thus draw incorrect or faulty conclusions from the results.

The solution to this is simple. The programmer should know the mathematics behind the statistical test they are using to ensure a critical eye on the results.

### 10.4.2 Know #2: Know the Language Before Relying on LLMs

Though knowing the mathematics of the model is essential to working with LLMs, it is not sufficient. If the researcher is unfamiliar with the R language, they will not be able to interpret mathematics within the code. Therefore, anyone using R or any other statistical tool with LLMs should know the coding language before they attempt to conduct statistical research.

Imagine traveling to a faraway place and relying on an AI tool to interpret the speech of the locals and deliver your speech in their language (can already do this to some degree). Even though this is convenient, there is also a lot of room for the AI tool to make subtle mistakes in the translation and cause problems and headaches. For example, imagine the tool mistaking the word “hotel” the tourist as a “home” to the local thus making the local think the tourist wants to visit their home. Suddenly, the goal of finding the hotel has led to an unexpected dinner party at the local’s habitable house with their family.

Though this is a bit of an absurd and positive outcome, the dangers of getting “lost in translation” with statistical software such as R could be dire, especially when programming languages are often used to make decisions related to national security, safety, weather, or make lower-stakes decisions such as placement in college courses. If the R user is not familiar with the language, they will be unable to determine if the program code produced is correct, represents the purpose accurately, and provides the directions correctly. Blindly relying on AI to generate code is a dangerous risk in one instance but collectively, it could be catastrophic.

Therefore, it is imperative that R programmers code their statistical models before using LLMs or other AI tools. By coding prior to using AI, the researcher not only ensures their own foundational knowledge of the theory and content of their study, but it also ensures that when these tools are used, the prior code is there to further prompt and lead the LLM towards the correct and prudent output. In other words, instead of blindly prompting an LLM to run a specific model on the data, the R programmer should have significant development in the project to cage the AI tool to stay within the parameters of the objectives of the project.

### 10.4.3 Know #3: Know How to Effectively Prompt LLMs

Related to this, the R programmer should learn how to prompt LLMs to produce correct code. There is no exact way to do this, but here I propose a few guidelines. First, the programmer should type out their objectives for the model, listing all variables and even hypotheses. This is best practice in research regardless of the use of LLMs. Articulating the purpose, variables and hypotheses of the model allows the R user to accurately direct the work. Second, related to the last “know” the programmer should attempt to program their model or project themselves prior to prompting the LLM. The process of programming generates ideas in the mind and will allow the programmer to better communicate needs to the LLM. Third, the user should try to run the model with their own code. If the model runs and the results are accurate and answer the question, then there is no need for an LLM. If the model does not run or the results are not correct, they should note any error codes and see if they can trouble-shoot them without AI assistance. If all else fails, the user should prompt the LLM with their entire purpose, variables, code, and any error codes to trouble-shoot the model that will not work.

### 10.4.4 Know #4: Know How to Verify LLM’s Coding Suggestions

Once the R programmer gets coding suggestions from the LLM, they should be able to verify the suggestions for accuracy. First, read the solution and identify what has changed. When you identify what has changed, compare it to your own coding knowledge. It might make sense. You may even slap yourself in the face (gently) because the problem was simply a typo or something simple and “human.” If it is more complex, and the LLM begins introducing you to new command prompts or packages, research them on R’s documentation to verify that the purpose of the command or package matches the purpose of your project. Then run the corrected code and check the math to make sure the results are calculated correctly. No, you do not need to run the entire model by hand, but you could run the model on a smaller set of your data that you can compare to a hand-calculation or to a calculation with another tool such as SPSS. You can also calculate some of the smaller vectors derived from the equation. For example, you could calculate the sum of squares in a model and compare it to the output given by R after utilizing the adjustment in code.

### 10.4.5 Summarizing the “Knows”

These four suggestions ensure that the power and knowledge of R programming remain with the human researcher and programmer and that the LLM is used to help debug and support the work. Some of my fellow data scientists may have a more progressive view in the use of these tools for statistical modeling. Others may be entirely against it—even to debug. Either way, these tools are here and there needs to be a clear framework and guideline for using them. Hopefully, this instigates conversation, and I am very interested in other perspectives and ideas in this area.

## 10.5 Cases for Using LLMs for Coding

Related to the four “knows” before using LLMs with R programming, I here offer very specific suggestions where these tools can help with coding.

### 10.5.1 Using LLMs to Identify Bugs and Other Coding Errors

First, as mentioned in the last section, _LLMs can be very useful for identifying bugs, typos, and other errors_. Even as I type this I often hit delete and correct a word. This happens when coding. Humans make little blips. An LLM can scan through a coding stream and identify typos, incorrect commands, missing commas or brackets, and other annoying details instantly. These are tasks that used to take several minutes to hours to do that LLMs can address instantly. The following prompt is an example of how I might ask an LLM to debug my code.

LLM Prompt 1: Asking the LLM to Debug my Code

_The following code sequencer produces the following error, “Error: object ‘total’ not found.” Please analyze this code and suggest corrections._

_Code to analyze:_

_\# Calculate the sum of a numeric vector_

_x <- c(1, 2, 3, 4, 5)_

_total <- sum(x +_

_print(total)_

**ChatGPT Suggestion**

**You are missing a closing parenthesis and have an unnecessary plus sign in your sum() try:**

**\# Corrected code to calculate the sum of a numeric vector**

**x <- c(1, 2, 3, 4, 5)**

**total <- sum(x)**

**print(total)**

### 10.5.2 Using LLMs to Write Functions

Second, LLMs can help with writing functions to condense code and increase efficiency. For example, instead of repeatedly writing the same sequence of commands, an LLM can suggest a custom function that encapsulates the workflow, allowing you to reuse it with different inputs. This not only reduces the risk of errors from copy-pasting but also makes your code cleaner, easier to read, and faster to modify. Prompt 2 is an example of how to ask ChatGPT to write a function around descriptive statistics.

Also include an example of how to use this function on a sample vector.

**ChatGPT Suggestion**

**\# Define the function to summarize a numeric vector**

**if(!is.numeric(x)) stop(“Input must be numeric”)**

**result <- list(**

**mean = mean(x, na.rm = TRUE),**

**sd = sd(x, na.rm = TRUE),**

**median = median(x, na.rm = TRUE)**

**)**

**return(result)**

**}**

**\# Example usage**

**sample\_vector <- c(4, 7, 2, 9, 5)**

**summary\_result <- summarize\_vector(sample\_vector)**

**\# Print the results**

**print(summary\_result)**

### 10.5.3 Using LLMs to Help with Syntax

Third, LLMs are useful at writing syntax. They can generate the correct commands and structure for a programming language based on a description of what you want to do. This is especially helpful for beginners or when using functions and packages with which you are not fully familiar. By providing a natural language prompt, an LLM can produce working code that follows proper syntax rules, includes the right parentheses, commas, and operators, and handles common edge cases, saving time and reducing frustrating errors. Prompt 3 gives a concise, practical example of asking ChatGPT to help write R code to calculate the mean and standard deviation for the age and income columns in my dataset and store the results in a new summary table.

LLM Prompt 3: Asking the LLM to Assist with Syntax

_Prompt for chat:_

_Write R code to calculate the mean and standard deviation for the age and income columns in my dataset and store the results in a new summary table._

**ChatGPT Response**

**\# Load necessary library**

**library(dplyr)**

**\# Example dataset**

**data <- data.frame(**

**age = c(25, 30, 22, 40, 28),**

**income = c(50000, 60000, 45000, 80000, 52000)**

**)**

**\# Calculate mean and standard deviation for selected columns**

**summary\_table <- data %>%**

## 10.6 Ethical Transparency

Using an LLM to purely calculate statistical results without the researcher practicing the above protocols is unethical and synonymous with plagiarism. It is academic dishonesty. However, even following the “KNOWS” protocols requires, in my view and in the view of many publishers, universities, and other entities, the need for transparency. Therefore, to maintain academic integrity, and to acknowledge the use of these tools, I recommend that the data scientist, researcher, student, or other person using AI to assist with coding, writing, or other elements of a project to disclose their use of AI tools at the end of their paper, or somewhere in their project, and that person should cite the tool for their reference list. For example, I might write the following statement (which is found at the beginning of this book):

> _ChatGPT_ (OpenAI, [2025](A668427_1_En_10_Chapter)) _was used to help debug code, and it was used to formulate functions and apply old model code to new models. The data scientist takes full responsibility in the results of this work and meticulously check all code, mathematics, and elements of the end-product._

While it may be tempting to leave this out, especially since there is no sure-fire way to tell if a researcher used AI for their code or writing, it is important to include it for a few reasons. First, at the bare level, it is moral and ethical to do so. Utilizing these tools and representing our work as purely from our mind is dishonest. Second, it covers the researcher if someday a new detector or process can tell if AI is used. Remember, publications stay on record for a long time. Finally, and most importantly, it normalizes the use of AI tools and provides a signal to other researchers that there are ways to use these tools ethically and responsibility and by following the ethical code, we all accept it and still allow the important work that gets done with their help to be considered, respected, and used to improve the world.

## 10.7 Conclusion

Though technologies such as the printing press certainly accelerated human development, science, equal rights, and other aspects of human existence, they also came with dangers, misinformation, and conflict. AI tools are an exponentially equivalent phenomenon, in my view. Their ability to accelerate human knowledge and development, not just in R programming but in many areas, is unprecedented. However, there is also the danger of these tools allowing intellectual dishonestly and flat-out laziness.

LLMs should never replace literacy, mathematical competency, and coding proficiency in humans. Instead, humans must maintain these foundational skills and use tools to accelerate projects and development accurately. This requires mindful oversight and intentionality on the part of data scientists, researchers, and academics. Thus, I hereby propose the “KNOWS” of using LLMs and offer my few suggestions for their specific uses.

It is my hope that other members of the R, Python, and similar communities will continue to develop very specific ethical guidelines and practices of LLMs. These codes of conduct promise to provide roadmaps to future programmers. It is also my hope that future users of LLMs will consider these ethical guidelines and be transparent in the use of these tools. We should not be avoidant of these tools; they are here to stay. We also should not be cavalier. Like a well-engineered sports car, we need to slip into the driver’s seat, buckle our seatbelts, and follow the rules of the road. But first, we need roads and rules. Hopefully, this offers some of these things.
