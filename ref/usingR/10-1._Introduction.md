## Abstract

This chapter introduces the book, which provides a succinct and practical guide for social science graduate students and academics to learn R for basic statistical processes. The book covers core topics such as measures of central tendency and variance, frequencies and proportions, data visualization, chi-square tests, t-tests, ANOVA, correlations, and regression. The book also teaches data importing and manipulation, explains statistical concepts and assumptions, and guides interpretation of R output. Practice datasets and freely available R code support hands-on learning and skill development throughout this volume.

## 1.1 Background to the Book

The purpose of this project was to provide a succinct, and useful guide for the social science graduate student or academic who wants to learn R programming for basic statistical processes. The literature is full of volumes about using vender statistical tools such as SPSS and SAS, products that may cost the graduate student hundreds of dollars and the non-student academic up to thousands of dollars. However, in my teaching experience, I have been unable to find any condensed and to-the-point volume on R that covers basic descriptive and inferential statistical processes most likely to be used in Social Sciences. There are conceptually similar volumes on SPSS by authors such as Field et al. ([2012](A668427_1_En_1_Chapter)) and Leech et al. ([2015](A668427_1_En_1_Chapter)) have contributed to the collection of statistics books by generating comprehensive volumes. There is a need for an elegant volume on R, and here I present my attempt at such a volume.

The objective of this book is to target select categories of statistical topics. These include: (a) measures of central tendency and variance, (b) frequencies and proportions, (c) generating histograms, charts, and plots, (d) the chi-square test family, (e) the t-test family, (f) the analysis of variance (ANOVA) family, (c) bivariate correlation and the regression family. Under each section, this book will also introduce techniques for data importing and munging (often called “manipulating”) and will provide the reader with the basic conceptual principles of each statistical test including statistical assumptions. This volume will also provide information about interpreting the results on R output including statistical significance, effect sizes, and other important parameters. Finally, this volume links to R datasets to practice each of the activities.

In sum, the overall objective of this book is to provide the reader with access to R programming language for basic statistical tests and data munging techniques in a succinct and affordable volume. Mastery of these concepts will empower the reader not only to complete these statistical tasks but will also prepare them to advance their knowledge with more complex statistical tools. In my experience, once one learns the foundation of R programming language and gains a basic understanding of statistical concepts, they are equipped to advance their statistical and programming skills on R. Therefore, this book promises to lay a foundation from which the reader can grow as an R programmer and as a quantitative researcher.
