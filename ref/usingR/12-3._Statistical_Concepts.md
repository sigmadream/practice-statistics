Dr. Christie’s wine example lists the four types of variables typically used in social science quantitative research. **A variable is simply any characteristic, trait, or quality that can be measured or categorized** (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). ### 3.1.1 Nominal Variables

A nominal variable is an attribute or quality that is not “larger”, “smaller”, “more”, “less”, “better”, “worse” or any other qualifier than any other category (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). For example, if we were studying houses, we may have a variable called “Front Porch” as shown below. We mark “Y” for them having a front porch and “N” for not. Now some folks might think front porches are great, but Y is not better than N. We may even assign a “1” for having a front porch or a “0” for not. Some statistical packages require numeric coding. R does not always care! In the example below, we have four houses, two have porches and two do not have porches. These are marked with both a letter and a number, but most researchers would choose to use either a letter (e.g., “Y”) or a number (e.g., “1”) in their data set.

| House ID | Front Porch |
| -------- | ----------- |
| House 1  | Y(1)        |
| House 2  | N(0)        |
| House 3  | N(0)        |
| House 4  | Y(1)        |

Other nominal variables may include gender (M, F, NB, GQ, etc.), race/ethnicity (W, B, PI, etc.), planet of origin (Mars, Earth, Venus), and many others. Typically, nominal variables emerge from the research question and the data available. In statistical tests, it is often optimal to have a workable number of categories for any given variable. For example, if I had a variable called name (Mark, Sarah, Jason, Cora, Hank, etc.), it may not be useful to treat everyone as their own category in a sample of 100 but instead to see how they cluster or self-identify with a different category. For example, I might have a nominal variable called “hair color” and classify my sample with that category.

### 3.1.2 Ordinal Variables

Like nominal variables, ordinal variables are categorial, but they have some sort of rank (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). For example, consider the terms first-year student, sophomore, junior, senior (terms that are being replaced but are useful here nonetheless). We know that a first-year student is not as progressed as a sophomore, but that distance may not be the same between every first-year student and every sophomore. Some first-year students may be a few credits away while others are far behind, yet they reside at the same rank. For example, consider a variable representing **Course Satisfaction**:

| Student ID | Course satisfaction   |
| ---------- | --------------------- |
| Student 1  | Very satisfied (5)    |
| Student 2  | Satisfied (4)         |
| Student 3  | Neutral (3)           |
| Student 4  | Dissatisfied (2)      |
| Student 5  | Very dissatisfied (1) |

Ordinal variables have a rank that does not represent a quantifiable consistent difference. Another good example is the Likert scale on a survey (e.g., strongly agree, agree, disagree, strongly disagree). Whereas we know strongly agree is “higher” than “agree” and “strongly disagree,” we cannot consistently measure the distance across everyone who ranks the survey. Likert scales are therefore ordinal at individual survey question (item) levels. Other examples might include grading scales (A, B, C, D, F) and course difficulty levels (difficult, introductory, etc.). There are many phenomena in the world that seem to have rank, but we cannot quite measure with precision.

### 3.1.3 Interval Variables

Interval variables, in contrast to ordinal variables, not only have a defined order but also have a consistent, quantifiable difference between each value. A key feature of interval variables is that the intervals between values are equal, meaning the difference between 10 and 20 is the same as the difference between 90 and 100\. However, they lack a true zero point. A good example of an interval variable is temperature measured in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit. Here is an example of **Temperature** measured in degrees Celsius:

| Day   | Temperature (°C) |
| ----- | ---------------- |
| Day 1 | 10               |
| Day 2 | 20               |
| Day 3 | 30               |
| Day 4 | 0                |

Zero degrees does not represent the absence of temperature; it is simply a point on the scale. This lack of a true zero means that we can measure differences between temperatures, but we cannot make statements about ratios (e.g., 20 °C is not “twice as hot” as 10 °C). While interval variables allow for meaningful comparisons of differences, their lack of an absolute zero means they fall short of ratio variables in terms of full-scale measurement (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

### 3.1.4 Ratio Variables

Ratio variables are the most powerful in terms of measurement because they possess both a consistent, quantifiable difference between values and a true zero point. With ratio variables, zero truly represents the absence of the attribute being measured, making ratio comparisons meaningful (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). For example, weight is a ratio variable. Zero kilograms means there is no weight, and 20 kilograms is indeed twice as heavy as 10 kg. Here is an example:

| Object ID | Weight (kg) |
| --------- | ----------- |
| Object 1  | 0           |
| Object 2  | 10          |
| Object 3  | 20          |
| Object 4  | 40          |

Other examples of ratio variables include height, age, income, and time. These variables allow for all kinds of mathematical operations, including addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. The presence of a true zero point gives ratio variables the flexibility and precision needed for sophisticated statistical analyses.

The distinction between nominal and ordinal is important with various statistics as is the distinction between interval and ratio. However, researchers frequently treat them similarly in certain statistical tests. For example, some tests may call for a “scale” dependent variable, meaning that interval or ratio will do. ### 3.1.6 Independent and Dependent Variables

To correctly conduct statistical research, we need to be familiar with independent and dependent variables. This is especially important because they help determine the statistical test that we are going to conduct. In experimental research, we are often interested to see if _X_ caused a change in _Y_. For example, did the medication cure the patient’s disease? Did the curriculum improve student reading? In these sorts of studies, independent and dependent variables are easy to define. It becomes a little bit trickier in difference testing. For example, we might want to know if there is a difference in shoe size between Martians, Earthlings, and Venusians. In correlational studies, it becomes even trickier if we are interested, for example, if there is a relationship between a community’s cost of living and its community college enrollment.

The **independent variable** is the variable that is deliberately manipulated or varied in an experiment to observe if it causes any change in the dependent variable (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). It is called “independent” because its variation is not influenced by other variables in the experiment. This is the variable you control. For example, imagine you are conducting a study to see how different amounts of sunlight affect plant growth. In this case, the **amount of sunlight** would be the independent variable because you are controlling how much light each plant receives to see how it impacts growth. Or, if you are assessing whether caffeine improves student test performance, the independent variable would be the **amount of caffeine** given to the students. Another example: if you are assessing the effect of exercise on mood, the **type of exercise** (jogging, yoga, weightlifting, etc.) would be your independent variable. You are changing what type of exercise participants do to see if that leads to different mood outcomes.

The **dependent variable**, on the other hand, is the outcome that is measured in response to the changes in the independent variable (Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Johnson & Christensen, [2025](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). It is “dependent” because its value depends on the manipulation of the independent variable. This is the variable that you are trying to understand or predict. For instance, in the plant sunlight example, the **growth of the plants** would be the dependent variable since you are measuring how much the plants grow based on the amount of sunlight they receive. In the caffeine test, the **test scores** of the students would be the dependent variable since you are measuring their performance to see how caffeine impacts it.

In the exercise and mood study, the **mood** of participants (measured through a questionnaire or scale) would be the dependent variable. You are measuring how different types of exercise affect mood, so mood is the outcome that “depends” on the type of exercise.

In more complex situations, it can sometimes be difficult to decide which variable is independent and which is the dependent. For example, if you are exploring whether there is a relationship between the amount of sleep a person gets and their productivity at work, is sleep the independent variable because it is being “manipulated,” or is productivity the dependent variable? The answer usually depends on your research question, but typically in this case, sleep is the independent variable, and productivity is the dependent variable because you hypothesize that sleep affects productivity.

In short, **independent variables** are what you change or manipulate, and **dependent variables** are what you measure to see the effect of those changes. They are like a cause-and-effect duo, where the independent variable acts as the cause, and the dependent variable is the effect.

### 3.2.8 Standard Error of the Mean

Now that we have explored standard deviation, we can now understand standard error of the mean (SEM), which uses standard deviation in its calculation as shown in Eq. ([3.4](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). Simply put, standard error (_SE_) is the standard deviation (_s_) divided by the square root of _n_, or sample size (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). Ideally, SEM is calculated by taking the standard deviation of the population (_σ_) divided by the square root of the number in the population (_N_). However, we rarely have data on the whole population, so we apply it to sample data and can get remarkably similar results. Like standard deviation, _SEM_ is a measure of variability. It estimates how much the sample mean is expected to fluctuate from the population mean if the sample were repeated multiple times. A smaller _SEM_ indicates that the sample mean is likely closer to the true population mean, while a larger _SEM_ suggests more variability. Simply put, the standard error estimates how much sample means would be from the true population. This is an important statistic when understanding statistical significance as it is used to calculate confidence intervals (something we will get into later). See

## 3.3 Distributions

Imagine you were in one of those gymnasiums with bleachers and the bleachers were full of 500 second-grade children. In each child’s possession is a slip of paper reminding them of their height. In their pocket is a quarter. Now imagine, a line was set up ranging from 40 inches to 60 inches on the gym floor. The kids then stand in line, and each child stacks their quarter on the inch closest to their height (their slips rounded to the nearest inch). Now imagine sitting at eye level (or close to it) with the quarters looking at how they stacked. The result would be one or a few quarters on the left end of the diagram. As you moved your eye right, the quarter stacks would get higher, would peak in the middle like a mountain, and gently slope down until they approached 60 inches as shown in Fig. [3.2](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter). This is known to many as the “bell curve.” In statistics, we call it the “normal distribution” (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Huck, [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

The y-axis indicates the number of kids, with values up to 80. The distribution peaks around 49 to 51 inches, forming a bell curve.](./images/668427_1_En_3_Fig2_HTML.png)

As shown in the figure, we would expect that most kids would share similar heights. On the edges of the distribution, we have a few kids who are unusually taller or shorter than their peers. Most of the kids in this simulated dataset are around 49 to 50 inches tall. Thus, the normal distribution is a way to tell us quite a bit of information about our dataset. It is one of the most fundamental concepts in statistics.

Interestingly, many things in nature are “normally distributed” or would end up on a bell curve if plotted. For example, outside of human height, body temperature, IQ scores, blood pressure, flower petal lengths, speed of animals within species, rainfall, and others all reside on a normal distribution. However, many human-created variables are skewed and not normally curved (we will get to that later in this chapter). In addition, some natural phenomena are also skewed, meaning they may have many low or high values.

In statistics, the normal distribution is significant because it allows us to model and understand variability in data. It is essential for predicting probabilities, conducting hypothesis tests, and making inferences about populations from sample data. The symmetry of the normal distribution, with most values clustered around the mean, provides a foundation for numerous statistical techniques, including regression analysis and confidence intervals (we will get to these later). Normal distribution is also an important statistical assumption of many tests that we will learn in this book.

### 3.3.1 Normal Distribution and Skewness

Figure [3.3](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter) shows three different distributions. The first is called the “normal distribution” and is often referred to as the “bell curve” since it resembles a bell-like shape. The normal distribution is symmetrical, meaning that when sliced in half, the left side is equal to the right side. In normal distributions the mean, median, and mode are all the same number (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

Normal, positive, and negative distributions

Positively skewed distributions are not symmetrical and occur when a high proportion of the sample score is low, resulting in a skewer (the main tail of the distribution) pointing to the right, or the positive direction of the values. One should not conflate the word “positive” to mean “good.” It simply refers to the location of the skewer on the distribution. In this case, it points towards the ascending, or positive, values on the X axis. Sometimes, positively skewed distributions are undesirable. For example, a positively skewed distribution of math test scores would indicate that most of the students scored low on that math test. However, sometimes positively skewed distributions are desirable. A positively skewed distribution of a sample’s time spent in prison indicates that most folks spend little time committing crimes! In positively skewed distributions, the mode is still at the top of the hump but is less than the median, which is less than the mean (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

A negatively skewed distribution is asymmetrical and indicates a high proportion of scores at higher values. Therefore, the skewer points towards the lower end of the X-axis scale, or negatively. High scores would be desirable for a math test but would be undesirable if a sample took a test on criminality, depression, or other concerning scales. These distributions have a mode at the top of the hump, with a median lower than the mode and the mean as the lowest score (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

Therefore, we see a trend where the median is always between the mean and the mode. When skewed negatively, the median is a lower value than the mode. When skewed positively, the median is a higher value than the mode. When normal, again, all three are the same value. Figure [3.3](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter) illustrates this and was custom programmed on R. The code for this is available at the supplemental code link.

### 3.3.2 Kurtosis

Many statistical tests also consider kurtosis when evaluating the “normality” of a distribution. While skewness, representing the symmetry of the curve, is one indicator, kurtosis gives another insight into how extreme the data points are in the tails. This can be thought of in terms of the distribution’s peak (kurtosis). As shown in Fig. [3.4](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter), we are examining the steepness or sharpness of the curve. The figure presents three types of kurtosis: (a) **Mesokurtic** refers to a normal, bell-shaped curve with a moderate peak, (b) **platykurtic** distributions are flatter, with a broader peak and tails that trail off slowly, and (c) **leptokurtic** distributions have a steeper, more pronounced peak and heavy tails, resembling a sharp peak or a Dr. Seuss hill (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

### 3.3.3 Calculating Skewness and Kurtosis to Evaluate Normality

Many statistical tests require examining the normality of a distribution overall and within different levels of a categorical variable. Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution. It can be estimated using Pearson’s skewness (based on the mean–median difference) or more formally using the standardized third central moment. Equation ([3.5](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)) presents this standardized moment, which corresponds to the Type 3 skewness (_g_) estimator described by Joanes and Gill ([1998](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). This is the estimator implemented in the _psych_ package in R (Revelle, [2020](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). If the value is negative, the distribution is negatively skewed; if positive, it is positively skewed; and values between −1 and 1 are typically considered approximately normal.

g\=1n∑i\=1nxi−x¯s3Where:S\=Σx1−x¯2n−1

(3.5)

Kurtosis is more complex to calculate than skewness. To determine kurtosis, we first calculate the fourth central moment (FCM), which is a measure of how the data points deviate from the mean to the fourth power. This is like the calculation of variance (which uses the square of deviations), but instead, we raise the deviations to the fourth power. Once we have the FCM, we divide it by the standard deviation raised to the fourth power, as shown in Eq. ([3.6](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). The FCM is calculated by subtracting the mean from each data point, raising the result to the fourth power, summing these values, and then dividing by _n_ − 1 to correct for bias in the sample.

When we apply this to the equation for kurtosis (Eq. [3.7](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)), we are comparing the relative “spread” of the data, as measured by the FCM, to the spread measured by the standard deviation raised to the fourth power. If the result is exactly 3, the distribution is mesokurtic, meaning it follows a normal distribution. If the result is less than 3, the distribution is platykurtic, indicating flatter tails and fewer extreme values. If the result is greater than 3, the distribution is leptokurtic, suggesting heavier tails and more extreme values (Chattamvelli & Shanmugam, [2023](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). Note that this differs from Chattamvelli and Shanmugam ([2023](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)) by subtracting 1 from _n_.

FCM\=∑i\=1nxi−x¯4n−1

(3.6)

Kurtosis\=FCMs4

(3.7)

In R, kurtosis is typically calculated as excess kurtosis (EK), which measures how the “tailedness” of a distribution compares to that of a normal distribution. For a normal distribution, excess kurtosis is zero. Functions such as _describe\_by()_ from the _psych_ package (Revelle, [2020](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)) calculate excess kurtosis using a equation that accounts for sample size bias. This calculation involves determining the fourth central moment of the data and then adjusting the result by subtracting 3\. The subtraction of _K_ allows the normal distribution, which has a kurtosis of 3, to have an excess kurtosis of 0\. As a result, distributions with excess kurtosis greater than 0 are leptokurtic, indicating heavy tails and sharp peaks, while distributions with excess kurtosis less than 0 are platykurtic, indicating light tails and a flatter peak (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Tabachnick & Fidell, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). This approach helps to standardize kurtosis. Equation ([3.8](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)) for _K_ and 3.9 for _EK_ given _K_ demonstrates this process.

K\=3n−12n−2n−3

(3.8)

Here, _n_ represents the number of data points in the equation. _K_ is a correction factor that improves the accuracy of EK, compensating for small-sample biases (Chattamvelli & Shanmugam, [2023](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

As a result, when EK is zero then it is considered mesokurtic, when it is greater than zero it is leptokurtic and less than zero is platykurtic. Researchers consider excess kurtosis > |1| (>1 or <−1) non-normal. In later chapters, we will discuss ways to evaluate normality with kurtosis and skewness for different statistical tests and ways to troubleshoot or move ahead with testing when these values are outside the acceptable ranges.

### 3.3.4 Normal Distribution, Standard Deviation, and Percentiles

The normal distribution provides valuable insights into our data. Figure [3.5](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter) illustrates the relationship between the normal distribution, percentile scores, and the number of standard deviations from the mean. The number of standard deviations from the mean, often referred to as the _Z_\-score, is calculated by subtracting an individual score from the mean and dividing the result by the standard deviation, as shown in Eq. ([3.11](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)) (Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). A percentile score represents the percent of individuals whose scores are equal to or below that of a specific test-taker, offering a straightforward comparison of individual performance within the distribution. For example, you may have scored in the 99th percentile on the GRE, meaning that you scored better than or equal to 99% of the population (Agresti, [2018](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)).

Z\=score−x¯s

(3.11)

A closer examination of Fig. [3.5](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter) offers deeper insights into the properties of the normal distribution. The figure highlights that one standard deviation accounts for approximately 34% of the population, so 68% of individuals fall within one standard deviation above and below the mean (i.e., between −1 and 1 _Z_\-scores). The mean, which corresponds to a _Z_\-score of zero, represents no deviation from the average and aligns with the 50th percentile. This signifies that 50% of the population scores at or below the average on any normally distributed scale. For instance, the 50th percentile in height corresponds to the average height.

![A bell curve chart illustrating a normal distribution with Z-scores on the x-axis and density on the y-axis. The curve is divided into sections showing percentages of data within standard deviations: 68% between -1 and 1 SD, 95% between -1.96 and 1.96 SD, and 99.7% between -3 and 3 SD. Percentiles are marked above the curve.](./images/668427_1_En_3_Fig5_HTML.png)

Fig. 3.5

The normal distribution given percentile and Z score (number of standard deviations)

Further analysis of the figure reveals that individuals scoring two standard deviations above the mean (_Z_ \= 2) are at the 98th percentile, meaning they score higher than or equal to 98% of the population. For males, this would correspond to a height of approximately 6′2″. As the curve extends from the peak (at the mean, median and mode) toward either side of the x-axis, the frequency of scores decreases, illustrating that most people score near the average, while extreme scores (both high and low) are increasingly rare. This decline in frequency underscores the fundamental nature of the normal distribution: the majority of values cluster around the mean, with fewer occurrences as one moves further from it.

Theoretically, the distribution does not end at |4| _Z_ (between −4 and 4 standard deviations). The tails shrink continuously. The range on the X-axis is only limited by the literal minimum and maximum values in the population. In theory, one could be 9 standard deviations from the mean, which would represent an extremely rare 99.99999999999997th percentile, meaning that fewer than 1 in 100 trillion would achieve a score that extreme. The only requirement would be to have a large enough sample to obtain such a percentile. However, in practice, most real-world datasets have finite ranges, and such extreme values are often truncated or considered outliers, depending on the context. These long, thin tails in the normal distribution reinforce their utility in capturing both typical variations near the mean and rare, extreme deviations. Yet, in all of science in an infinite universe, we can see how conceptually fascinating the normal distribution can be.

### 3.3.5 Other Standardized Scores and Tests

Percentiles and _Z_\-scores are only two ways to examine the normal distribution. Over the past century (and even longer), psychometricians have developed and standardized several measures, each relying on the normal “bell” curve to compare individual scores to those of all test-takers.

Cognitive assessments such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children and the Weschler Adult Intelligence Scale (colloquially known as intelligence quotient (IQ) tests), are a widely recognized example, with a mean score of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale | Fourth Edition, [2024](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter); Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children | Fifth Edition, [2024](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). This framework places most individuals within the range of 85 to 115\. _T_\-scores (not to be confused with t-test in this book), another standardized system, have a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 and are commonly used in psychological and educational assessments such as the Behavior Assessment System for Children, Third Edition (Behavior Assessment System for Children | Third Edition, [2024](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter)). Stanine scores (short for “standard nine”) represent yet another system, scaling raw scores into a 9-point distribution with a mean of 5 and a standard deviation of 2\. Stanine scores categorize results into three ranges: 1–3 for below average, 4–6 for average, and 7–9 for above average. This system is particularly prevalent in educational settings to evaluate and compare student performance. Each of these standardized scoring methods offers a different perspective for interpreting data within the context of the normal distribution. Many tests that used to use stanine scores switched to other distributions in the last 25 years. Figure [3.6](A668427_1_En_3_Chapter) shows these tests on a distribution using R code. As shown, the curves are slightly different given their scales and standard deviations.
