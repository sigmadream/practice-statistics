## Abstract

This chapter covers both parametric and nonparametric methods for comparing groups. Parametric approaches include the independent samples t-test and the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), which assess differences in a scale variable between two or more groups. Post-hoc tests are discussed for cases in which the ANOVA omnibus test is statistically significant. Nonparametric alternatives are introduced for situations where assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance are violated. The Mann–Whitney _U_ test serves as the nonparametric counterpart to the t-test, and the Kruskal–Wallis test parallels ANOVA. When the Kruskal–Wallis test is significant, post-hoc comparisons can be conducted using Dunn’s test to identify specific group differences. Parametric tests generate test statistics such as _t_ (for the t-test) and _F_ (for ANOVA), whereas nonparametric tests produce rank-based statistics. Finally, measures of effect size, such as Cohen’s _d_ for t-tests and eta-squared (_η_ _2_) for ANOVA, quantify the strength of group differences.

## 7.1 Background to Group Comparisons

This chapter will cover two types of group-comparison statistical tests. The first test covered, the t-test, is used to compare differences between two groups given a scale variable. The second test used, analysis of variance (ANOVA) is used to compare differences in a scale dependent variable when there are two or more groups. T-test generates a _t_ statistic and ANOVA generates an _F_ statistic (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)). ANOVA can be used instead of t-test when there are two groups, but t-test is often the method of choice in this circumstance. In addition, this chapter will cover post-hoc pairwise comparisons tests for ANOVA. These test for differences between paired combinations of an ANOVA test. For example, if an ANOVA compares the difference in height between Group1, Group2, and Group3, post-hoc tests will compare all paired combinations (the more groups in an ANOVA, the more combinations there are):

* Group1 to Group2
* Group1 to Group3
* Group2 to Group3

In cases where assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance are not met, nonparametric alternatives provide robust options for comparing groups. The Mann–Whitney U test serves as the nonparametric equivalent of the t-test, and the Kruskal–Wallis test parallels ANOVA when analyzing differences across two or more groups. When a Kruskal–Wallis test yields a significant result, post-hoc comparisons can be conducted using Dunn’s test to identify which specific group pairs differ significantly. These tests rely on ranked rather than raw data values, making them appropriate for ordinal data or non-normally distributed scale data.

Finally, we will be discussing measures of effect size to determine the strength of the differences between groups. These will include Cohen’s _d_ for t-test and for ANOVA omnibus tests, they will include eta-squared (_η_ _2_).

## 7.2 Independent Samples T-test

After analyzing differences in eye color, our astronaut decided that she wanted to see if there was a difference in ACT scores between Martians and Earthlings. (It turns out that ACT has been secretly giving the test on Mars for years). She obtained a sample of data from both Mars and Earth and asked the following research question: _Is there a difference in ACT scores between Martians and Earthlings_. Since she wanted to conduct a two-tailed test, the null hypothesis was that there would be no difference in ACT scores, one alternative hypothesis was that Martians scored higher, and the other alternative hypothesis was that Earthlings scored higher.

To test the null hypothesis, the astronaut would need to run an independent samples t-test, which tests to see if there is a difference in a scale variable between two groups. Equation ([7.1](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how the t-test is calculated. First, find the difference in means between the two groups. Next, calculate the standard error of the difference by dividing the variance of each group by their respective sample sizes and add them together, square rooting the result. Finally, divide the differences in mean by that number. This generates a _t_ statistic, which is matched with the degrees of freedom in a t-table to see if there is statistical significance. Degrees of freedom are calculated by (_n_ _1_–1) + (_n_ _2_–1) where _n_ _1_ is the number of people in group 1 and _n_ _2_ is the number of people in group 2 (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

### 7.2.1 Checking for Assumptions

Independent samples t-test has several assumptions. First, we assume there is **independence of observations**. Like chi-square test and all other tests, this means that the two groups should be independent of each other where no participants or cases overlap. Second, we assume that both groups’ **distributions are normal**, meaning skewness is <|1| as is kurtosis. However, much literature suggests that t-test is robust to normality assumptions. Regardless, extreme deviations from normality should be met with caution and may require data transformations or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Third, we assume **homogeneity of variance**, which means that the variance of each group is equal. If violated, we can conduct an adjustment such as a Welch’s test. Fourth, we assume that the **dependent variable is scale and the independent variable is categorical** at two levels (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

### 7.2.3 Cohen’s d Effect Size

From the descriptive statistics we see that married people are younger on average than older people and the average difference in age is 6.87 years. However, because of variance, we are not sure how strong the difference in age is between the two groups. We can quantify that difference by calculating Cohen’s _d_ effect size, which measures how strong the difference is between two groups. Equation ([7.2](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows Cohen’s _d_ when each group has the same sample size. Equation ([7.3](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows d when each group has a different sample size (Cohen, [1988](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Wall Emerson, [2023](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

(7.3)

We know from our analysis that there is a difference in age given married status. To calculate the strength of that difference, we will run a Cohen’s _d_ effect size test using the _rstatix_ package in R (Kassambara, [2023](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)). Generally, _d_ is interpreted as the following:

## 7.3 Mann Whitney U Non-Parametric Test for Two Groups in the DV

Finally, for good measure, we will run a Mann Whitney _U_ test (also called a Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test). If equality of variance and normality were severely violated, we could still test the hypothesis of equal age given married status using this test. Equation ([7.4](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how it is calculated. The U test does not rely on means and variance but instead ranks all the values and compares the sum of the ranks between the two groups. The Mann-Whitney _U_ test works by ranking all the values from both groups together, then summing the ranks for each group. The _U_ statistic is calculated by taking the total possible pairwise comparisons between groups, adjusting for the expected rank sum under the null hypothesis, and subtracting the actual rank sum of one group. The _smaller_ U value is used for statistical comparison. This statistic adjusts for sample sizes but may be affected when sample size differences are extreme. In the equation, _n_ _1_ and _n_ _2_ are the sample sizes of group one and two, and _R_ _1_ and _R_ _2_ are the sum of the ranks of the respective groups. R reports on the _W_ value, which is the smallest sum rank of both groups _R_ _1_ or _R_ _2_) (Dedecker & Saulière, [2017](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Wall Emerson, [2023](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

U\=n1n2n1n1+12−R1

U\=n1n2n2n2+12−R2

(7.4)

The following code with bold and centered results reports on the results of the non-parametric test in R. The results indicated that there was a significant difference in the location shift between the two groups, _W_ \= 44,398, _p_ < 0.001\. Thus, both t-tests and the non-parametric test indicate a statistically significant result.

### 7.3.1 Effect Size r for Mann Whitney U

A Mann Whitney U test produces an effect size _r_ (not to be confused with the Pearson’s _r_) called a Wilcoxon signed rank correlation (_r_sr) by dividing the absolute value of a _Z_ statistic by the square root of the sample size, or rsr\=∣Z∣n (Tomczak & Tomczak, [2014](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)). Since R software does not produce a _Z_ value, we can calculate it manually. This effect size could be different than _d_ because it examines the difference in rank instead of using standard deviations and means. Therefore, I recommend only using _r_ _sr_ if statistical assumptions are not met for the parametric d. The results of our code given _r_ _sr_ \= 0.312.

 \# Calculating effect size for U

 \# Compute Mann-Whitney U statistic manually (SPSS-style)

 compute\_U <- function(group1, group2) {

 combined\_data <- c(group1, group2)

 ranks <- rank(combined\_data)

 rank\_sum1 <- sum(ranks\[1:length(group1)\])

 rank\_sum2 <- sum(ranks\[(length(group1)+1):length(combined\_data)\])

 U1 <- rank\_sum1 - length(group1)\*(length(group1) + 1)/2

 U2 <- rank\_sum2 - length(group2)\*(length(group2) + 1)/2

 return(min(U1, U2))} # Smaller U value matches SPSS

 \# Define groups correctly for Mann-Whitney U

 group1 <- CPS85$age\[CPS85$married == "Married"\]

 group2 <- CPS85$age\[CPS85$married == "Single"\]

 \# Compute U statistic manually

 print(paste("Mann-Whitney U (Custom Calculation):", U\_value))

 n2 <- length(group2)

 n <- n1 + n2

## 7.4 One-Way ANOVA

After testing the difference in ACT scores between Martians and Earthlings, our astronaut friend decided that she wanted to see if there was a difference in ACT between Martians, Earthlings and Venusians, adding one more category to her nominal variable. She obtained data from Venus and updated her research question to: _Is there a difference in ACT scores between Martians, Earthlings and Venusians_? Like with the t-test, she wanted to conduct a two-tailed test to determine whether there was any difference between the two planetary creatures, testing the null hypothesis that no difference existed between them.

To test the null hypothesis, an independent samples analysis of variance (ANOVA) would be used (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)). This is an independent samples test because the three groups of Earthling, Martian, and Venusian are independent of each other. The next chapter covers dependent samples tests. As the name of the test suggests, ANOVA analyzes the variances and the means of each group.

Equation ([7.5](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how one-way ANOVA is calculated where X¯ represents the grand mean and X¯_i_ represents the group mean. The _F_ statistic is calculated by dividing the mean squares between (MSB) groups by the mean squares within (MSW). Thus, _F_ \= _MSB/MSW_. MSB is calculated by dividing the sum of squares between groups (SSB) by the degrees of freedom between (DFB). SSB measures the variation in the data that is explained by differences between group means. It is calculated by summing the squared differences between each group mean (X¯_i_) and the grand mean (X¯), weighted by the number of observations in each group. DFB is equal to _k_ − 1, where _k_ is the number of groups. MSW is calculated by dividing the sum of squares within groups (SSW) by the degrees of freedom within groups (DFW). SSW measures the variation within each group, which represents random error or individual differences not explained by group differences. It is computed by summing the squared differences between each individual observation and its respective group mean. DFW is equal to _n − k_ where _n_ is the total number of observations (Sawyer, [2009](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); St»hle & Wold, [1989](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

F\=MSBMSW\=SSBDFBSSWDFW\=∑X−X¯2−∑X−X¯i2k−1∑X−X¯i2n−k

(7.5)

Our astronaut conducted ANOVA in R and found no statistically significant difference between the three groups as shown on the left of Fig. [7.3](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter) where the boxplots and distributions of the three planets overlap. The right plots on Fig. [7.3](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter) show what it would look like if the ANOVA was statistically significant. Note the little to no overlap of the plots.

### 7.4.1 Checking Assumptions for ANOVA

ANOVA comes with six statistical assumptions that must be checked and met. The first assumption is **independence of observations**, which was discussed in previous chapters and is the same here. The second assumption is that the **dependent variable is scale, and the independent variable is categorical** at three or more levels. The third assumption is there are **no significant outliers**. Fourth, **each level (group) of the independent variable must be normally distributed** (so our astronaut checked to see if Earth, Venus, and Mars were normally distributed on ACT). Finally, we check for **homogeneity of variance**, or if the variances are equal between each level of the dependent variable (Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

Many researchers note that ANOVA is often robust to the assumption of normality. However, if this and other assumptions are violated, a non-parametric Kruska-Wallis test can be run, which we cover after ANOVA (Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

### 7.4.2 Post-Hoc ANOVA Tests

If there is a statistically significant difference in the ANOVA omnibus (overall) test, pairwise post-hoc comparisons can be conducted to see if the pairs of groups of the independent variable are different from each other. For example, our astronaut may be interested in these combinations of pairwise tests:

* Mars to Venus
* Mars to Earth
* Venus to Earth

As you might imagine, the more levels of the independent variable, the more pairs you will get. Where _k_ represents the number of groups, you can calculate the number of pairwise tests with this equation kk−12 (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)). That means for 3 levels (groups) of the independent variable, you have three pairwise combinations, but for four levels, one would have 6 combinations. These combinations increase with more groups as five groups results in 10 combinations, and six groups results in 15, etc. Therefore, when conducting ANOVA, consider the complexity of your independent variable and whether ANOVA is the right choice. It may be possible to have too many groups. Methods such as regression may be better in these instances (later chapter).

There are several options for post-hoc tests. Anyone who has ever used SPSS is familiar with names like Bonferroni, or Games-Howell. They all have one common goal to reduce the chances of Type 1 error (finding a difference when none exists) if one were to simply do separate t-tests. Thus, each of these methods adjusts the critical alpha to make it more difficult to make a Type 1 error. You can look up the different tests, and they all are available on R, but for this book, I will introduce you to Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test, which compares each combination of groups with an adjustment and is used when equal variances are assumed given a Levene’s test. Then I will introduce the Games-Howell test, which can be used when the variances are not equal and the assumption is violated.

Equation ([7.6](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how Tukey’s HSD test is calculated for each pair within a multigroup one-way ANOVA when group sizes are equal, and Eq. ([7.7](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how it is calculated when group sizes are unequal (the Tukey–Kramer adjustment). Tukey’s HSD assumes that the variances of all groups in the independent variable are equal (homogeneity of variance).

q\=X¯i−X¯jMSWn

(7.6)

q\=X¯i−X¯jMSW21ni+1nj

(7.7)

To perform Tukey’s HSD test, first compute the mean differences for each pair of groups. Using these differences, calculate the Mean Square Within (MSW) from the ANOVA table. When the group sizes are equal, the MSW is divided by the common sample size per group to obtain the standard error for each pairwise comparison. For example, if each group contains ten observations, MSW is divided by ten. When the group sizes are unequal, the Tukey–Kramer adjustment is applied: the harmonic mean of the two group sizes is calculated by taking the reciprocal of each group size, summing these reciprocals, and then taking the reciprocal of that sum. MSW is then divided by this harmonic mean to account for the unequal sample sizes. The square root of this result yields the standard error for the comparison, which is then multiplied by the critical value from the Studentized range distribution, or q-value, to determine the threshold for significance. The q-value depends on the number of groups being compared and the within-group degrees of freedom associated with MSW. In R, the qtukey() function provides this critical value, and the TukeyHSD() function automates the calculation of statistical significance without the need to consult tables (Brown, [2005](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Copenhaver, [1988](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Dohm, [2024](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Schaarschmidt & Ritz, [2020](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

When variances are assumed not to be equal, a Games-Howell test can be used to calculate pairwise effects (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)). Equation ([7.8](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how the Games Howell statistic is calculated, and Eq. ([7.9](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows how degrees of freedom is calculated. As shown, the Games-Howell statistic (_t_) uses the difference in mean given the variance of groups and samples divided by the counts. Likewise, degrees of freed uses variances and sample sizes. Both statistics are used to refer to a _t_ table, but the statistical significance is given by R on the output. After calculating _t_ _ij_, the test corrects for multiple comparisons by using Tukey’s Studentized Range (q) distribution: _q_ \= _t_ _ij_(2) (Games & Howell, [1976](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

tij\=X¯i−X¯jSi2ni+Sj2nj

(7.9)

### 7.4.3 Effect Size of ANOVA

ANOVA uses two types of effect size tests. One is eta-squared (_η_ _2_), which gives the strength of the overall difference of all levels of the independent variable given the dependent variable. Equation ([7.10](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) shows that _η_ _2_ is the ratio of sum of squares between and sum of squares between plus sum of squares withing, thus considering the difference between variance within groups and between groups. Eta squared is interpreted as the following (Cohen, [1973](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

* <0.09: Small effect size
* 0.09 to 0.24: Medium effect size
* \>0.25: Large effect size

η2\=SSBSSB+SSW

(7.10)

Effect size for post-hoc pairwise tests typically are calculated using Cohen’s _d_, which is covered earlier in the chapter. Cohen’s d is calculated for every pair of post-hoc tests (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter), [2015](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Levine & Hullett, [2002](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Norouzian & Plonsky, [2018](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

## 7.5 Kruskal-Wallis Test: Non-parametric ANOVA

Since we ran our ANOVA test with normality and homogeneity of variance violated, it would be better to run a Kruskal-Wallis (KW). The KW test is like the Mann-Whitney U in that it uses rank instead of means and variance to test the null hypothesis that the means of groups are not different. Since KW may use more categories of groups than Mann-Whitney _U_, the calculation is a bit more involved. Equation ([7.11](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)) provides the equation where _N_ is the total number of observations (or the sum of all sample sizes from each group), _k_ is the number of groups; _n_ _i_ is the number of observations in the _i_ _th_ group, _R_ _i_ is the sum of the ranks of the observations of the _i_ _th_ group, and Ri2 is the sum of the squared ranks of the observations in the ith group. Once calculated, _H_ is compared to a chi-square distribution table with K-1 degrees of freedom to determine statistical significance. In some cases, the _n_ _i_ denominator is not used (Bates, [n.d.](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Kruskal & Wallis, [1952](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter); Najab et al., [2010](A668427_1_En_7_Chapter)).

H\=12NN+1∑i\=1kniRi2ni−3N+1

(7.11)

## 7.6 Upgrades to Independent Samples Group Comparisons

Building on the introduction to one-way ANOVA, the intermediate book in the series will expand these techniques to accommodate more complex designs that allow researchers to examine multiple independent variables simultaneously. Factorial ANOVA enables the analysis of two or more categorical predictors and their interactions on a single continuous outcome, while ANCOVA introduces continuous covariates to control for additional sources of variation. MANOVA extends the framework further by analyzing multiple dependent variables at once, with variants such as factorial MANOVA and MANCOVA providing tools for testing multiple outcomes while controlling for covariates. These enhancements encourage multivariate thinking and allow for more nuanced interpretations of group differences.
