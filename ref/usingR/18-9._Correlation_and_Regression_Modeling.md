## Abstract

This chapter introduces regression analysis, focusing on three commonly used types: bivariate, multiple, and logistic regression. Bivariate regression examines the relationship between two scale variables, multiple regression explores how two or more independent variables predict a scale dependent variable, and logistic regression assesses how one or more independent variables predict a binary outcome. Key concepts, including correlation coefficients, regression equations, effect sizes, and statistical assumptions, are explained with practical examples and R code demonstrations. While the chapter covers foundational models, more advanced regression techniques, such as ordinal logistic regression, are addressed in later chapters and subsequent books in the series.

## 9.1 Evaluation of Relationships Between Variables

Sometimes, researchers are interested in the relationship between an independent variable and one or more dependent variable rather than differences between groups. Therefore, the purpose of this section is to introduce the reader to the concept of regression analysis covering bivariate regression, multiple regression, and logistic regression. The array of statistical models under regression analysis is much broader than what is covered in this chapter, and an overview of many of these is provided in Chap. [11](./20-11._More_to_Come.md). In addition, the next book in this series will cover more sophisticated models, which include things such as ordinal logistic regression. This chapter, however, will cover three commonly utilized types of regression. The first, bivariate regression (or bivariate correlation) examines the relationship between two scale variables. The second, multiple regression, examines how two or more independent variables predict a scale dependent variable. The third, logistic regression, examines how one or more independent variables predict a dichotomous (two-level) dependent variable (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Leech et al., [2015](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Tabachnick et al., [2019](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

## 9.2 Bivariate Correlation Analysis

Before diving into bivariate correlation, it is helpful to review the algebraic equation that underlies simple linear regression: _y = a + bx + e_. In this equation, _y_ represents the outcome or dependent variable that we are trying to predict. The term _a_ is the intercept, which is the predicted value of _y_ when _x_ equals zero. The variable _x_ is the predictor or independent variable, and _b_ is the slope coefficient that indicates the expected change in _y_ for a one-unit change in _x_. The final term, _e_, represents the error or residual, which is the difference between the observed value of _y_ and the value predicted by the equation. Understanding this equation is essential because bivariate correlation examines the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables. While correlation focuses on the degree to which two variables move together, regression extends this concept by quantifying the relationship and allowing predictions. The slope _b_ reflects the same information captured in the correlation coefficient but in units of _y_ per unit of _x_, and the intercept _a_ provides a reference point for where the line crosses the _y_\-axis. Together, these components allow researchers to move from describing relationships to modeling and predicting outcomes in a systematic way (Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

For example, let us assume our astronaut friend wanted to predict the height of a human (_y_) given their shoe size (_x_), so she sampled 20 humans on Earth, recorded their shoe sizes (converting them to men’s equivalent sizes to standardize them) and came up with a dataset as shown in Table [9.1](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter). ### 9.2.1 Pearson’s Moment Product r Correlation

Pearsons’s correlation coefficient, denoted as _r_, is a statistical measure that quantifies the strength and direction between two scale variables. It produces a value (_r_) between 1 and −1, the closer to |1| indicating a stronger correlation. If the value is positive, as Y increases, so does X. For example, the correlation between shoe size and height is certainly positive as shown in Fig. [9.1](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter). In contrast, if the _r is_ negative then as X increases, Y decreases. The correlation is 0, then there is no relationship. Figure [9.2](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter) shows positive, negative, and no correlation examples. Figure [9.2](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter) and Table [9.2](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter) offer interpretation of |_r_| correlation coefficients (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

Statistical Assumptions of Pearson’s r

Though this statistic is robust against many of these assumptions (Nefzger & Drasgow, [1957](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)), it is important to consider them and check them when conducting a correlation analysis. First, there is the assumption of a **linear relationship** between both variables, meaning there is a straight line. We examine the scatterplot for this. Second, we assume that both variables are **normally distributed**. We check this with QQ plots or a Shapiro-Wilke test. Next, we check for **homogeneity of variance** with Lavene’s test or a scatterplot of residuals. Then we check for **independence of observations**, meaning each data point is independent of others. Finally, we check to see if there are **no outliers** in the data. The extent to which these matter is debated in statistics, and much research emphases that a linear relationship and scale dependent variables matter the most (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Schober et al., [2018](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

Calculating Pearson’s r

If the statistical assumptions are met, the parametric Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficient _r_ can be used to calculate the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables. The equation for calculating _r_ is as shown in Eq. ([9.1](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)). The numerator is calculated by computing the deviations of each data point in variable _x_ from its mean, and similarly for variable y_._ These deviations are multiplied pairwise meaning each deviation of _x_ is multiplied by the corresponding deviation of _y_ and all these products are summed. This gives the numerator, which represents the sum of the products of the deviations.

For the denominator, calculate the squared deviations for each variable separately. That is, subtract the mean of _x_ from each value of _x_, square the resulting differences, and do the same for _y_. Then, sum the squared deviations for _x_ and _y_, and take the square root of the product of these two sums. This gives the denominator, which standardizes the numerator by accounting for the variance in both variables.

Finally, divide the numerator (sum of the products of the deviations) by the denominator (the square root of the product of the squared deviations) to obtain the correlation coefficient _r_. The value of _r_ will range between −1 and 1, where _r_ \= 1 indicates a perfect positive linear relationship, _r_ \= −1 indicates a perfect negative linear relationship, and _r_ \= 0 indicates no linear relationship. The closer _r_ is to either −1 or 1, the stronger the linear relationship between the variables (Kuhlman, [1929](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

r\=∑xi−x¯yi−y¯∑xi−x¯2∑yi−y¯2

(9.1)

### 9.2.2 Spearman Rank ρ Correlation

If assumptions of Pearson’s _r_ are grossly violated (even though many consider it to be robust), a Spearman Rank correlation rho (_ρ_) can be used. Equation ([9.2](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)) shows how to calculate _p_. Lard statistics has a good tutorial on this (Spearman’s Rank-Order Correlation, [2025](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)). In general, begin by ordering the values from largest to smallest for each variable, then rank them. Then subtract the values of _x_ from _y_ (or vice-versa) to get _d_, which is then squared. Sum the squared values and then divide by _n_ (the number of rows) times n-squared minus 1\. The result is a value between −1 and 1 (Kendall, [1949](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Spearman, [1904](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

ρ\=1−6∑di2nn2−1

(9.2)

To answer the research question about how biological parents’ height predicts the height of their adult child, the astronaut will conduct a multiple regression analysis. Multiple regression takes binary regression and simply adds one or more variables to the independent variable list. Thus, the equation for multiple regression is _y_ \= _a_ \+ _b_1_x_1 \+ _b_2_x_2⋯ + _b_ _i_ _x_ _i_ \+ _error_ (Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Tabachnick et al., [2019](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter))_._ In this equation, _y_ is the dependent variable, _a_ is the intercept, _b_1, _b_2 … _b_i are the regression coefficients representing the effect of each independent variable (_x_1, _x_2, …, _x_ _i_) on the dependent variable, and the error term represents the portion of _y_ that is not explained by the predictors. The error captures all other factors influencing the outcome that are not included in the model, as well as random variability. In other words, it is the difference between the observed value of _y_ and the value predicted by the combination of independent variables.

For this specific research question, the equation can be written as: _height_ \= _a_ \+ _b_1(_biom_) + _b_2(_biof_) + _error_. Here, _height_ is the dependent variable (adult child’s height), _biom_ represents the biological mother’s height, _biof_ represents the biological father’s height, and the error term captures all other unmeasured influences on the child’s height, including environmental factors, nutrition, and measurement variability. The astronaut will load this model into R to examine how the heights of the parents predict the adult child’s height. Before running the model, however, she must check the statistical assumptions for multiple regression, which include those shared with bivariate regression and additional assumptions such as absence of multicollinearity among predictors and homoscedasticity of residuals.

### 9.3.1 Multiple Regression Statistical Assumptions

The first statistical assumption of multiple regression is there is a **linear relationship** between the dependent variable (height) and the independent variables (biological parents’ heights). This is checked using scatterplots. The second statistical assumption is there is **independence of errors**, meaning there is no correlation between the residuals, or the difference between the predicted values and the actual values. This is checked using a Durbin-Watson test. The third assumption is **homoscedasticity**, or constant variance of the errors, meaning the spread of the residuals is constant across all levels of the independent variables tested with a Breusch-Pagan Test and a plot of residuals vs fitted values. Fourth, the **residuals should fit a normal distribution**, checked with QQ plots. Fifth, there should be no **multicolinearity**, meaning the dependent variables should not be highly correlated, this is checked with a variance inflation factor (VIF) statistical test and values >5 suggest multicollinearity. Finally, there should be **independence of observations** (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Leech et al., [2015](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Tabachnick et al., [2019](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

If there are violations of the multiple regression assumptions, various solutions can be applied. For violations of **linearity**, transforming the variables using logarithmic, square root, or polynomial terms can help, or you might consider using a non-linear regression model. In cases where there is a violation of **independence of errors**, introducing time or group identifiers into the model or employing generalized least squares (GLS) to account for autocorrelation can resolve the issue. For **heteroscedasticity** (non-constant variance), using weighted least squares (WLS) regression or transforming the dependent variable may stabilize the variance and improve the model. When **normality of residuals** is violated, transforming the dependent variable, using robust regression techniques, or applying bootstrapping methods can help in obtaining accurate p-values and confidence intervals. Lastly, in the presence of **multicollinearity**, one approach is to drop one of the highly correlated variables, combine them into a composite variable, or use ridge regression or principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce redundancy and mitigate the impact on the model’s estimates (Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Tabachnick et al., [2019](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

### 9.3.2 Effect Size R2

While bivariate correlation reports an _r_ value, multiple regression reports an _R_2 value. The calculation of is, SumofSquaresresidualSumofSquarestotal. From that a \[capital\] _R_ can be calculated by simply R2. The interpretation of _R_2 often includes “the percent of the variance explained.” This indicates the proportion of the variation in the dependent variable (_Y_) that can be accounted for by the independent variables (_X_1, _X_2, …, _X_ _p_) included in the model. In simpler terms, it tells you how much of the changes in the dependent variable can be explained by the factors you have measured or included in the model. _R_2 ranges from 0 to 1\. The closer to 1, the stronger the model as an _R_2 of 1 would indicate that 100% of the variance in the dependent variable is explained by the independent variables, meaning a perfect prediction (Stevens, [2007](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

Sometimes R will report an adjusted _R_2 value, a modified version of _R_2 (coefficient of determination) that adjusts for the number of predictors in a regression model. While _R_2 tells you how much of the variance in the dependent variable is explained by the model, it has a limitation: it will always increase when adding more predictors to the model, even if those predictors do not improve the model. Adjusted _R_2 addresses this issue by taking the number of predictors and the sample size into account. Equation ([9.3](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)) shows how it is adjusted where _p_ is the number of predictors (independent variables) in the model and _n_ is the sample size (Kutner et al., [2005](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

Radj2\=1−1−R2xn−1n−p−1

(9.3)

### 9.4.1 Overview of Logistic Regression

Logistic regression uses a similar equation as multiple regression to make predictions. However, since the dependent variable is binary, it uses the log of the odds of obtaining the “1” of the dependent variable. Equation ([9.4](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)) illustrates how it is calculated. The left side of the equation begins with _P_(_Y_ \= 1) which represents the probability (_P_) of the outcome occurring (_Y_ \= 1). The ratio PY\=11−PY\=1 defines the odds of the outcome occurring. Taking the natural logarithm of the odds yields the log-odds transformation (logit): logPY\=11−PY\=1. The right side of the equation includes the intercept (_β_0), and then the regression coefficients given the independent variables (_β_ _n_ _X_ _n_) (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Vaske, [2008](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

logPY\=11−PY\=1\=β0+β1X1+β2X2…βnXn

(9.4)

There are three reasons why logistic regression converts odds to logit odds. First, this ensures that the predicted values fall between 0 and 1 range. Without the log odds transformation, a linear regression model could predict values outside the valid range of probabilities (i.e., less than 0 or greater than 1), which is not meaningful in the context of probabilities. By transforming to the log-odds scale, the logistic function (also called the sigmoid function) maps the result of the linear equation (the logit) to a value between 0 and 1, ensuring that the predicted probabilities are valid. Second, the transformation makes the relationship between the predictor and the outcome more linear. If we did not apply the log transformation, the relationship between predictors and the probability could be non-linear, which complicates the model and the interpretation of coefficients. Finally, it provides a clear interpretation of the results. This transformation makes it possible to interpret the results in terms of odds ratios (Cox & Snell, [1989](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

### 9.4.2 Statistical Assumptions of Logistic Regression

Logistic regression analysis relies on several key statistical assumptions to ensure the validity of its results. The first assumption is that the **dependent variable must be binary**. In this case, the outcome variable, getting arrested (Yes = 1, No = 0), satisfies this requirement. Additionally, _observations must be independent_, meaning that everyone’s data point is unrelated to the others.

Another important assumption is the **linearity of the logit function** with respect to the continuous predictor variables. Unlike linear regression, logistic regression does not assume a direct linear relationship between the independent and dependent variables; rather, the log-odds of the dependent variable should have a linear relationship with the predictors. This can be assessed using the Box-Tidwell test, which checks whether a transformed predictor (e.g., GPA \* log(GPA)) significantly improves the model. Additionally, plotting the logit-transformed probability of getting arrested against the predictor variables with a smoothed line in _ggplot2_ can visually inspect this assumption (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Hosmer & Stanley, [2013](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

**Multicollinearity among predictor variables should also be minimal**, as strong correlations between independent variables can distort coefficient estimates. This can be checked by computing Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), where values above 10 indicate severe collinearity. A correlation matrix can further reveal whether any predictors have correlations > |0.8|, which could suggest redundancy in the model. If high multicollinearity is detected, one possible solution is to remove or combine correlated predictors (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Hosmer & Stanley, [2013](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

**The presence of extreme outliers** can affect logistic regression results, particularly if a few observations disproportionately affect the estimated coefficients. To check for outliers, boxplots of each predictor can be examined for extreme values, and standardized residual plots can help identify influential points. Additionally, Cook’s distance can highlight observations that significantly impact the model’s predictions (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Hosmer & Stanley, [2013](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

**Sufficient sample size** is also crucial for logistic regression to ensure stable estimates. A common guideline is to have at least 10 events per predictor variable (e.g., at least 10 individuals who got arrested and 10 who did not for each predictor). Given the small sample size of 20 in this case, the analysis may be underpowered, potentially leading to unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals. If necessary, increasing the sample size or collapsing categories may improve statistical reliability (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Hosmer & Stanley, [2013](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

Finally, logistic regression assumes that there is **no perfect separation in the data**, meaning that no predictor completely determines the outcome. If all individuals with an Arrested have a GPA above a certain threshold while all non-arrested participants fall below it, the model may struggle to converge. This can be detected by examining cross-tabulations of the predictor variables and the outcome. If perfect separation exists, techniques such as Firth’s logistic regression or regularization methods may be necessary to produce meaningful (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter); Hosmer & Stanley, [2013](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

### 9.4.3 Odds Ratios

In logistic regression, odds ratios (ORs) provide a way to interpret the effect of each predictor on the likelihood of an event occurring. In review, the odds of the outcome are defined as the probability of the event occurring divided by the probability of it not occurring: PY\=11−PY\=1. As shown in Eq. ([9.5](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)), an odds ratio compares the odds at two levels of a predictor, typically differing by one unit.

OR\=OddsatXn+1OddsatXn

(9.5)

This ratio indicates how the odds of the outcome change when the predictor increases by one unit. An OR greater than 1 means that increasing the predictor increases the odds of the event occurring, an OR less than 1 means that increasing the predictor decreases the odds, and an OR equal to 1 indicates that the predictor has no effect on the odds. By comparing the odds at different levels of the predictor, the odds ratio provides a standardized and interpretable measure of effect size in logistic regression models.

Equivalently, an odds ratio can be calculated by exponentiating the regression coefficient from the logistic model: OR = eβn. Here, _β_ _n_ is the coefficient for the predictor variable. The odds ratio represents the change in the odds of the outcome for a one-unit increase in the predictor. Specifically, an OR greater than 1 indicates that increasing the predictor increases the odds of the outcome, an OR less than 1 indicates decreasing odds, and an OR of 1 indicates no effect. In R, odds ratios can be obtained directly from model coefficients using _exp(coef())_ (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter)).

By converting coefficients into odds ratios, we can more easily interpret the influence of the predictors in real-world terms. However, it is crucial to remember that odds ratios should be considered in the context of the entire model and the significance of each predictor. A significant odds ratio indicates a meaningful relationship between the predictor and the outcome, while a non-significant odds ratio suggests no reliable effect.

### 9.4.6 Results Writeup for Logistic Regression Analysis

Logistic Regression Analysis for Predicting the Likelihood of Getting Arrested

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine whether high school GPA and college GPA were significant predictors of being arrested. The outcome variable was whether an individual had been arrested (coded 0 = No, 1 = Yes), and the predictors were high school GPA and college GPA. Table [9.9](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter) provides descriptive statistics and frequencies by arrest status. All assumptions for logistic regression were checked and met, including linearity in the log-odds, absence of multicollinearity, and no influential outliers. Variance inflation factors (VIFs = 1.38 for both predictors) indicated no multicollinearity concerns. An analysis of deviance comparing the full model (including high school GPA and college GPA) to the null model (no predictors) indicated that the addition of the predictors marginally improved model fit, _χ_2(2, _n_ \= 20) = 4.97, _p_ \= .083\. This suggests that, collectively, high school and college GPA provide limited improvement in predicting the likelihood of being arrested. The logistic regression coefficients are shown in Table [9.10](A668427_1_En_9_Chapter). Results indicated that college GPA was a marginally significant positive predictor of arrest likelihood (_β_ \= 2.43, _SE_ \= 1.42, _p_ \= .087), while high school GPA was not a significant predictor (_β_ \= −2.12, _SE_ \= 1.34, _p_ \= .113). The odds ratio for college GPA was 11.32, meaning that for each one-point increase in college GPA, the odds of being arrested were approximately 11 times higher. Conversely, the odds ratio for high school GPA was 0.12, suggesting that higher high school GPA was associated with lower odds of being arrested, though this relationship was not statistically significant.
