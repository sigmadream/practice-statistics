#  8.1 Time series features 

The trend, seasonality, cyclical, and remainder components define an observed time series and provide insights into the underlying data generation process. Other attributes, such as mean, variance, and autocorrelation, are calculated from a series and provide information about the observed system. Just as we can compare the mean and variance of two series to comment on their population distributions, time series features, such as the strength of trend or tiling window variance can be used to compare different time series in a similar way.

In other words, time series features can be used to quantify the characteristics of a time series in a way that is independent of specific values in the series. This allows us to compare different time series even if they have different scales or units of measurement.

For example, you could compare two sales series using their strength-of-trend feature. A higher strength of trend would indicate that the sales are increasing or decreasing more rapidly. You could also use the tiled-window-variance feature to compare the variability of two different time series. A higher tiling window variance would indicate that the values in the time series are more spread out.

By comparing time series features, you can gain a better understanding of the similarities and differences between various time series. This can be helpful for a variety of tasks, such as forecasting, anomaly detection, and time series classification.

The ` `` feasts `` ` (which stands for **Feature Extraction and Statistics for Time Series** ) package (O’Hara-Wild et al., 2023) provides a collection of feature computations for time series data. You have already used functions from this package, such as ` `` ACF() `` ` , ` `` gg_season() `` ` , ` `` gg_subseries() `` ` , and so on, in previous chapters.

The computed features and statistics from the ` `` feasts `` ` package can be arranged into several groups for explanation and usage purposes:

-  Autocorrelation-based features
-  STL-based features
-  Tiling window features
-  Sliding window features
-  Statistical test-based features
-  Statistical summary-based features
-  Other useful features

In the examples of this chapter, most features are computed using default parameters and are optimized where required. However, you can test these arguments and find optimal settings for your data and use case. The important part is to understand what these features offer and how to use them appropriately.

#  8.3 Autocorrelation based features 

The **autocorrelation functions** ( **ACF** ) measure the long-term strength and direction of the linear relationship between the current values and the lagged values in a time series. The autocorrelation coefficient at various lags from an original series indicates the strength and direction of trend. The ACFs computed from a first-differenced series indicate the effectiveness of first differencing, whether that has stabilized the mean and variance over time. The ACFs from second differencing indicate whether double differencing introduces an unwanted dependency structure due to over-differencing. The **Partial Autocorrelation Function** ( **PACF** ) shows the overall strength of short-term relationships.

*Table 8.1* provides a short description of ACF and PACF features. In general, they provide insights into how strong the trend in the original series is and what level of non-seasonal and seasonal differencing is required to make a series stationary:

|  **Feature** |  **Description** |
|----|----|
| ` `` acf1 `` ` |  The autocorrelation at lag 1 of the original series |
| ` `` acf10 `` ` |  The sum of squared autocorrelations for lags 1 through 10 of the original series |
| ` `` diff1_acf1 `` ` |  The autocorrelation at lag 1 of the first-differenced series |
| ` `` diff1_acf10 `` ` |  The sum of squared autocorrelations for lags 1 through 10 of the first-differenced series |
| ` `` diff2_acf1 `` ` |  The autocorrelation at lag 1 of the second-differenced series |
| ` `` season_acf1 `` ` |  The autocorrelation at the first seasonal lag 1 of the original series (lag 12 for monthly data) |
| ` `` pacf5 `` ` |  The sum of the squared partial autocorrelations for lags 1 through 5 of the original series |
| ` `` diff1_pacf5 `` ` |  The sum of the squared partial autocorrelations for lags 1 through 5 after one round of non-seasonal differencing |
| ` `` diff2_pacf5 `` ` |  The sum of the squared partial autocorrelations for lags 1 through 5 after two rounds of non-seasonal differencing |
| ` `` season_pacf `` ` |  The partial autocorrelation at the first seasonal lag (lag 12 for monthly data) |

Table 8.1: Description of ACF and PACF based features

#  8.4 STL-based features

#  8.5 Tiling window features 

The ` `` var_tiled_mean `` ` (“stability”) and ` `` var_tiled_var `` ` (“lumpiness”) features analyze the time series by dividing it into a series of non-overlapping “tiled” windows to assess how the central tendency and volatility change over time:

- ` `` var_tiled_mean `` ` : This feature measures how stable the mean of the series is over the full length of the observed period. For non-seasonal data, the size of the overlapping tile is <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_051.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_051.png</span> . - ` `` var_tiled_var `` ` : This feature calculates the variance of the variance across all the different non-overlapping windows (tiles).

#  8.6 Sliding window features 

Three features are computed on sliding windows (over-lapping windows) that measure when the maximum shift occurs at the average level, variance and in data distributions. They also report the time index when those shifts occurred:

- ` `` shift_level_max `` ` : Measures the largest mean shift between consecutive sliding windows. This is useful for identifying sudden jumps or drops in the average level (i.e., structural breaks) of the time series.
- ` `` shift_var_max `` ` : Calculates the largest variance shift between consecutive sliding windows. This feature is useful for detecting when a stable process becomes noisy, or vice versa, by capturing changes in the volatility.
- ` `` shift_kl_max `` ` : The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence measures the difference between two probability distributions. By computing the KL divergence on sliding windows of a time series, the ` `` shift_kl_max `` ` feature identifies the time and magnitude of the largest distributional shift. It is the most comprehensive measure of change as it detects shifts in the overall shape of the data distribution (average level, variance, and shape) between different periods.

Let’s compute the sliding windows features for the example series:

sw_feat <- retail_df |>
  features(turnover,
           list(shift_level_max,
                shift_var_max,
                shift_kl_max))

Next, we discuss features that are based on statistical properties of a time series.

#  8.7 Statistical test-based features 

In time series analysis, we use different statistical tests to decide whether a series has unit roots, or how many differences are needed to make a series stationary. An important model fitting diagnostic is to check whether the residual from a model is white noise.

A unit root is a property in a time series where the average level shifts randomly over time. This makes the series level wise non-stationary and causes problems in parameters estimation and statistical inference. If a time series has unit root, then it needs to be differenced (i.e., made stationary) before applying any model.

There are various tests available to identify unit roots. The following features compute the test statistic and corresponding p-value to determine the existence of unit roots in a time series. In other words, these features identify whether a series is stationary or not:

- ` `` unitroot_kpss `` ` : This function computes the statistic from the Kwiatkowski et al.  (1992) unit root test with linear trend and lag <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_062.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_062.png</span>
- ` `` unitroot_pp `` ` : This function computes the statistic for the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_063.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_063.png</span> version of Phillips and Perron unit root test with constant trend and lag <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_064.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_064.png</span>

There are various arguments in the functions that you can study further and apply to your scenario.

Once you have identified that a time series is not stationary, the next step is to figure out how to make it stationary. A non-stationary series becomes stationary via a simple operation- taking the difference from its consecutive values for a non-seasonal series or taking the difference at seasonal periods for a seasonal series. Sometimes a double difference is required to make a series stationary. The following two features provide you with the optimal number of differencing required to obtain a stationary series:

- ` `` unitroot_ndiffs() `` ` : Identify the optimal differencing order that lies between 0 to 2 by applying the Kwiatkowski et al.  (1992) unit root test.
- ` `` unitroot_nsdiffs() `` ` : Identify the optimal seasonal differencing order by evaluating the seasonal strength of an STL decomposition. Even though this method does not depend on a statistical test, it works well in identifying the order of seasonal differencing by evaluating the strength of the seasonal component in the data.

Let’s check the stationarity status of the two example series, and what level of differencing is needed to make them stationary. You can perform all the unitroot tests using the following code:

```
uroot_feat <- retail_df |>
  features(turnover,
           list(unitroot_kpss,
                unitroot_pp,
                unitroot_ndiffs,
                unitroot_nsdiffs))
```

The series will become stationary after taking non-seasonal differencing once (subtract the previous consecutive value from each observation) and seasonal differencing once (subtract values of the same previous season from an observation). The trend adjustment ( ` `` ndiffs `` ` = <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_067.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_067.png</span> ) and the seasonal adjustment ( ` `` nsdiffs `` ` = <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_068.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_068.png</span> ) helps a forecast model to accurately capture data characteristics.

Once a forecast model is fitted, it is expected that the residuals from the forecast model should be white noise. That means, the residuals should be serially uncorrelated, have zero mean, constant variance (usually <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_069.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_069.png</span> ), and be independent and identically distributed. A forecasting model diagnostic usually involves testing the autocorrelation structure in residuals. If the residuals are not white noise, then it suggests that the model could not capture the data dynamics well enough and needs improvement.

Two statistical tests, Ljung-Box and Box-Pierce, are commonly used to test white noise. Both tests examine the null hypothesis of independence in a given time series by computing autocorrelation at different lags and testing whether the sum of squared autocorrelation coefficients equals <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_070.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_070.png</span> . The main difference between these two tests is that they use slightly different test statistics.

Let’s check whether the remainder series from the STL decomposition of the turnover series is white noise, using the following code:

```
retail_df |>
  model(stl = STL(turnover)) |>
  components() |>
  features(remainder,
           list(ljung_box, box_pierce)) |>
  mutate(across(3:6, \(x)round(x ,2))) |>
  select( - .model)
```

#  8.8 Other useful features 

Apart from what has already been discussed, the features listed in this section provide valuable insights about data characteristics:

- ` `` guerro() `` ` : Identify the optimal <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_076.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_076.png</span> value for a Box-Cox transformation using the Guerrero method. - ` `` coef_hurst() `` ` : The Hurst coefficient measures the degree to which a time series exhibits long-term memory. A value of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_077.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_077.png</span> or below indicates that the time series is a random walk, which means there is no long-term memory and that is difficult to forecast. A value greater than <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_078.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_078.png</span> indicates that the time series is likely to exhibit patterns that persist over a long period of time and could be easier to forecast.
- ` `` n_crossing_points `` ` : Computes the number of times a time series crosses the median.
- ` `` longest_flat_spot `` ` : Flat spots are computed by dividing the sample space of a time series into <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_079.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_079.png</span> equal-sized intervals and computing the maximum width within any single interval. This feature returns the number of intervals for which the data is relatively unchanged.
- ` `` stat_arch_lm `` ` : Checks the Autoregressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity (ARCH) condition based on a Lagrange Multiplier (LM) test.
- ` `` feat_spectral `` ` : This feature provides information about the forecastability of a time series. Based on the Shannon spectral entropy, this measures how much or how little noise there is in the data. A value close to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_080.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_080.png</span> indicates that a series is easy to forecast as it has a strong trend and seasonal pattern. A value close to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_081.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_081.png</span> indicates that the series is noisy and will be difficult to forecast.
- ` `` feat_intermittent `` ` : This feature provides useful information about intermittent time series. It returns four values: the average interval between non-zero observations (zero run mean), the squared coefficient of variation of non-zero observations (nonzero squared), the proportion of data that starts with zero (zero start prop), and the proportion of data that ends with zero (zero end prop).

All these features provide important information about different characteristics of time series. They provide insights into time series that are helpful for either understanding time series data or applying appropriate forecasting models. However, not all features are needed in every scenario. The choice of features will depend on what type of data you have and how you plan to analyze them.

The primary goal of time series features is to examine data characteristics from various perspectives and to suggest which kind of model would be suitable for analysis and forecasting. They can also be utilized to group similar time series together. In the next section, you will learn how some of these features are used to group a large collection of time series data.

#  8.9 Clustering using time series features 

Time series features become a useful tool when you have a large collection of data and would like to summarize its characteristics. You may want to group series that have similar characteristics together. In literature multiple methods for time series clustering are available that incorporate features calculated from time series; see Wang et al. (2006) for details.

In the following example, you will find an application of feature-based time series clustering. The steps involved are the following:

-  Compute important features for each series.
-  Apply a dimension reduction technique, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or Unifold Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction (UMAP), to reduce the dimension without compromising data variability.
-  Apply a clustering technique, such as K-means or hierarchical clustering, to identify the optimal number of clusters for the entire dataset.

Each of these clusters consists of a series that shares common properties but is different from other clusters.

##  8.9.1 PCA on time series features 

PCA is a dimensionality reduction technique that is commonly used on a dataset with a large number of interrelated variables. PCA transforms a large set of potentially correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated components, known as the principal components (PCs). The PCs are statistically independent from one another. They provide a linear transformation of the original variables while capturing maximum variance (i.e., information) in the data.

So, why do we apply the PCA on the M3 dataset? In analytics, performing an explanatory analysis before modeling is a crucial step. PCA on features from M3 data will simplify the explanatory analysis. Instead of analyzing and visualizing each series individually, PCA will identify the most important patterns of variation and help us visualize this high-dimensional data ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_083.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_083.png</span> features reduced to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_08_084.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_08_084.png</span> PCAs). Among other things, it will identify any outliers (if any) and may highlight inherent clusters by combining series that exhibit similar patterns into a group.

A number of PCA implementations are available in various R packages. We will use the ` `` tidymodels `` ` implementation of PCA. The ` `` tidymodels `` ` framework ( <a href="https://www.tidymodels.org/" style="text-decoration: none;">  https://www.tidymodels.org/  </a> ) is an ecosystem of R packages designed to streamline and enhance the process of modeling and statistical analysis based on ` `` tidyverse `` ` principles. The ` `` tidymodels `` ` include packages such as ` `` parsnip `` ` for model specification, ` `` recipes `` ` for pre-processing, ` `` tune `` ` for hyper parameter tuning, and ` `` yardstick `` ` for model evaluation. A detailed discussion on ` `` tidymodels `` ` framework is beyond the scope of this book.

Let’s apply PCA on the data by performing the following steps:

-  Data processing steps are defined using the ` `` recipe() `` ` function.
-  The ~. formula specification denotes the use of PCA as an unsupervised technique, leveraging all available features in ` `` monthly_features `` ` dataset.
-  The ` `` series_name `` ` column is designated as an identifier using the ` `` update_role() `` ` function
-  The ` `` step_nzv() `` ` function will identify features with near zero variance. Since all the predictors in the data are numeric, the ` `` all_predictors() `` ` function selects them at once.
-  The ` `` step_normalize() `` ` function is applied to normalize all predictors.
-  The ` `` step_pca() `` ` function is used to transform the features into four PCs.

The code described in the preceding steps is shown here:

```
pca_recp <- recipe(~., data = monthly_features) |>
  update_role(series_name, new_role = "id") |>
  step_nzv(all_predictors()) |>
  step_normalize(all_predictors()) |>
  step_pca(all_predictors(), num_comp = 4)
```

Apply the ` `` prep() `` ` function on the recipe object to performs the preprocessing steps, and fit the PCA model using the training data, ` `` monthly_features `` ` :

```
pca_prep <- prep(pca_recp, training = monthly_features)
```

##  8.9.2 Clustering using time series features, UMAP, and K-means 

UMAP (Leland et al., 2018) is a dimension reduction technique to simplify complex datasets by creating a graphical representation that shows how data points relate to each other. It compresses the information into a smaller space while keeping the most important connections and structures (both local and global) intact. This technique is particularly efficient at capturing non-linear relationships.

We use the ` `` umap() `` ` implementation from the ` `` uwot `` ` package. Before applying UMAP on the ` `` monthly_features `` ` data, the near zero variance features and the ID variables are removed. The parameters in the following code are provided as a guide, and you are encouraged to experiment. The ` `` set.seed() `` ` function is used for reproducibility.

```
nzv_var <- c("zero_run_mean","zero_start_prop","zero_end_prop")
id_var <- "series_name"

set.seed(121123)
umap_mod <- uwot::umap(

To identify the optimal number of clusters, we will repeatedly run K-means clustering on the UMAP embedding, testing between two to eight clusters.

There are many K-means implementations available in multiple R packages. Here, we use the ` `` eclust() `` ` function from the ` `` factoextra `` ` package:

```
kmean_list <- list()
for (j in 2:8){
  kmean_list[[j]] <- factoextra::eclust(
    umap_embed |>
      select(-series_name),
    FUNcluster = "kmeans",
    k = j,
    k.max = j,
    graph = FALSE,
    nboot = 0,
    verbose = FALSE
  )
}
names(kmean_list) <- 2:8
```

The optimal cluster is the one for which any further grouping provides minimal reduction in the total within sum of squares values. Let’s extract the optimal number of clusters identified using the following code:

```
km_totwss <- tibble(
  clust_num = seq(2,8,1),
  totwss =  do.call(c, lapply(kmean_list, '[[', "tot.withinss")))

opt_cluster <- km_totwss |>
  mutate( first_diff = totwss - lag(totwss)) |>
  mutate(second_diff = first_diff - lag(first_diff)) |>
  drop_na() |>
  slice_max(second_diff)
```

#  Summary 

In this chapter, we have discussed time series features. There is a large collection of features that can be computed from a time series. Some features are based on the ACF, while some are purely based on statistical tests. They vary from simple descriptive statistics (mean) to information theory (Shannon entropy). The time series features provide valuable insights when you need to analyze a large collection of data on a regular basis.

The use of time series features was highlighted with two detailed examples: PCA and UMAP techniques were used for dimension reduction to reduce many time series features into a smaller feature space. Further, the optimal number of clusters was identified via K-means, which segmented the whole M3 monthly dataset into four distinct groups.
