#  4.1 What is a time series? 

A time series is a sequence of observations recorded over multiple time periods for a single unit of interest. These measurements are typically taken at regular intervals, such as hourly, daily, monthly, or annually, creating a natural temporal ordering in the data. This chronological ordering might introduce dependence between successive observations. This means observations at the current time point may be influenced by past observations. This dynamic behavior is a defining feature of time series data and allows it to be analyzed in ways that are distinct from other types of data.

This book primarily explores the analysis of univariate time series, which refers to the study of temporal data collected from a single variable or unit of interest. Univariate time series are foundational in time series analysis and serve as the starting point for understanding more complex temporal structures.

However, as we progress through the chapters, we will extend our focus beyond univariate series to include both multivariate and multiple time series. These broader frameworks allow us to capture richer dynamics and interdependencies that arise in real-world data.

Multiple time series are collections of univariate time series that might be interrelated or independent in terms of time series dynamics. Several interrelated univariate time series observed simultaneously are generally referred to as multivariate time series. This structure is commonly encountered in macroeconomic datasets, for instance, the **gross domestic product (GDP)** growth rate, the unemployment rate, and the official interest rate of a country. Even though each of these can be analyzed independently as a univariate time series, when studied together, they often exhibit dynamic interactions. Such interaction can help to explain both short-term fluctuations and long-term equilibrium relationships between the series; both of them must be accounted for in a comprehensive analysis. In short, multivariate time series analysis incorporates modelling interdependence among multiple series collected at the same period.

It is important to note that multivariate time series typically involve only a small number of time series. This limitation arises because the complexity of the analysis increases rapidly with the number of series involved. Specifically, the volume of data required to perform robust multivariate analysis grows rapidly with each additional series, often making it impractical to include a large number of time series without compromising analytical feasibility. To avoid these constraints multivariate time series with a large number of univariate series (say, 10 or more) are often analyzed as independent univariate time series in the first stage and, in a subsequent stage of analysis, between series time dynamics are overlaid. In this book, this is categorized as a univariate time series.

#  4.2 Distinction of time series from other time-indexed data 

In real-life analysis, you might encounter situations where multiple units have data collected over time but are independent or need to be assumed independent due to an inadequate amount of data for enabling either a univariate or a multivariate time series analysis. This typically happens when univariate time series for tens to thousands of units are relatively short and/or have missing values for modeling the time dynamic adequately from individual series.

For example, suppose you have annual debt and turnover data for small-business customers (with turnover between \$500K and \$1M) for a bank for the last 10 years from the bank’s customer database. While it may seem intuitive to apply time series methods to each business individually to analyze their debt-to-turnover ratio, practical challenges often arise. Small businesses may evolve, disappear, or emerge over time, resulting in datasets with inconsistent temporal histories and varying levels of noise. These complexities can limit the effectiveness of standard time series techniques. This results in three distinct types of data, all of which can have a time dimension but are not necessarily considered time series data.

An effective approach to analyzing the debt profile of small businesses over time is through panel data analysis. Panel data combines both cross-sectional and temporal dimensions by capturing repeated observations for a relatively large number of units, such as businesses across multiple time periods. This structure enables you to study how changes in turnover influence debt levels while accounting for characteristics unique to each business, such as management practices or industry-specific factors. Even when some businesses have missing data for certain years, forming an unbalanced panel, the method remains robust. By leveraging both within-unit and between-unit variation, panel analysis allows for stronger attribution and inference and is particularly well suited to answer questions such as: Do businesses with growing turnover tend to reduce their debt ratios over time?

An alternative analytical strategy is longitudinal data analysis, which is particularly well suited to studying within-unit changes over time. Longitudinal data typically consists of observations for a relatively small number of units tracked across a moderate to extended time span. The central aim of this approach is to model individual trajectories and examine how characteristics evolve within each unit.

For instance, you might focus on a cohort of businesses established in 2015 and still operating in 2025, allowing for an in-depth exploration of the debt evolution over the business life cycle. This method is ideal for identifying patterns such as early-stage borrowing, mid-phase expansion, or late-stage deleveraging. Because longitudinal analysis relies on consistent tracking of the same units, it requires relatively complete data histories, and missing observations can significantly constrain the scope of analysis. Nevertheless, when such data is available, longitudinal methods offer powerful insights into how financial behavior develops and transforms within stable business units over time.

Cross-sectional analysis offers a valuable approach for examining the financial characteristics of businesses at a specific point in time. Unlike longitudinal or panel data, cross-sectional data does not capture temporal variation within units, making it unsuitable for analyses that require tracking changes over time. Instead, it provides a snapshot of the population, allowing researchers to explore patterns such as the distribution of debt-to-turnover ratios, identify high-risk businesses, or compare financial metrics across industries and regions. This approach can also be extended across multiple years by treating each year as a separate cross-section, enabling comparisons over time without the need to follow individual businesses longitudinally. Cross-sectional analysis is particularly useful when historical data is incomplete or when the analytical focus is on population-level insights rather than individual trajectories.

#  4.3 How to classify time-indexed data to apply suitable analytics 

There are formal statistical tests available to determine the most suitable analytical framework. When deciding whether to use panel, longitudinal, or time series methods, a simple heuristic may be helpful to guide the initial decision. One such approach is the use of the **N–T** decision space, where *N* represents the number of units (e.g., businesses, individuals, or households) in the dataset, and *T* denotes the number of time points covered. This framework provides a practical starting point for evaluating the structure of the data. *Figure 4.1* presents a convenient framework for classifying data structures commonly used in empirical analysis.

When *N* is large and *T* is small, panel data methods are typically appropriate, as they leverage cross sectional breadth with limited temporal depth. Conversely, when *T* is large and *N* is small, longitudinal analysis becomes more suitable, focusing on individual trajectories over time. If the dataset consists of a single unit observed over many time points, time series analysis is the natural choice.

This simple classification can help you align your analytical strategy with the inherent structure of the data, especially when dealing with incomplete records or limited metadata. However, it is important to recognize that this classification is approximate. The thresholds for the number of units (N) and time periods (T) are not rigidly defined; rather, they are context-dependent and often shaped by the specific analytical objectives at hand.

#  4.4 Components of a time series 

Components, also known as **features** , **patterns** or **characteristics** , are the building blocks of an observed time series. There are three main components: trend ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_04_001.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_04_001.png</span> ), seasonal ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_04_002.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_04_002.png</span> ), and cyclical ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_04_003.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_04_003.png</span> ). These components, along with random error ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_04_004.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_04_004.png</span> ), generate the observed time series.

##  4.4.1 Trend 

A trend is the long-term behavior in a time series depicted by an upward or downward movement over an extended period. This reflects a persistent increase or decrease in the measurements of an univariate time series and is often driven by factors, such as long term economic growth, technological progress, or both.

The main distinguishing factor to notice for trend is the persistent and consistent movement over a longer period. Identifying the trend is crucial for understanding the overall dynamics of a time series and analyzing what could account for such dynamics.

##  4.4.2 Seasonality 

Seasonality refers to the presence of repeating patterns in time series data that occur at regular intervals. These intervals, known as seasonal periods, are typically driven by systematic and recurring external factors such as the day of the week, the time of day, or the season of the year.

Each seasonal period is associated with a fixed number of observations, which is referred to as the frequency of the data. For example, in monthly time series data, the frequency is 12, corresponding to the 12 months in a year. This means that if a seasonal pattern exists, the data for a given month is expected to resemble the data from the same month in previous years.

Such patterns arise because similar conditions tend to recur at the same relative time each cycle. For instance, retail sales may consistently rise in December due to holiday shopping, or electricity usage may peak during summer months due to increased air conditioning. These recurring swings in the data reflect the influence of seasonal factors and are key to understanding and forecasting time series behavior.

Recognizing and modeling seasonality is essential for accurate predictions, as it allows you to separate short-term fluctuations from long-term trends and make more informed decisions based on historical patterns.

##  4.4.3 Cyclical 

In time series, a cyclical pattern refers to a long term fluctuation that occurs over an extended period. Generally, a peak-trough pattern repeats over many years. The cyclical pattern has variable width and magnitude, and these characteristics differentiate the cyclical pattern from a seasonal pattern, which has a fixed width.

An example of a cyclical pattern in time series is business cycles. Expansion and recession are common business cycles that appear in a lot of economic data. During an expansion phase, economic activity increases, often driven by rising consumer confidence, investment, and production. Conversely, a recession marks a period of declining economic performance, reduced spending, and contraction in business activity. However, each cycle does not repeat at fixed intervals. When a business senses it is about to enter a recession, it does everything to delay it, which makes the width wider than the previous recession cycle. In the expansion stage, a business adopts steps that make the upward movement steeper than the previous expansion period.

Understanding cyclical behavior is crucial for your analysis involving strategic planning, particularly in fields such as economics, finance, and policy making. Along with historical data and external factors and market dynamics that influence the timing and intensity of these cycles, you can perform a much deeper analysis.

##  4.4.4 Random errors or remainders 

Like any form of data, time series data is subject to random error or unpredictable variations that cannot be attributed to identifiable patterns such as trend, seasonality, or cyclical behavior. Once these structured components are accounted for, what remains is the residual or error component, which captures the influence of irregular, unforeseen events.

These errors often arise from disruptions in the underlying data generation process. For example, the COVID-19 pandemic had a major impact on sales, employment, and economic indicators across industries. Such events are not part of the regular dynamics of the data and cannot be anticipated by analyzing historical data.

While the random fluctuations are valuable for exploratory analysis often to understand the impact of past disruptions, they hold limited relevance for predictive modeling. Forecasting future values based on random error would require predicting future unexpected events such as pandemics, natural disasters, or geopolitical shocks, which is highly speculative.

Now that you know the different components of time series, let’s discuss how they can be present in data. Does one component always dictate the data, or is it a combination of components?

#  4.5 Combining time series components 

A time series is rarely composed of a single component. Instead, it typically reflects a combination of components: trend, seasonality, cyclical variation, and random error. The presence and strength of these components can vary significantly across datasets, and understanding their interplay is essential for effective analysis.

To exhibit temporal dynamics, a time series must contain at least one of the structured components: trend, seasonality, or cyclical patterns. In practice, it is common to encounter time series that exhibit all four components, including random error. The presence of different components can dictate how the data needs to be analyzed and modeled.

It is worth noting that the error component is always present in any time series. This is because no data is perfect, and there will always be some random fluctuations that cannot be explained. Conceptually, it is possible to imagine a time series that is made up of the error component only. However, such data will be better treated as cross sectional data and will benefit from using cross-sectional data analysis. This is because such a series lacks time dynamics.

#  4.6 Stationarity 

Stationarity is a fundamental property of a time series that indicates whether its statistical properties, such as mean, variance and the correlation structure, remain constant over time. In simple terms, a stationary series behaves consistently throughout its time duration. The way components are combined and repeated does not depend on the specific point in time at which the measurements are taken. This stability allows you to analyze the series with greater confidence as the underlying dynamics of the process are assumed to be time invariant.

In a stationary time series, the average level remains roughly constant as time progresses. Pure trend and trend-seasonal patterns are not stationary as the average level changes as time moves forward. Some pure cyclical patterns are stationary, such as the Canadian lynx in *Figure 4.4* . The number of unemployed in the US and sunspots series in the same figure are non-stationary due to the trend-cyclical pattern. All other example series you have seen in this chapter are non-stationary.

If a dataset shows departure from stationarity, such as the presence of trends or seasonality, it is a strong indicator that the data is a time series rather than cross-sectional. In this sense, departure from stationarity helps distinguish time series data from other types and thus considered a characteristic of a time series.

There are proper statistical methods to test stationarity in an observed series. If data is non-stationary, then suitable transformations such as differencing and seasonal differencing are performed to make it stationary before analysis.

Next, we will extend the well-known concept of correlation in the context of time series. Let’s see what autocorrelation means in a time series context.

#  4.7 Autocorrelation 

In cross-sectional data, correlation measures the strength of the linear relationship between two variables. A conceptually similar measure for time series data is known as autocorrelation, a defining feature of a time series. In fact, this characteristic is so important that its absence might indicate that data could be adequately analyzed without using a specialized time series analysis.

There are many other types of time series available in real scenarios:

-  **Intermittent time series** : In an intermittent series, the observations between time intervals are not constant. In this type of series, there is a period of observations followed by periods of no data or missing or zero observations. This can be due to a variety of factors, such as the nature of the process being measured, the availability of data, or the limitations of the measuring instrument. Sales data of a specialized product that depends on cultural or religious festivals are examples of intermittent time series. For example, Father’s Day cards are exclusively accessible a few weeks prior to the celebration, and the rest of the year the sale value would be 0.
-  **Irregular time series** : In irregular time series, the intervals between the observations are inconsistent, but they still can form a continuous sequence without gaps. The irregularity can occur due to varying measurement schedules, data collection methods, or other factors. For example, sales at different times of day will become an irregular time series. There will be an observation for each day, but due to inconsistent intervals, it will be hard to depict any patterns from the observed data within a day.
-  **Non-linear time series** : In non-linear time series data, relationships, patterns, or trends exhibit non-linear progression. This could be due to either parameters being non-linear ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_04_036.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_04_036.png</span> ) or past values having a non-linear impact ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_04_037.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_04_037.png</span> ) on the current value. The spreading of infectious disease is a perfect example of a non-linear series. Initially, the number of people affected grows exponentially and slows down toward the saturation point due to public health intervention. A regular time series with non-linearity over a time domain can be analyzed by making it linear using an appropriate transformation, such as logarithmic or square roots.

Analyzing irregular or non-linear time series requires a different set of tools, which is out of the scope of this book.
