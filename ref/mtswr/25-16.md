#  Changepoint Detection 

In our modern world, data is a continuous, high-speed flow where changes in processes are not just an event, but a constant state. When we deal with continuous signal processing, such as **electrocardiograms** ( **EEGs** ) or revenue uplift due to a new product launch, changes in the underlying data-generating process are inevitable. Changepoint detection is a rigorous statistical framework that is used to identify sudden, persistent shifts in the behavior of a system over time. While anomalies in a time series are short-lived spikes that quickly return to the previous baseline, changepoint detection captures persistent shifts, where the underlying data generation process shifts and enters to a “new normal.”

Changepoint detection allows us to identify systematic shifts, enabling responses to changes in market trends, equipment failure, patient health, or strategic business decisions. In this chapter, we discuss changepoint detection in the context of a retrospective study. From the methods discussed and the example presented, you will learn about prominent approaches to detecting changepoints in observed data.

#  16.1 What is changepoint detection? 

Time series often contain structural breaks or abrupt shifts in their underlying patterns. When a persistent shift occurs in fundamental statistical properties, such as mean, variance, or trend, the phenomenon is attributable to a changepoint. A changepoint pinpoints the boundary between two different segments of behavior.

Detection of a structural break is a topic of interest in any discipline with a focus on process monitoring. Some application areas include the following:

-  Finding structural breaks in economic time series, such as GDP, unemployment rate, and national debt
-  Identifying a regime change in financial time series, such as market volatility, returns, or trading volumes
-  Detecting transitions in physiological measurements, such as EEG, ECG, or blood pressure
-  Uncovering anomalous behavior or load changes in a telecommunications network
-  Detecting shifts in climate patterns, vegetation indices, or hydrological systems in environmental monitoring

The detection of changepoints opens doors to understanding the impact of events in the dataset and accounting for those changepoints to improve subsequent analysis and forecasting. For example, by detecting changepoints, you can reduce forecasting errors and also avoid invalid statistical inferences that could compromise the decision-making process. Changepoint detection is different from measuring intervention impact (covered in *<a href="Chapter_15.xhtml#h1_318" class="chapref"> Chapter 15 </a>* ). In changepoint detection, factors that contribute to change are considered external, and the analysis is retrospective in nature. On the other hand, causal impact measurement assumes that the factors arise from an internal business strategy or decisions, and the business wants to understand the impact of those strategies, whether to claim success or potential refinement of the strategy.

Changepoint detection techniques offer several powerful methodological approaches. The use of a suitable method should match the analytical needs of a problem.

*Table 16.1* provides a brief summary of the three changepoint detection methods that are covered in detail later in the chapter:

|  **Criteria** |  **Breaks for additive season and trend (BFAST)** |  **Pruned exact linear time (PELT)** |  **Bayesian approach (BEAST)** |
|----|----|----|----|
|  Overview |  A recursive approach that splits data in two parts at a single most significant changepoint, then repeats the process on each sub-segment |  An exact search algorithm that minimizes a cost function (mean or variance) across all possible segments, while non-optimal points are discarded |  A probabilistic method that updates the likelihood of a changepoint with each new data point, incorporating prior beliefs about the system |
|  Application conditions |  Works best when segments are relatively long and the number of changepoints is low |  Requires a predefined penalty value (e.g., AIC or BIC) to avoid overfitting |  Requires selecting appropriate prior distributions |
|  Suitability |  Suitable for very large datasets due to its computational efficiency |  Ideal for identifying the exact number and location of multiple changepoints |  Highly suitable for online (real-time) detection, where quantifying uncertainty and immediate response are critical |
|  Common use case |  Environmental monitoring, detecting land-cover changes, seasonal shifts in satellite imagery time series |  Finance and engineering, identifying regime switches in market volatility, structural breaks in sensor data |  Healthcare and cybersecurity, real-time monitoring of heart rates (ECG), detecting sudden spikes in network traffic |

Table 16.1: Comparison among changepoint detection methods

All of these methods are applicable to both seasonal and non-seasonal data. In this chapter, we have used the ` `` Nile `` ` data for an example of non-seasonal application and the ` `` AirPassengers `` ` data for a seasonal application. Using the same data for all three methods would also allow you to compare their performance.

Next, we will discuss the BFAST method for changepoint detection.

#  16.2 Breaks for Additive Season and Trend 

The Breaks for Additive Season and Trend (BFAST) framework, developed by Verbesselt et al. (2010), is analogous to binary segmentation as it splits the data into two segments in every iteration to check for changepoints. The recursive process terminates when a stopping criterion is met. BFAST is one of the most intuitive and computationally efficient approaches for detecting changepoints.

A central tool used in BFAST is the Moving Sum (MOSUM) test, which allows for detecting an unknown number of changepoints without assuming their locations in advance.

The key idea is that this representation captures regular patterns without reacting to short-term noise. Structural breaks in seasonality correspond to changes in the coefficients <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_015.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_015.png</span> . It is worth noting that for simplicity, seasonality can also be modeled using dummy variables. Once the trend and seasonal components are estimated from the time series, they are subtracted from the observed data, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_016.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_016.png</span> , to extract remainders, as follows:

If the model is stable, these remainders behave like random noise and fluctuate around <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_018.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_018.png</span> . If a structural change occurs, the residuals will show a systematic bias, and they will tend to be mostly positive or mostly negative for a period of time. The MOSUM test detects such instability by aggregating remainders over short time windows.

The expression <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_019.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_019.png</span> is equivalent to the residuals of a regression model. In this sense, the right-hand side in *Equation (16.1)* can be interpreted as the difference between the observed values and the corresponding fitted values. The fitted values are obtained using **ordinary least squares** ( **OLS** ) estimates of the parameters in the trend and seasonal components of the model. Consequently, the MOSUM statistics employed in BFAST can be viewed as being computed on OLS residuals. This procedure is commonly referred to as OLS-based MOSUM (OLS-MOSUM) in the literature and in the R package we adopted.

In previous chapters, we used STL decomposition to separate (or estimate) remainders from an observed series. Can we use that method in the BFAST scenario? STL decomposition could be an option; however, by expressing trend and seasonality as explicit models, we can test whether their parameters remain stable. The initial definition of “normal” trend and seasonality strongly determines what is detected as change later in the process. Without such a model assumption, we won’t be able to perform a test for departure.

The MOSUM test starts with a pre-determined fixed window length, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_020.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_020.png</span> . Then, at each point <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_021.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_021.png</span> , the MOSUM is computed as follows:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_022.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_022.png</span>

This quantity is simply a local average of scaled residuals, so that values are comparable across window sizes. When there is no change, positive and negative residuals cancel out, and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_023.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_023.png</span> stays close to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_024.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_024.png</span> .

When a change is detected, residuals in the window tend to have the same sign, causing <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_025.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_025.png</span> to grow in magnitude. This makes the MOSUM transform from a subtle deviation to a visible signal. To determine whether the observed MOSUM values are unusually large, they are compared to a reference boundary that represents what we would expect under normal random variation. The MOSUM test statistic is defined as follows:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_026.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_026.png</span>

If <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_027.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_027.png</span> remains below the boundary, the model is considered stable. If <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_028.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_028.png</span> exceeds the boundary, BFAST concludes that a structural change has occurred.

The boundary represents the range of values we would expect the MOSUM to take if the underlying process has not changed. The time at which the boundary is crossed provides an estimate of the location of the break. ##  16.2.1 BFAST implementation on non-seasonal data 

In this example, we use the Nile data from the dataset package. The data is in ` `` ts `` ` format and contains a record of historical annual flow from the River Nile between 1871 and 1970. You can apply the BFAST method using the ` `` bfast() `` ` function from the ` `` bfast `` ` package. The function expects input data as ` `` ts `` ` format.

To enforce a non-seasonal additive decomposition on an annual series, the seasonality option is disabled by setting ` `` season = "none" `` ` . We use a minimum segment length of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_029.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_029.png</span> of the total observations (pass in the argument h in the ` `` b `` ` ` `` f `` ` ` `` ast() `` ` function). There is no strict guidance for choosing the minimum segment length; however, it should not be too small. Apply domain knowledge and context of the observed time series to set a suitable number in your application. We set ` `` max.iter = 10 `` ` , which controls the maximum number of iterations allowed for estimation of changepoints in the trend component (and seasonal, where applicable). ##  16.2.2 BFAST implementation on a seasonal series 

The well-known AirPassengers series that contains monthly totals of international airline passengers from <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_037.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_037.png</span> to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_038.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_038.png</span> , is used to show how you can apply BFAST to detect changepoints in a seasonal series.

Since BFAST decomposes a time series assuming an additive model, a logarithmic transformation is required to ensure the model’s appropriateness for this data. For seasonal series, it is better to use a full seasonal period as the minimum segment size. It is computed as the ratio of the frequency in the dataset to the total observations. In this example, it will cover about <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_039.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_039.png</span> ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_040.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_040.png</span> ) of the total observations.

Let’s look at some advantages and limitations of the BFAST method.

The following are advantages:

-  Computationally efficient, making it suitable for long time series
-  Intuitive and relatively easy to implement
-  Ability to detect multiple changepoints
-  Handles both trend and seasonal changes in an integrated framework
-  Well-established theoretical properties

The following are limitations:

-  The method may not find the global optimum solution
-  Performance degrades when changepoints are close to each other
-  The method may struggle with heteroscedastic data (changing variance)
-  It has a tendency to overestimate the number of changepoints with noisy data
-  It requires specification of the minimum segment size parameter

In the next section, we discuss a method that guarantees finding an optimum solution via optimization.

#  16.3 Pruned Exact Linear Time method 

The **Pruned Exact Linear Time** ( **PELT** ) algorithm was introduced by Killick et al. (2012). The method guarantees finding the changepoint in a time series, if it exists, via a global search. This method treats changepoint detection as an optimization problem that finds the segmentation by minimizing a cost function. To avoid overfitting during optimization, the cost function penalizes the model for the number of changepoints.

-  Residual sum of squares for mean shifts
-  Likelihood-based costs for variance changes

The PELT process starts with an initialization step: identify plausible changepoints in an iterative recursion fashion in the second step, then discard non-optimal changepoints in the pruning step, and finally, update candidate changepoints or terminate when the stopping criteria are met. The key component of the PELT algorithm is the pruning step. In this step, the non-optimal changepoints are discarded, which in turn reduces computational complexity.

##  16.3.2 PELT implementation on a seasonal series 

PELT works on the fundamental assumption that data between changepoints originates from a stationary distribution; hence, before applying PELT to identify changepoints, an observed series needs to be stationary.       season(window = "periodic"))) |>
```
  components()

ap_remainder_kpss <- ap_decomp |> 
  features(remainder, feasts::unitroot_kpss)

ap_remainder_kpss
```

Let’s summarize the advantages and limitations of PELT method.

The following are advantages:

-  It is guaranteed to find the optimal segmentation under the specified cost function
-  It has a linear computational complexity for many common cost functions
-  It has a flexible framework that can detect changes in mean and variance
-  It has a robust theoretical foundation with well-defined statistical properties
-  It automatically determines the optimal number of changepoints

The following are limitations:

-  It requires specification of a penalty parameter (though information criteria, such as the BIC)
-  Its model assumptions must be carefully considered for the chosen cost function
-  It is sensitive to model misspecification
-  It may struggle with strongly autocorrelated time series

So far, we have covered two frequentist-based methods, BFAST and PELT, that can be applied to both non-seasonal and seasonal series. However, these decomposition-based methods have notable limitations when non-stationarity arises from the complex interaction of trend and seasonality. For instance, seasonal data with complex trends and seasonality might require appropriate differencing to obtain a stationary remainder. Like BFAST, the PELT method is also sensitive to the specification of the minimum segment length ( ` `` minseglen `` ` ). If we had used ` `` minseglen = 2 `` ` in this example, there would have been an additional changepoint detected. This limitation necessitates the exploration of an alternative approach. A Bayesian-based changepoint detection method is discussed in the following section.

#  16.4 Bayesian changepoint detection 

The Bayesian approach of changepoint detection begins with specifying prior beliefs about the number and locations of changepoints. This belief is updated using observed data. This process results in the posterior distribution, which quantifies uncertainty about the changepoint configuration.

Unlike frequentist methods, which typically produce point estimates, Bayesian methods provide full posterior distributions, enabling robust uncertainty quantification for all parameters.

Under the Bayesian framework, we want to identify points where the underlying data-generating process changes in an observed time series, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_079.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_079.png</span> . Let, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_080.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_080.png</span> represent a vector of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_081.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_081.png</span> changepoint locations, where <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_082.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_082.png</span> .

This approach requires specification for the following parameters to initiate the process:

-  A prior distribution, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_083.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_083.png</span> , for the number of changepoints
-  A prior distribution, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_084.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_084.png</span> , for the locations of changepoints given their number
-  A likelihood model, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_085.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_085.png</span> , describing the data-generating process, with parameters <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_086.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_086.png</span>
-  A prior distribution, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_087.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_087.png</span> , for the model parameters

Bayesian inference then proceeds by computing the posterior distribution:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_088.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_088.png</span>

This posterior encapsulates all available information about the changepoint configuration and model parameters given the observed data.

In Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution can be computed using various methods. When the analytical solution of the posterior distribution is non-trivial, the inference process relies on a numerical parameter estimation algorithm known as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. The algorithm comprises the following steps:

1.   Initialize the chain by starting with an initial guess of the parameters <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_089.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_089.png</span>

2.   Iterate through:

    -  Propose a new parameter value, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_090.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_090.png</span>
    -  Taking <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_091.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_091.png</span> as a proposal distribution, compute acceptance probability <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_092.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_092.png</span>

    <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_093.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_093.png</span>

    -  Accept or reject <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_094.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_094.png</span> by comparing <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_095.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_095.png</span> with a random number, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_096.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_096.png</span> , drawn from a uniform distribution. If <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_097.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_097.png</span> , set <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_098.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_098.png</span> to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_099.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_099.png</span> .

3.   Repeat for many iterations to build a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior.

4.   Estimate parameters by using the chain samples (after discarding burn-in) to estimate quantities, such as the posterior mean, variance, or credible intervals.

Further details on the MCMC can be found in standard textbooks on Bayesian inference.

**Bayesian Estimation of Abrupt changes, Seasonal variation, and Trend** ( **BEAST** ) is a Bayesian method-based changepoint detection framework that handles complex time series with multiple components. The method is implemented in the ` `` Rbeast `` ` package (Zhao et al., 2019). Like BFAST, BEAST uses additive decomposition of time series where a component can undergo structural changes independently, such as the following:

-  The trend component, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_100.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_100.png</span> , is modeled as a piece-wise linear function with a possible changepoint in both level and slope
-  The seasonal component, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_101.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_101.png</span> , is modeled as a periodic function that can change in amplitude and phase
-  The noise component, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_16_102.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_16_102.png</span> , is typically assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution with potentially changing variance

The BEAST model allows for an unknown number of changepoints in each component, providing a flexible framework for time-series decomposition and changepoint detection.

The primary steps in the BEAST algorithm are as follows:

1.   Initialization: Set initial values for the number and locations of changepoints in trend and seasonal components
2.   MCMC sampling: Iteratively update the model configuration through a series of move types. For example, add a new changepoint or remove an existing changepoint.
3.   Posterior estimation: Approximate the posterior distributions for changepoint number, locations, and model parameters using the MCMC samples after a burn-in period.

The algorithm automatically balances model complexity and data fit through the Bayesian framework, avoiding the need for explicit penalty terms or cross-validation procedures for controlling overfitting.

Let’s see the implementation of the BEAST method in the following sub-sections.

The advantages and limitations of the BEAST algorithm are presented next.

The following are the advantages:

-  It provides a full posterior distribution over changepoint locations, quantifying uncertainty
-  It naturally incorporates prior knowledge about changepoint frequency or locations

-  It requires specification of prior distributions
-  The model complexity increases rapidly for multiple changepoint types
-  Its interpretation can be more challenging than simpler methods
-  Its implementation may require specialized knowledge of Bayesian statistics

#  16.5 Method selection guidelines 

In real-life applications, when choosing between changepoint detection methods, several factors should be considered beyond their advantages and limitations. These have been summarized and grouped under three major categories to guide you based on your requirements.

The first relates to data characteristics:

-  Data size: For very large datasets, binary segmentation may be preferred due to its computational efficiency
-  Noise level: Bayesian methods often perform better with noisy data due to their probabilistic nature
-  Changepoint frequency: PELT is well-suited for detecting numerous changepoints
-  Changepoint magnitude: Smaller shifts may be better detected by PELT or Bayesian approaches

The following are analysis objectives:

-  Computational efficiency: Binary segmentation is fastest, followed by PELT and then Bayesian methods
-  Theoretical guarantees: PELT provides optimality guarantees under its cost model
-  Uncertainty quantification: The Bayesian method excels when uncertainty estimates are needed
-  Online detection: Modified Bayesian approaches are particularly suitable for detecting changepoints in streaming data

The final grouping is implementation complexity:

-  Technical expertise required: Binary segmentation is simplest, while Bayesian methods typically require advanced statistical and computing skills
-  Parameter tuning: The Bayesian method typically requires a wider range of parameter tuning than PELT and BFAST.

#  Summary 

Changepoint detection represents a critical component of time-series analysis, enabling you to identify structural breaks that may significantly impact subsequent analyses and forecasts. Three methods, BFAST, PELT, and BEAST, were explored in this chapter, along with their unique strengths and limitations.

Binary segmentation (BFAST) provides an efficient and intuitive approach suitable for many applications, particularly when computational resources are limited or when dealing with a very long time series. PELT offers optimal segmentation guarantees while maintaining reasonable computational efficiency, making it an excellent choice for applications requiring high precision. Bayesian changepoint detection excels in quantifying uncertainty and incorporating prior knowledge, providing richer insights at the cost of increased computational complexity.

When applying these methods in practice, careful consideration should be given to the specific characteristics of a dataset, the analytics objectives, and the available computational resources. Often, employing multiple approaches and comparing their results can provide more robust insights than relying on a single method.

As time-series data continues to grow in volume and importance across domains, changepoint detection will remain an essential tool for understanding structural shifts, improving forecasting accuracy, and gaining insights into the underlying dynamics of complex systems.

For time series with pronounced trend and seasonality components, domain expertise becomes essential for the meaningful interpretation of detected changepoints. Blind application of methods without contextual understanding may produce ambiguous or misleading results when confronted with complex temporal patterns.
