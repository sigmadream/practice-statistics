#  14.1 The dynamics of multiple interconnected time series 

When analyzing multiple time series, it is crucial to understand the nature of the relationships between them. These relationships can be classified as follows:

-  Direction: either unidirectional or bidirectional
-  Structure: either hierarchical or grouped time series

A unidirectional relationship occurs when future values of one time series depend on its own past values, and potentially on the past values of other, independent (exogenous) variables. Crucially, the exogenous variables are not influenced by the time series of primary interest. For example, consider forecasting a supermarket’s total sales. These sales may depend on the company’s marketing expenditure for various product categories. The marketing expenditure acts as an exogenous regressor because the sales volume does not typically influence the planned marketing budget in the same immediate time frame. Models such as ARIMA with exogenous regressors, as discussed in *Section 12.6* , can effectively capture and model this type of one-way dependency.

In contrast, bidirectional relationships occur when time series mutually influence one another, creating a feedback loop. This introduces a “lag-lead effect” where changes in one series are followed by changes in the other, and vice versa. In economic scenarios, complementary or substitute goods within a sector rarely have a one-way relationship. For example, in many major economies, natural gas demand and electricity demand are bidirectionally linked due to the complementary effect. An increase in the demand for electricity necessitates greater use of natural gas for generation, which in turn increases the demand for natural gas as a commodity, influencing its price and supply dynamics. Both variables influence each other.

While less common in typical business forecasting, bidirectional modeling is a crucial part of government policy analysis for understanding complex macroeconomic and financial systems with multiple time series with bidirectional relationships. For example, agencies such as the Treasury and Reserve Bank of Australia studied interconnected time series such as **Gross Domestic Product** ( **GDP** ), interest rates, inflation, and unemployment. These exhibit both short- and long-term lag-lead relationships. Raising interest rates is a policy decision designed to curb inflation, but it also impacts business growth, employment levels, and GDP. Analyzing these variables in isolation offers limited insights, underscoring the need for bidirectional approaches to assess comprehensive policy impacts.

Putting uni/bidirectional modeling aside, a distinct class of use cases has garnered significant attention in modern business applications, driven by advancements in large-scale data collection capabilities. For instance, large supermarkets are increasingly interested in understanding demand across hundreds or thousands of product categories. These categories often exhibit short-term, point-in-time relationships (not lag-lead) due to factors, such as the substitution effect, complementing effect, and price elasticity.

Take, for example, the consumption of berries in a geographically diverse country, such as Australia. Seasonal demand can vary significantly between states, such as Queensland and Victoria, due to differences in climate. Consumers’ taste varies in tropical versus subtropical regions, influencing short-term demand for berries. While these product categories may not exhibit long-term equilibrium relationships, their short-term dynamics are of critical importance for inventory management and planning. This area of forecasting has seen substantial academic development over the past two decades.

For modeling such relationships, specialized models are required. We will discuss the Vector Autoregression model in the following section.

#  14.2 Vector Autoregression model 

Vector Autoregression (VAR) is a statistical model used to capture the relationship between multiple time series variables. Unlike univariate models, VAR treats all variables as endogenous or dependent, allowing each variable to depend not only on its own past values but also on the past values of other variables. This makes it ideal for analyzing systems where variables are interrelated, such as economic and financial indicators. VAR models are widely used in econometrics to assess dynamic impacts and forecast multiple variables simultaneously. This class of model was advocated in the macroeconomic literature through seminal works by Sims (1980) and Sims (1987).

##  14.2.2 Inspect time series for stationarity 

To illustrate VAR modeling, we will utilize GDP data for Australia and the United States covering the period from 1960 to 2022. This dataset has been obtained from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (WDI), and is accessible via the ` `` WDI `` ` package in R.

Download GDP for Australia and USA between 1960 and 2022 and make it a ` `` tsibble `` ` object using the following code:

```
gdp_data <- WDI(
  indicator = 'NY.GDP.PCAP.KD',
  country = c('AU', 'US'),
  start = "1960", end = "2022" ) |>
  tibble() |>
  select(country, year, GDP = 'NY.GDP.PCAP.KD')
```

To facilitate ease of analysis, the data has been pre-processed and prepared specifically for modeling. The GDP figures for 2022 have been used as the baseline, allowing for a clearer interpretation of the estimated coefficients across the time series for both countries. Each series is normalized by its value in 2022. This data can be interpreted as an index with a 2022 baseline. The operation is performed in the following code:

```
GDP_norm <- gdp_data |>
  left_join(
    gdp_data |>
      filter(year == max(year)) |>
      rename(gdp2022 = GDP) |>
      select(-year)
  ) |>
  mutate(GDP_index = (GDP/gdp2022)*100) |>
  select(-gdp2022) |>
  as_tsibble(key = country, index = year)
```

##  14.2.3 Identifying VAR model orders 

A crucial step in fitting a VAR model is determining the appropriate order of the lag dependencies between the observed time series. One approach is to approximate the lag structure through visual inspection, which can provide initial insights into the bidirectional relationships between variables over time. A more systematic method is to utilize a **Cross-Correlation Function** ( **CCF** ) plot. You have used ACF plots to detect inter-dependency on an univariate series. A CCF plot provides similar information for two-time series by displaying both lag and lead correlations between them. This tool helps to identify significant temporal relationships by highlighting how one time series correlates with past or future values of another, thereby aiding in the selection of the optimal lag order for the VAR model.

A CCF plot between the GDP growth series of Australia and the USA is obtained by first computing the cross-correlation between the two series using the ` `` CCF() `` ` function from the ` `` feasts `` ` package, and plotting them. You can perform the operations using the following code:

```
ccf_plt_growth <- GDP_change |>
  select(GDP_growth) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = country, values_from = GDP_growth) |>
  CCF() |>
  autoplot() +
  labs(y = "Correlation", x = "Lag",
       title = "Cross correlation plot") +
  theme_bw()

ccf_plt_growth
```

##  14.2.4 Interpretation of VAR model coefficients 

The estimated parameters of a VAR model provide valuable insights into the underlying bidirectional relationships within the system. By examining these parameters, particularly the slope coefficients associated with lagged variables, we can gain a deeper understanding of the temporal dependencies in the dynamic system. The direction of the slope indicates whether the effect is positive or negative. This analysis is a crucial starting point for determining whether the influence of a variable operates with a lag or leads to changes in other system components over time.

In the example of the VAR model of GDP using two countries, Australia and the United States, we observed that Australia reveals a strong positive relation between the prior year’s GDP growth in the United States and the current year’s GDP growth in Australia (estimated coefficient = <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_14_098.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_14_098.png</span> ). This suggests that economic expansion in the USA, is likely to boost Australia’s economic performance in the subsequent year. Conversely, the impact of Australia’s prior year GDP growth on USA GDP growth is smaller and negative (estimated coefficient = <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_14_099.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_14_099.png</span> ), indicating a more limited influence in the opposite direction.

When analyzing this relationship using a VAR model with only two time series and a lag of one period, VAR( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_14_100.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_14_100.png</span> ), the dynamics between the two economies are relatively straightforward to conceptualize. However, when the model is extended to a VAR( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_14_101.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_14_101.png</span> ) framework involving <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_14_102.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_14_102.png</span> time series, where each series potentially affects all others in the system, the interpretation of estimated coefficients becomes increasingly complex. In such scenarios, disentangling the influence of one time series on another poses a significant challenge. This leads to a classic chicken-and-egg dilemma: which series is the true driver of the other’s behavior?

This problem is further compounded when the original scales of the time series differ, as is the case in our analysis. The GDP of the United States is several times larger than that of Australia, which can complicate both the analysis and interpretation of cross-country economic influences within a multivariate model. However, this problem is mitigated in the analysis as the GDP data was normalized before fitting a model. In general, for VAR, understanding the direction of causality and the relative impacts between these economies thus requires more sophisticated methods beyond simple coefficient analysis as we will see in the following sub-section.

##  14.2.6 Impulse Response Analysis 

**Impulse response analysis** ( **IRA** ) is a valuable technique employed to assess the impact of a shock to one time series on the entire system over a specified time horizon. In our analysis, we established that growth in the GDP of the United States has a positive effect on the GDP growth of Australia. A pertinent question arises: What would be the repercussions for Australia’s GDP growth if there were a shock in the USA GDP growth, particularly in the context of an event such as the COVID-19 pandemic? In a business domain, an application of IRA is analyzing how a sudden marketing campaign (that is, impulse) affects web traffic, and subsequently sales and server load (the responses).

To explore the impact of impulses, we utilize the **impulse response function** ( **IRF** ), which allows us to quantify the dynamic responses in one source while the shocks originate from another source of information.

For illustration, we will continue to use the GDP data of Australia and the USA, and measure the response in the Australian economy to shocks originating from the USA economy. We’ll use the ` `` irf() `` ` function from the ` `` vars `` ` package to perform an IRF on the GDP data. We will investigate the impact of the initial shock for 10 steps into the future, passed in the ` `` n.ahead `` ` argument to the function. The following code snippet computes two IRF models, one for each country:

```
# IRF: US -> Australia
irf_fit_us_2_oz <- vars::irf(
  varfit,
  impulse = "United.States",
  response = "Australia",
  n.ahead = 10,
  ortho = TRUE
)

# IRF: Australia -> US
irf_fit_oz_2_us <- vars::irf(
  varfit,
  impulse = "Australia",
  response =  "United.States",
  n.ahead = 10,
  ortho = TRUE
)
```

Among many elements returned by the ` `` irf() `` ` function, we are interested in the following:

-  **Impulse response coefficients** : Represents the dynamic reaction of a variable to a sudden, isolated shock in another variable over time
-  **Confidence intervals** : Lower and upper bounds of the coefficients that provide information on their statistical significance

It is important to note that VAR is well-suited for short- to medium-term forecasting in systems where the dynamic relationships between the variables remain stable.

You should be aware of some important remarks when using the VAR model in analysis, such as the following:

-  **Non-stationary data** : If your time series are non-stationary and cannot be made stationary through differencing, then a VAR model is inappropriate. In such cases, you might consider a **Vector Error Correction Model** ( **VECM** ) if the series are cointegrated.
-  **Many time series with short history** : If you have too many time series or very few data points, VAR can become overly complex. This leads to overfitting and poor out-of-sample performance.
-  **Highly correlated time series** : If your time series variables are highly collinear, VAR can produce unstable estimates. In such cases, regularization techniques, such as Ridge or Lasso, or dimensionality reduction methods, such as PCA, might be more appropriate.
-  **Nonlinear relationships** : VAR assumes linear relationships between the time series. If you suspect nonlinear interactions, other models, such as **Vector Autoregressive Moving Average** ( **VARMA** ), nonlinear autoregressive models, or machine learning techniques may be more suitable.
-  **Long-term forecasting** : VAR typically works best for short-term forecasting. Over longer periods, the relationships between time series might drift, making VAR less reliable.

It is also worth noting that the VAR model does not assume any underlying theory in economics. Despite these facts, VAR has proven useful for forecasting a collection of related variables where no explicit interpretation is required.

When forecasting is required for multiple time series that are non-stationary and have complex correlation patterns, but do not necessarily exhibit bidirectional relationship, then we need more flexible approaches to handle such data structure. Hierarchical and grouped time series are ideal options for such a scenario, which we discuss in the following section.

#  14.3 Hierarchical and grouped time series 

Many business applications face the challenge of managing multiple time series that exhibit a hierarchical structure. This hierarchy implies that time series data is aggregated across multiple categorical levels. For instance, the time series representing the total number of tourists visiting a country can be disaggregated into time series at the state level and further broken down by the purpose of travel (e.g., business, leisure). Each of these categories can then be further subdivided into more granular segments, such as adult versus child travelers.

Forecasting at different levels within this hierarchy is often required to support distinct business decisions tailored to various stakeholders. For example, national-level tourism forecasting is crucial for broad economic and policy planning, providing insights into overall trends. At the state level, more specific forecasting may be required to address localized needs. A state that attracts many business travelers may require a different set of services and infrastructure compared to a state that caters primarily to family vacations, where a higher proportion of children is expected. The ability to forecast demand at each hierarchical level is critical for ensuring that the appropriate resources and services are allocated effectively, reflecting the unique requirements of each segment of the tourism market.

A grouped time series is a variation of hierarchical time series in which the aggregation structure arises from the crossing of categories rather than from a strict nesting hierarchy. In hierarchical time series, the relationships between series are defined by parent-child structures, where higher-level aggregates roll up from the lower-level series. However, in grouped time series, categories of interest intersect to create groupings that are not naturally nested.

For example, consider a large retail chain that sells various product categories such as clothing, electronics, and kitchenware. These categories could be further segmented by region, such as metro versus non-metro areas. In this context, the demand for products can be grouped in multiple ways. One approach could be to first group by product category (for example, electronics) and then by region (such as, metro). Alternatively, the demand could be first grouped by region, and then by product category. In a grouped time series, these different groupings exist side by side, and there is no inherent parent-child relationship, unlike in a hierarchical structure.

A fundamental characteristic of both hierarchical and grouped time series is that they must remain aggregate consistent. This means that the data from the lower, more granular levels must sum up correctly to the higher, aggregated levels. Ensuring this consistency is essential for accurate forecasting and planning at multiple levels.

To forecast these time series, specialized hierarchical forecasting methods are employed. These methods are designed to handle both hierarchical and grouped structures, maintaining aggregate consistency across all levels. In this book, for the sake of simplicity, we will refer to the forecasting of both hierarchical and grouped time series as hierarchical forecasting. This terminology simplifies the discussion, but the methods applied are equally valid for grouped time series.

##  14.3.1 Traditional hierarchical forecasting: top-down, bottom-up, and middle-out approaches 

Hierarchical forecasting involves specialized methods for forecasting that can be used for both hierarchical and grouped time series. These methods have matured over time in the inventory planning and demand forecasting literature (Dangerfield et al., 1992) and (Fliedner, 2001). Some traditional and widely used methods are top-down, bottom-up, and middle-out approaches. The top-down approach generates an aggregate forecast and distributes it downward using historical or predicted proportions. The bottom-up approach aggregates granular-level forecasts into higher-level totals, while the middle-out method combines both by forecasting at an intermediate level, then reconciles upward via aggregation and downward via disaggregation. Each method has its own use cases, advantages, and disadvantages.

In hierarchical forecasting, the process starts by fitting a univariate time series forecast at the level defined by the approach used for reconciliation. That means, for the top-down approach, the base forecasting model will be fitted on the series at the highest level. In the bottom-up approach, the univariate forecasting models are fitted on each individual series at the bottom most-level. Irrespective of which level’s data the models are applied to, you can choose the “base” univariate forecasting model from any available model classes, such as automated ARIMA, ETS (Hyndman et al., 2008), or effort-heavy DL or ML forecasting models. In further discussions, we use an ETS model as the “base” model across all approaches so that we can compare the forecasting performance across the reconciliation techniques.

For the same reason, we will use a single dataset, the ` `` aus_retail `` ` data from the ` `` tsibbledata `` ` package, in all examples in this section. The dataset has monthly retail turnover (millions of AUD) from the main industries in Australia. The retail turnover from January 2008 to December 2018 is used for the selected industries: (i) clothing retailing, (ii) food retailing, and (iii) other recreational goods retailing, for the states of Queensland and Victoria. We made this choice deliberately to mimic real business scenarios, for example, the difference in seasonality patterns in clothing retail turnover between the two states due to the fact that Queensland enjoys a tropical (warmer) climate compared to the temperate, cooler climate with a wet winter in Victoria. Hence, the example and discussion will equip you well, so that you will be able to apply these techniques in a business context.

###  14.3.1.1 Top-down approach 

In the top-down approach, the forecasting process begins by generating a forecast at the highest, most aggregated level of the hierarchy. The “base” model should be selected according to the characteristics of the overall, top-level aggregated time series. Once an optimal base model is fitted and total forecasts for the top-level series are obtained, then it is disaggregated down to the lower levels of the hierarchy.

Several methods can be used to perform this disaggregation; two of the most applied methods are as follows:

-  **Historical proportion** : In this method, the top-level forecast is allocated to the lower-level series based on historical proportions (Gross et al., 1990). This means that each lower-level series receives a share of the total forecast corresponding to its historical contribution to the top-level series. The assumption is that the relationships between the levels of the hierarchy remain stable over time, with each lower-level series maintaining a consistent proportion of the total.
-  **Forecast proportion** : This method disaggregates the top-level forecast using base forecasts generated for the lower-level series or the proportion series (Athanasopoulos et al., 2009). Here, base forecasts (which can also be derived from univariate forecasting models) are used to determine how the top-level forecast should be split among the lower levels. Unlike the historical proportion method, this approach allows for more flexibility by adjusting for expected changes in the lower-level series. The proportions can vary over time, reflecting dynamic relationships between the levels. Although conceptually better, this is a very effort demanding approach.

The top-down approach is straightforward and simple to implement, primarily because it only requires a single base model for the aggregate series. This simplicity makes it computationally efficient, especially for large hierarchies, as there is no need to develop separate models for each lower-level series. The reduced number of forecasts required also makes this approach more practical when dealing with limited data or computational resources.

However, the top-down approach has several limitations; the most alarming one is that it often fails to account for variability or distinct patterns that may exist at the lower levels of the hierarchy. Because the forecasts for the lower-level series are derived entirely from the top-level forecast, important information such as localized trends, seasonality, or irregular fluctuations at the disaggregated levels may be overlooked. As a result, the method can lead to inaccurate forecasts for individual lower-level series, particularly when there are significant differences in behavior between series at different levels of the hierarchy.

Furthermore, the dependence on the accuracy of the top-level forecast is a potential drawback. If the top-level forecast is inaccurate, it can propagate these errors down the hierarchy, negatively impacting the forecasts at all subsequent levels. Additionally, the method may not be well-suited for capturing demand fluctuations or evolving trends at lower levels, which can result in suboptimal forecasting performance for finer granularities, particularly in dynamic environments where relationships between levels are changing over time.

The top-down forecast using historical proportions can be derived by fitting a suitable univariate model to the aggregated turnover. We use automated ETS models for this purpose. After the top-level base forecast is generated, it is distributed to successive lower levels using historical proportions using the ` `` average_proportions `` ` method. From the ` `` fabletools `` ` package, the ` `` reconcile() `` ` function in combination with the ` `` top_down() `` ` function achieves that.

First, in the training, we fit automated ETS models for each series in the hierarchy for comparison purposes. Then we just take the fitted ETS model of the top-level aggregated series, and the forecasts from that model will be distributed to all levels according to the average proportions. This process avoids further re-fitting the model. Once forecast redistribution is performed for the hierarchy, then use the model object to generate forecasts for the next <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_14_166.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_14_166.png</span> years. ###  14.3.1.2 Bottom-up approach 

The bottom-up approach to hierarchical forecasting begins by fitting individual forecasting models to every series at the bottom-level of the hierarchy. Depending on the data characteristics, a user can choose from a wide variety of forecasting model ranges, from simple naive to DL/ML. Once forecasts for all the bottom-level series have been generated, they are aggregated upwards by summing the individual forecasts to produce forecasts for the next level of the hierarchy, eventually reaching the top level.

One of the key advantages of this approach is that it can capture patterns, trends, and unique features present in individual time series at the detailed level of the hierarchy. This approach provides more flexibility to handle series-specific events, such as localized seasonality or irregular fluctuations. This can result in improved accuracy at the bottom levels of the hierarchy, since each series is modeled independently. The bottom-up approach is particularly beneficial when there is significant heterogeneity across the bottom-level series, allowing the forecasts to account for varying demand behaviors, trends, and seasonal effects in different segments of the hierarchy.

The bottom-up approach performs well at the disaggregated level. However, aggregating forecasts from the bottom level to a higher level can lead to an accumulation of errors. Even small errors in individual bottom-level forecasts can compound when aggregated, leading to higher variance and potentially less accurate forecasts at the upper levels of the hierarchy. This approach commonly results in under-forecasting of the top-level series, as noise or bias in individual forecasts may not balance out when aggregated.

The bottom-up approach requires more computational power compared to the top-down approach involving historical proportions. Each bottom-level series needs to be forecast separately, which means that for large hierarchies, hundreds or even thousands of individual forecasts might be needed. This can be computationally expensive in terms of resources, especially when large-scale models are applied. Moreover, it requires sufficient and reliable data for each bottom-level series. In many real-world applications, data at the bottom level may be sparse or noisy, which can make univariate forecasting more difficult.

###  14.3.1.3 Middle-out approach 

The middle-out approach to hierarchical forecasting is a hybrid method that combines elements of both the top-down and bottom-up approaches. Instead of focusing on the highest (top-level) or the most disaggregated (bottom-level) series, forecasts are generated at an intermediate (middle) level of the hierarchy. Once forecasts are produced for the middle level, the approach then diverges into two paths:

-  **For levels below the middle** : The top-down approach is applied to distribute forecasts from the middle level to the lower levels. This allows forecasts for the more detailed series to be derived from the intermediate-level forecasts.
-  **For levels above the middle** : The bottom-up approach is used, where forecasts for higher levels are the aggregation of middle-level forecasts upwards.

The forecasting process begins by selecting a middle level in the hierarchy. This level should strike a balance between the level of detail needed and the computational resources available. Univariate time series forecasting models, such as ARIMA or ETS, are applied to each series at the middle level. These models are chosen based on the characteristics of the intermediate-level time series.

By focusing on an intermediate level, the middle-out approach balances the granularity of the bottom-up approach (which captures detailed patterns at lower levels) with the computational efficiency of the top-down approach (which requires fewer forecasts). This makes it a good compromise between the two methods. Nevertheless, in the middle out approach the number of forecasts is reduced compared to the bottom-up approach, as the method does not require individual forecasts for every bottom-level series. At the same time, it retains more detail than the top-down approach, which might overlook key patterns in the data at lower levels.

One of the primary challenges of the middle-out approach is determining the optimal middle level to generate forecasts. If the chosen middle level is not optimal, the benefits of the approach may be diminished.

A suboptimal choice could result in forecasts that are either too aggregated or too detailed, leading to less accurate results. Since different levels of the hierarchy are forecast using different methods (top-down for lower levels and bottom-up for higher levels), there is a risk of inconsistencies across levels if the two methods do not align well. Ensuring consistency across the hierarchy requires careful handling of the transition between the top-down and bottom-up components of the method.

##  14.3.2 Optimal reconciliation forecast for hierarchical time series using trace minimization 

The optimal reconciliation approach using trace minimization reconciles individual forecasts from different levels of the hierarchy using some automated modeling (for example, models discussed in Hyndman et al. (2008)), ensuring that these forecasts sum up to match aggregated totals, minimizing errors across all levels. This is achieved by solving an optimization problem that minimizes the forecast error covariance matrix.

```
hfm_mint_fit <- retail_sel |>
  filter(month <= yearmonth("2016 Dec") ) |>
  model(base = ETS(turnover)) |>
  reconcile(ols = min_trace(base, method = "ols"),
            mint_cov = min_trace(base, method = "mint_cov"))

hfm_mint_fcst <- hfm_mint_fit |>
  forecast(h = "2 years")
```

##  14.3.3 Forecast accuracy evaluation 

In a hierarchical structure, visual inspection is challenging to distinguish the performance of alternative forecasting methods as the scale of the data is different in different panels and therefore, accuracy at a given series or level or across the hierarchy is not easy to estimate. An objective approach is to calculate accuracy of alternative forecasts in the test data.

To facilitate the forecasting performance comparison for all the methods, we refit them and organized in one object, generate forecasts and compute accuracy metrics as follows:

```
fit_hfm <- retail_sel_train |>
  model(base = ETS(turnover)) |>
  reconcile(
    td_hp =  top_down(base, method = c("average_proportions")),
    bu = bottom_up(base),
    mo = middle_out(base, 1),
    ols = min_trace(base, method = "ols"),
    mint_cov = min_trace(base, method = "mint_cov")
  )

acc_hfm <- fit_hfm |>
  forecast(h = "2 years") |>
  accuracy(data = retail_sel |>
             filter(month >= yearmonth("2012 Jan")))
```

#  14.4 Machine learning for multiple time series forecasting 

In *<a href="Chapter_13.xhtml#h1_275" class="chapref"> Chapter 13 </a>* , you learned how to use machine learning for univariate time series forecasting and implemented it using the ` `` forecastML `` ` R package. This method was applied to the ` `` AirPassengers `` ` time series, which exhibits strong trends and seasonality, similar to retail turnover. Consequently, ML can also be utilized for forecasting multiple time series, regardless of whether individual series are stationary. The challenge of accounting for potential complex correlations between multiple time series can be addressed by empirically learning from the data. This means that when modeling one series using a machine learning model, the remaining series can be used as features to account for the impact of correlations.

As discussed in *Subsection 13.3.1* , you can include other time series as dynamic features within the ` `` forecastML `` ` workflow. You can also incorporate static features relevant to your models. With properly curated features, machine learning-based methods can produce highly accurate point forecasts, as evidenced by results from recent forecasting competitions. Despite their high accuracy, in real-life applications, you may need to weigh the trade-off between speed and accuracy. Many modern machine learning algorithms are fast when run at scale, but additional static and dynamic feature curation can be time-consuming and should be factored into the project's timeline.

Nevertheless, machine learning-based forecasts do not reconcile in the same way as hierarchical forecasting methods. If you require a single forecasting framework that serves multiple stakeholders at different levels of the hierarchy and requires aggregation consistency in reporting, the machine learning-based approach might not be the optimal choice, despite its favorable accuracy measures.
