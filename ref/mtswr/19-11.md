#  Exponential Smoothing 

Exponential smoothing is a simple, yet powerful time-series forecasting technique. The method was introduced in the late 1950s when computers were in their infancy. Still, this family of models effectively solves real-life forecasting problems, and is widely used in business decision-making in sales, inventory management, finance and so on.

In exponential smoothing, recent observations are more important to forecasts compared to values in the distant past. The weights attached to observations decay exponentially as the data points move further from the present, hence the name exponential smoothing. This model leverages trend and seasonality components of a time series along with various specifications for how the error components are incorporated.

In this chapter, we will first explore the fundamental theory behind exponential smoothing methods. It is important to gain a deep understanding of how the exponential smoothing model framework has evolved over time, so that you can choose the optimal model for your forecasting problem. There are plenty of coding examples and discussions presented in this chapter for you to learn from, which will allow you to generate accurate, actionable forecasts for decision-making.

#  11.1 Forecasting methods versus models 

Initially, if exponential smoothing was used for forecasting, they were known as *methods* because the underlying statistical properties were not explicitly modeled. In 2002, the statistical models underlying each exponential method were introduced in the literature, known as *state space exponential smoothing models* .

The terms *forecasting methods* and *forecasting models* are often used interchangeably. The difference lies in the formulation of equations and the levels of detail.

Forecasting methods are simple, and involve minimal mathematical representation and computation, such as an average method or naïve method. On the other hand, forecasting models incorporate elaborate mathematical or statistical frameworks, which range from simple to complex computation.

Time series data is a combination of three main components: trend (or trend-cycle), seasonality and random fluctuations (errors). They could be combined in several ways in an observed series:

-  Additive: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_001.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_001.png</span> ,
-  Multiplicative: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_002.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_002.png</span> ,
-  Additive trend and seasonality with multiplicative error: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_003.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_003.png</span> .

When time series characteristics were discussed in *<a href="Chapter_4.xhtml#h1_113" class="chapref"> Chapter 4 </a>* , we used <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_004.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_004.png</span> to represent the random variations instead of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_005.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_005.png</span> as shown here. This convention is adopted from technical literature to distinguish decomposition of historical components from the development of active forecasting models. Theoretically, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_006.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_006.png</span> (Remainder) is preferred in decomposition contexts, where the random variation is treated as an identifiable component that can be extracted from an observed series using proper techniques, such as STL decomposition. In contrast, in forecasting and regression modeling, the error term <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_007.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_007.png</span> (typically denoted as <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_008.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_008.png</span> ) is used to represent the unexplained residual that is minimized through the estimation of model parameters.

The main differences between forecasting methods and forecasting models are based on two facts: the assumptions made about the error term ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_009.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_009.png</span> ), and the estimation techniques. Let’s elaborate on these differences.

Firstly, the forecasting method assumes no distributional characteristics of the error term in the forecasting process. In the case of the forecasting model, we explicitly assume that the error term follows a distribution, usually Gaussian with mean 0 and variance <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_010.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_010.png</span> , i.e. <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_011.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_011.png</span> . Making explicit assumptions of error terms is the backbone of any statistical modeling. This difference is discussed in later sections.

Secondly, forecasting methods usually find optimal parameters by minimizing the sum of squared one-step forecasting error:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_012.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_012.png</span>

In contrast, parameters of forecasting models are estimated by maximizing the likelihood calculated from the data. The likelihood is the probability of the data arising from a specified model. The technique of estimating model parameters by maximizing the log-likelihood (equivalently, minimizing the negative log-likelihood) is known as **Maximum Likelihood Estimation** ( **MLE** ). When the error term in the model is assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution, the estimation technique is called the **Ordinary Least Squares Estimation** ( **OLS** ). The specific estimation technique of a likelihood depends on the assumed distribution of the error term and the parameters of a model. However, in most scenarios, the likelihood reduces to the **Sum of Squared Errors** ( **SSE** ) computed based on the assumed model and the observed data. For example, for a regression model, the parameters are estimated by maximizing the SSE:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_013.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_013.png</span>

Forecasting models are much more complicated to estimate than regression models. Different software uses different estimation techniques and optimization algorithms. You may get slightly different answers from different software even though the same data and model specifications are fed into them.

Thirdly, purely procedural forecasting methods typically provide only point forecasts, while forecasting models provide both point forecasts and prediction intervals. However, the examples presented in *<a href="Chapter_10.xhtml#h1_216" class="chapref"> Chapter 10 </a>* showed prediction intervals for the forecasting methods, such as average, naïve, and seasonal naïve. The implementation of these methods computes prediction intervals by deriving the one-step ahead forecast standard deviation ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_014.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_014.png</span> ) from the residual standard deviation ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_015.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_015.png</span> ) under the assumption of uncorrelated residuals. It is also possible to generate prediction intervals from bootstrapped residuals, a technique used for some forecasting methods and machine learning forecasting models in which explicit assumptions for error distribution are not required.

The distinction between forecasting methods and models can be illustrated using exponential smoothing families. Each member of the exponential smoothing family has two mathematical formula representations: a forecasting method and a corresponding forecasting model. The estimation technique also changes accordingly. We are going to explore the exponential smoothing family of forecasting in detail in the following sections.

#  11.2 Exponential smoothing forecasting 

Between 1957 and 1960, exponential smoothing techniques were first introduced as forecasting methods. The formulas had no dedicated residual term and parameters were estimated by minimizing one-step-ahead forecast errors.

Hyndman et al. (2002) identified the statistical models underlying each exponential smoothing method. The models were presented in a state space framework with recursive equations that facilitate automatic forecasting for any horizons. The state space representation of models provides both point forecasts and prediction intervals.

The variations in exponential smoothing methods are predominantly based on which components are present and how they are combined in the data. We know that components can be combined in either an additive or multiplicative way.

A two-way classification of exponential smoothing methods was first proposed by Pegels (1969), considering the presence or absence of two components: trend ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_016.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_016.png</span> ) and seasonality ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_017.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_017.png</span> ). A pair of letters is used to define the presence of components, as well as how they are combined in the observed data. For example, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_018.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_018.png</span> stands for additive trend and additive seasonality. ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_019.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_019.png</span> , <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_020.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_020.png</span> ) indicates that neither of the components is observable in the data.

We have adapted the classification shown in *11.1* , from Hyndman et al. (2021), in which the methods based on multiplicative trend are excluded as they do not provide good forecasts in practice. Rather, variations on the additive damped trend ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_021.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_021.png</span> ) methods are considered. A “damping” is performed when there is a trend in the observed series that is less likely to persist for a longer period in the future.

By making explicit assumptions about error distribution, we are transitioning from *exponential* *smoothing methods* to *exponential smoothing models* . The mathematical formulas are adapted from the state space representation proposed by Hyndman et al. (2002).

There are two automated implementations of exponential smoothing models in R:

-  The ` `` ETS() `` ` function from the ` `` fable `` ` package is based on classifications included in Hyndman et al.  (2008)
-  The ` `` ets() `` ` function from the ` `` forecast `` ` package uses conventions from Hyndman et al.  (2002) and Hyndman et al. (2008), which cover a wider variety of models, including multiple trend.

We use ` `` ETS() `` ` from the ` `` fable `` ` package in this book.

Let’s take a deeper look at three prominent exponential smoothing methods and corresponding models with examples:

-  Simple exponential smoothing (SES): Applicable to data with no trend and no seasonality,
-  Holt’s linear trend method (Holt, 1957): Applicable to data with trend and no seasonality, and
-  Holt-Winters’ method (Winters, 1960): Applies to data with trend and seasonality. The seasonality could be of any frequency but works better with smaller periods, such as, quarterly, or monthly.

We will discuss mathematical equations of the component form (without error) and corresponding state space models (with error component) for each of them. The discussions and examples will help you apply this knowledge to other models that are not covered in this chapter.

#  11.3 Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES) 

SES (Brown, 1959) is the simplest exponential smoothing method. This forecasting method is applicable to a series that has no trend and no seasonal pattern. So, SES only provides an estimate of the level ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_046.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_046.png</span> ) in an observed data. While estimating the level, the associated smoothing parameter, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_047.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_047.png</span> , controls the rate at which the weights on past values decrease.

We have discussed SES forecasting models for two error structures. The additive error version was illustrated with a detailed example. Let’s see what some of the advantages and limitations of this model are:

-  Advantages:
  -  The SES method is suitable for short-term forecasting for series that have no trend and no seasonality. It is particularly effective when the underlying pattern is relatively smooth and does not exhibit complex fluctuations.
  -  One of the main advantages of SES is its simplicity. It is easy to understand and implement with minimal computational resources. This makes it particularly efficient for forecasting large collections of individual series that exhibit no trend and no seasonal components.
  -  SES allows for real-time forecasts as soon as a new data point is available. This feature makes it suitable for applications where the forecast needs to be continuously adjusted based on the latest information.

<!-- -->

-  Limitations:
  -  SES is sensitive to the choice of initial values for the smoothing process. Different initial values could lead to different forecasts when subjective judgment is involved.
  -  Assumptions of constant variance and lack of an explicit error model make SES a challenging option to assess the uncertainty around forecasts or to identify any systematic errors in the model.
  -  SES is sensitive to outliers and usually underperforms for long forecast horizons.

Business processes are usually measured with some form of trend, even though they may not have seasonality. You should use Holt’s linear trend exponential smoothing model for forecasting such data. The model is discussed in the next section.

#  11.5 Holt-Winters’ trend and seasonality method 

The work of Holt (1957) and Winters (1960) allows us to capture seasonality in exponential smoothing and is also known as Holt-Winters exponential smoothing method. This extension incorporates three components: level ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_115.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_115.png</span> ), trend ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_116.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_116.png</span> ), and seasonal pattern ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_117.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_117.png</span> ), each with smoothing parameters <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_118.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_118.png</span> , <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_119.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_119.png</span> , and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_120.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_120.png</span> , respectively. Let’s summarize the advantages and limitations of Holt-Winter’s exponential smoothing models:

-  Advantages:
  -  The Holt-Winters’ exponential smoothing method is designed to forecast series with trends and seasonality.
  -  It can capture both the additive and multiplicative seasonality, which makes it a sought-after method in business for generating short-term forecasts.
  -  The damped version allows the trend to accommodate any changes in growth in the future
-  Limitations:
  -  Like other exponential smoothing methods, the Holt-Winters’ methods are also sensitive to initial value choices and outliers
  -  They are not suitable for long-term forecasts
  -  There are many smoothing parameters that need sufficiently large historical data for estimation

From the discussion and examples presented, you may have understood that the optimal model choice largely depends on what components are observed and how they are combined in the data. Fitting a model is the simplest task once an optimal model is identified by assessing the characteristics of observed data. Practically, it is not desirable to assess each series individually to determine the best forecasting model. In a modern business scenario, you might have to forecast thousands of series on a regular basis. So, how could you accomplish such a mammoth task in a short time?

You can leverage the automated model selection capability of the ` `` ETS() `` ` function. The function evaluates the characteristics in a time series and suggests an optimal model for that series. Let’s discuss the automated model selection of the exponential smoothing family in the following section.

#  11.6 Automated model selection using ETS() 

There are three core parts of a forecasting problem:

-  Identify data characteristics
-  Find a forecasting model that best suits the data and estimate the model
-  Generate forecasts

The main problem lies in the first and second parts. What components are present in the observed series? How are they combined? What information are the estimated parameters providing? The model fits well on the training data, but why are the forecasts flat? And the questions go on.

The historical view held that optimal forecasting was more of an art than a science, as it is heavily reliant on subjective judgment. Even with the advancement of computation and technologies, identifying the best model to produce accurate forecasts is a balancing act between science and art. We should take advantage of automated processes as much as possible and allow human judgment to be strategically applied to model validation in a business context.

The ` `` ETS() `` ` function provides the functionality of automated model selection from the exponential smoothing family. Let’s explain with an example. We will use ` `` aus_livestock `` ` , which has data on monthly sheep production in different Australian states and territories. When no model specification is passed into the ` `` ETS() `` ` function, it will find the optimal model for each series by assessing individual data characteristics, evaluating candidate models. You can apply the automated model selection as shown in the following code snippet:

```
auto_ets <- aus_livestock |> 
  filter( Animal == "Sheep") |> 
  model(ets = ETS(Count))
```

The code produces a mable object, as shown here:

The ` `` ETS() `` ` function uses information criteria for model selection from the pool of plausible models. Information criteria is a measure that indicates how much information from the data is captured by a particular parameter set. It depends on the negative log-likelihood of a model and is penalized by the number of parameters used. Usually, three variations of information criteria are computed:

-  **Akaike** **’** **s Information Criterion (AIC)** : Taking <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_218.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_218.png</span> as the likelihood of a model and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_219.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_219.png</span> as the total number of parameters and initial states (including the residual variance) that need to be estimated, the AIC for ETS models is defined as follows:

  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_220.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_220.png</span>

  The model with the minimum value of AIC often provides better forecasts.

-  **Corrected Akaike** **’** **s Information Criterion (** **<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_221.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_221.png</span>** **)** : AIC tends to select models with more parameters, which pose a problem for small data. Hence, the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_222.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_222.png</span> , version is corrected for small sample bias and is defined as follows:

  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_223.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_223.png</span>

  The model with the lowest <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_224.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_224.png</span> value should be selected.

-  **Schwarz** **’** **s Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)** : The BIC (also known as SBIC or SC) is defined for ETS models as follows:

  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_225.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_225.png</span>

Like the other two criteria, the minimum value of BIC is used for selecting models. The model chosen by BIC is either the same as what AIC could have selected or has fewer terms.

The steps involved in an automatic model selection routine are summarized next:

1.   For each series, the ` `` ETS() `` ` function fits all models that are appropriate, optimizing the parameters of the models in each case.
2.   The information criteria are computed for each fitted model. By default, ` `` ETS() `` ` uses <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_226.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_226.png</span> . Users can change the criterion by passing a different information criterion name in the ic argument.
3.   The model with the minimum <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_227.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_227.png</span> is chosen as the best model.

By default, ` `` ETS() `` ` uses log-likelihood for parameter estimation. Users can choose from other optimization criteria, such as mean square error (MSE), average MSE over first forecast horizons (AMSE), standard deviation of residuals (sigma), or mean absolute error (MAE).

Existing literature suggests that three models, ETS( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_228.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_228.png</span> ), ETS( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_229.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_229.png</span> ), and ETS( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_11_230.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_11_230.png</span> ), suffer from numerical difficulties if input data has values close to zero. The ` `` ETS() `` ` function excludes these models during automated model selection. Multiplicative error models are also excluded when data contain zeros or negative values since they become numerically unstable.

Once the best exponential smoothing model is identified, you could use it to generate forecasts and calculate accuracy metrics following the coding examples presented in this chapter.

There are two limitations to ETS models:

-  It cannot handle missing values. Missing values should be imputed or interpolated before fitting any ETS models.
-  Exogenous regressors cannot be used in the modeling framework.

Despite these limitations, its simplicity compared to complex models, such as machine learning forecasting models, as well as its capability for producing short-term accurate forecasts, makes ETS a popular and ideal choice in many business forecasting scenarios.
