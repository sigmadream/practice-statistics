#  Business Applications of Time Series Analysis 

Time series analysis can be applied to solve a wide range of real-life problems. By time series applications, we refer to the domain or field of study that involves analyzing and predicting patterns, trends, and behavior in data indexed by time. The goal of time series analysis is to extract meaningful insights, understand the underlying dynamics of the data, and use them accordingly. This chapter highlights time series applications in various industries and disciplines, including finance, economics, business, medicine, retail, public health, tourism, healthcare, manufacturing, and more.

Identification of a time series analysis problem is crucial because the proper choice of analytics methods depends on the nature and objectives of a problem. A misalignment between a problem and the method adopted for a solution will cause confusion rather than being a solution. By consulting the problem spaces covered in this chapter and the high-level concept of the method, you will be able to assess your problem and which methods to use for a solution.

From the definition of time series, you must have realized that time series data are numerical data in nature. Standard textbooks on the analysis of numerical data cover four key analytics objectives:

-  Visualization
-  Summary/descriptive analysis
-  Inferential analysis
-  Predictive analysis

Time series analytics also encompass challenges commonly faced in numerical data analysis. However, due to the incorporation of a time index alongside numerical observations, this problem space necessitates the application of extended analytics methods that often go beyond standard numerical data analysis. These specialized methods will be explored in upcoming chapters. In the current chapter, you will gain insights into when time series analysis techniques are employed to tackle real-life problems.

It’s crucial to highlight that this chapter focuses exclusively on problem spaces that directly leverage time series characteristics. However, it’s worth noting that numerous inferential and predictive analytics methods within numerical data analysis offer the capability to incorporate time series data attributes, particularly serial dependency. These methods will be discussed in later chapters of the book.

-  Section 6.1: Time series characteristics level problem domains
-  Section 6.2: Inference and attribution problem domains
-  Section 6.3: Impact measurement problem domains
-  Section 6.4: Forecasting problem domains

#  6.1 Time series characteristics level problem domains 

Understanding the inherent characteristics of time series is crucial for effective analysis, modeling, and interpretation. The time series characteristics-based problem space focuses on understanding and describing fundamental properties and attributes of time series data. Recognizing these characteristics is essential for drawing meaningful insights. Three major problem spaces exist around time series characteristics:

-  Trend-cycle analysis
-  Seasonality analysis
-  Anomaly detection

We will now discuss the nature of these problems in detail. First, you will learn how trend analysis can be very helpful to businesses.

##  6.1.1 Trend (trend-cycle) analysis 

Trend analysis is one of the most encountered problems in time series analysis. This analysis can be performed on time series data with or without seasonality. Trend analysis can be a part of visual, descriptive, inferential, or predictive analytics depending on specific requirements. Trend analysis involves examining historical data over a period to identify and understand long-term patterns and changes. It helps in uncovering underlying trends that can provide insights into the direction of a phenomenon, predicting future behavior to aid decision-making. Some examples include the following:

-  **Economics** : Analyzing trends in economic indicators, such as gross domestic product (GDP), unemployment rates, and inflation, to understand the health of an economy.
-  **Finance** : Studying trends in stock prices, commodity prices, and exchange rates to make investment decisions in finance.
-  **Inventory management** : Using trend analysis to anticipate demand for products or services and make informed decisions about production, inventory, services, and marketing strategies.
-  **Public health** : Identifying trends in patient data to predict disease outbreaks and allocate medical resources effectively in public health.

Trend analysis is also known as trend-cycle analysis. Both trend and cycle are long-term behaviors, and that’s why they often need to be analyzed together. Short-term trends are often part of and could be influenced by economic cycles, business cycles, or other external factors. A common example of trend-cycle analysis is a change in economic indicators such as GDP, which is subject to movement of the official interest rate, which often follows a cycle due to monetary policy to tackle inflation.

Trend analysis in time series involves uncovering meaningful patterns and changes within historical data to gain insights into long-term behaviors. This process is crucial for making accurate predictions, informed decisions, and strategic planning in various domains. Intuitively, a trend analysis is as simple as fitting a trend line on a scatter plot, which is achievable using common software such as Excel, however, in the time series context, it requires additional care, and this is covered in more detail in the time series smoothing and decomposition chapter.

Use of trend analysis is widespread in business decision-making via the monitoring of key business metrics. Trend analysis is the most presented visual in the media when covering news about economics and finance.

To illustrate the idea, we will use the monthly retail sales index for Switzerland between 2000 and 2020. ##  6.1.2 Seasonality analysis 

Similar to trend analysis, seasonality analysis is a common type of time series analysis used in visual, descriptive, inferential, or predictive analytics. Seasonality analysis in time series involves the identification, characterization, and interpretation of repeating patterns or fluctuations that occur at fixed intervals such as days, weeks, months, or quarters. Understanding seasonality is essential for accurate trend analysis and forecasting, as it helps capture repeated behavior and thus adjust predictions accordingly. Seasonality analysis involves identifying the pattern, magnitude, and consistency of the seasonal component. As a systematic component of the data, this pattern can be used for the following:

-  **Marketing** : Understanding seasonal shopping patterns to optimize inventory management and marketing campaigns.
-  **Tourism** : Analyzing peak travel seasons to plan resource allocation and pricing strategies.
-  **Demand planning** : Predicting electricity or heating demand based on seasonal usage patterns.
-  **Agriculture** : Anticipating planting and harvesting seasons to optimize crop management.

One key element of seasonality analysis is quantifying the variation in the observed data that occurs due to seasonality. To provide an example of this, we will use the ` `` aus_accommodation `` ` data from the ` `` fpp3 `` ` package. This dataset contains the quarterly percentage of room occupancy rates from short-term accommodation from 1998 Q1 to 2016 Q2 in Australia. ##  6.1.3 Outlier/anomaly detection 

Time series anomaly detection involves identifying observations or patterns in time series data that deviate significantly from expected or normal behavior. Anomalies can signify unusual events, errors, faults, or other irregularities that require attention or investigation. Solving time series anomaly detection problems is crucial for ensuring the reliability and quality of data-driven processes. Some examples of time series anomaly detection problems are the following:

-  **Cybersecurity** : Detecting unusual patterns in network traffic that might indicate potential cyber-attacks or unauthorized access.
-  **Industrial IoT** : Identifying anomalies in sensor data to detect equipment malfunctions or deviations from expected performance.
-  **Finance** : Detecting fraudulent transactions or activities that deviate from typical financial behavior.
-  **Healthcare** : Identifying anomalies in patient vital signs or medical sensor data to alert medical professionals to critical conditions.
-  **Energy** : Detecting abnormal energy consumption patterns that might indicate system malfunctions or inefficiencies.

Solving time series anomaly detection problems involves methods and models that identify deviations from normal patterns. Accurate anomaly detection is crucial for ensuring the integrity, security, and efficiency of various systems and processes that rely on time-ordered data. Various statistical and machine learning (ML) algorithms can be applied too. This problem space can work with data with or without trend and/or seasonality. Time series-based anomaly detection and imputation techniques are presented in *<a href="Chapter_17.xhtml#h1_363" class="chapref"> Chapter 17 </a>* .

Time series characteristics-based problem spaces are a foundational step in time series analysis. Recognizing trends, seasonality, and other attributes helps in the proper selection of techniques, models, and strategies for extracting valuable insights from time series data.

#  6.2 Inference and attribution problem domains 

A time series attribution model refers to the analytical framework that is used to distribute the impact of different factors on changes observed in time-evolving series of outcomes. This problem space often arises in scenarios where you want to understand how multiple independent variables contribute to changes in a dependent variable that is structured as a time series. Examples of such problems are the following:

-  **Marketing attribution** : Understanding how various marketing channels (e.g., social media, email, paid ads) impact a time series metric such as sales or website visits.
-  **Economic indicators** : Assessing the influence of economic factors (e.g., interest rates, GDP, inflation) on time series outcomes such as stock prices or consumer spending.
-  **Healthcare** : Analyzing how different treatments, patient demographics, or external factors influence patient recovery rates over time.
-  **Supply chain** : Examining the impact of different factors (e.g., raw material costs, shipping delays) on inventory levels or production output.

Time series regression techniques can be used to meet the analytical needs of this problem space. This involves building regression models where time series data is used as both independent and dependent variables. This approach captures the effects of various factors on the time series outcome. An alternative could be using ML algorithms that can handle complex interactions and patterns in the data, such as random forests or gradient boosting.

When a statistical regression model is used, examining the significance of coefficients helps identify which variables have a statistically significant impact on the time series outcome. However, an ML model will achieve only attribution with a quantified direction without inference.

Accurate attribution can lead to better decision-making, improved resource allocation, and a deeper understanding of dynamics influencing time series outcomes. The methods discussed in *<a href="Chapter_15.xhtml#h1_318" class="chapref"> Chapter 15 </a>* are useful for solving inference and attribution problems involving time series.

#  6.3 Impact measurement problem domains 

An extension of attribution modeling is causal impact estimation using time series. Causal impact analysis in time series data involves assessing the impact of a specific intervention or event on a time series variable. This analysis aims to determine whether the intervention caused a significant change in the observed time series and to quantify the magnitude of that impact. The problem space of causal impact analysis using time series data can include the following:

-  **Policy evaluation** : Assessing how a policy change (e.g., tax reform, regulation) affects economic indicators such as GDP, unemployment rates, or consumer spending.
-  **Treatment/drug testing** : Analyzing whether the introduction of a new medical treatment or drug influences patient outcomes over time.
-  **Product launches** : Measuring the effect of introducing a new product on overall sales or market share.
-  **Social media trends** : Investigating whether a viral social media post or event affects user engagement metrics on a platform.

A critical challenge is estimating what the time series would have looked like in the absence of intervention, which serves as a baseline for comparison. This can be achieved by following a variety of avenues, such as before-and-after comparison. In such problems, we can compare time series behavior before and after the intervention to identify any meaningful changes. Causal effect can also be estimated through post-intervention change by assessing whether there is a statistically significant change in time series behavior after the intervention. Such analysis can both detect and measure the magnitude of causal impact.

Causal impact analysis is vital for making informed decisions, understanding the effectiveness of interventions, and distinguishing between causation and correlation in time series data. *<a href="Chapter_15.xhtml#h1_318" class="chapref"> Chapter 15 </a>* discusses time series based methods for causal impact analysis.

#  6.4 Forecasting problem domains 

Forecasting is perhaps the most intuitive problem space for time series data. Forecasting is often confused with prediction, and we distinguish forecasting from prediction by adopting the definition that forecasting is a process of estimating the future path of a variable. In contrast, prediction covers the problem space where the user wants to know the conditional mean of a variable based on given values of a set of correlated variables. Generally, trend (trend-cycle) line fitting with or without adjusting for seasonality is a predictive modeling problem space instead of forecasting.

The time series forecasting problem space entails predicting future values of a time-dependent variable based on historical observations. This problem is central to numerous domains, as accurate forecasts facilitate informed decision-making, resource allocation, and strategic planning. The time series forecasting problem space spans a wide range of domains, including the following:

-  **Business and finance** : Forecasting sales, revenue, marketing expenditure, and financial indicators to support budgeting and investment decisions.
-  **Supply chain management** : Forecasting demand for products or resources to optimize inventory levels and production scheduling.
-  **Economics** : Forecasting economic indicators such as GDP, inflation, and unemployment rates to guide policy decisions, such as setting official interest rates.
-  **Energy** : Forecasting energy consumption and demand patterns to ensure efficient resource allocation for uninterrupted supply.
-  **Demography** : Forecasting the future population of a country to support infrastructure planning.
-  **Meteorology** : Forecasting weather conditions and natural phenomena to enable preparedness and response efforts.

Accurate forecasts empower organizations to make proactive decisions, allocate resources effectively, and adapt to changing trends and conditions.
