#  ARIMA Forecasting Models 

The univariate **Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average** ( **ARIMA** ) models are classical statistical models that leverage the autocorrelation structure of a series. Introduced by Box et al. (1970), the ARIMA models were a revolutionary technique in time series analysis and forecasting during that era. The ARIMA model class includes a wide variety of individual models that have great applicability to data originating in many fields, such as finance, economics, retail, and supply chain management. In many business scenarios, ARIMA models are a good contender for generating accurate forecasts if the observed series is long enough and has sufficient autocorrelation structure that can be leveraged. The inferences provided by the ARIMA models and the insights from forecasts generated from them are crucial to making strategic business decisions.

As the benefits of the ARIMA models became evident in many areas, they were still deemed to be used by only experts, largely due to the subjectivity involved in the model selection process. ARIMA model selection required deep statistical knowledge, expertise in the corresponding subject matter, and a significant amount of subjective decision-making and intuition. There was a famous quote saying that ARIMA modeling was more art than science.

Over the decades, researchers developed various extensions and refinements to handle non-stationarity and seasonality, including statistical tests to bring objectivity to various parameter selections. The need for automation and simplification led to advancements in computational algorithms.

The automatic ARIMA algorithm (Hyndman et al., 2008) revolutionized the field by providing a systematic, automated approach to model selection and fitting. This algorithm has democratized the use of ARIMA models for practitioners with varying levels of expertise.

The automatic algorithm was first implemented as ` `` auto.arima() `` ` function in the forecast package. The fable package implements a variation of the Hyndman-Khandakar algorithm via the ` `` ARIMA() `` ` function. In this chapter, we will use the latter implementation in the discussion.

A univariate time series has a different structure compared to cross-sectional data. A time series has temporal autocorrelation, which does not exist in cross-sectional data. On the other hand, whatever techniques are used for modeling time series should be statistically sound too.

In the previous chapter, exponential smoothing models were discussed. The exponential smoothing models use descriptions of trend and seasonality in time series, whereas ARIMA models the inter-dependency by capturing autocorrelation structure in the data.

ARIMA models are best suited for univariate time series data that is collected at regular intervals. They can capture data dynamics better when a long historical pattern is available and well suited for generating long-term forecasts. The ARIMA model has the capabilities to detect and capture seasonality quite well. It can also incorporate exogenous variables along with a forecast variable, a characteristic uncommon in other time series techniques.

ARIMA is a framework that includes a range of models, even though the term is used to denote an individual model or the full framework interchangeably. It consists of three core components. Each component is discussed in the following sections before putting them into a general structure.

#  12.3 Integration (the I in ARIMA) 

Let’s revisit the assumption of the multivariate regression model in *Equation (12.1)* . We know that one critical assumption for estimating parameters using a statistical technique is the distribution of each variable <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_043.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_043.png</span> , should remain the same from sample to sample. In other words, their statistical properties are “stationary” (not changing) even though actual observations may change.

This is in stark contrast to time series data. For instance, if you sample a monthly time series with a positive trend, the average in May would be lower than the average in June, which would be lower than the average in July. The statistical properties (i.e., mean) change over time. Alternatively, we can say the statistical properties depend on the time period. This phenomenon is known as non-stationarity.

Time series data is, in general, non-stationary as the underlying distribution changes due to the trend and seasonality over time. A change in variance also makes a series non-stationary. Some purely cyclical time series can be stationary because their mean and variance remain constant over time, even if the length of their cycles varies.

So, what is the problem modeling a non-stationary series? As the mean, variance, and autocorrelation structure changes, the parameters of a model would be unstable and inconsistent throughout the data. You may model spurious correlations simply because the surface pattern could hide the actual inter-dependency between observations. The model identification would be complex, hard to interpret, and above all, the models would have limited predictive power, which could lead to poor forecasting performance.

Fundamentally, you cannot confidently estimate parameters from non-stationary data. A non-stationary time series must be transformed into a stationary series before any parameter estimation takes place.

The solution to the problem is quite simple. This method is known as differencing (the opposite of integration).

-  **Differencing** ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_044.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_044.png</span> ): In a non-seasonal series, differencing means to subtract subsequent observations from the previous one.
-  **Seasonal differencing** ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_045.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_045.png</span> ): When subtraction takes place between an observation and the observation from the same season in the previous years.

The parameters, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_046.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_046.png</span> or <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_047.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_047.png</span> , denote the degree of differencing required to make a non-stationary series a stationary one, and take different values based on the structure of the data. Usually, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_048.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_048.png</span> and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_049.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_049.png</span> .

For a stationary series <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_050.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_050.png</span> and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_051.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_051.png</span> .

Now the question is how do you objectively know whether differencing is even required? Or, if needed, which order would be the optimal one?

We’ve also discussed several statistical tests to identify unit roots in a time series in this book. Automated ARIMA uses the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin (KPSS) test (Kwiatkowski et al., 1992) to first check whether non-seasonal data is stationary or not. If data turns out non-stationary, then it determines the order of differencing by repetitive application of the KPSS test on differenced data.

For a seasonal series, the order of seasonal differencing is determined by evaluating the seasonal strength of an STL decomposition. The ` `` unitroot_ndiffs() `` ` and ` `` unitroot_nsdiffs() `` ` functions are used to identify the order of non-seasonal and seasonal differencing by applying KPSS tests or measuring seasonal strength, respectively.

Let’s use the ` `` unitroot_ndiffs() `` ` function on the USA GDP and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_059.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_059.png</span> to determine the order of differencing in an automated way:

```
usa_gdp <- usa_gdp |>
  mutate(gdp_log = log(gdp))

usa_gdp_diff <- usa_gdp |>
  select(year, gdp, gdp_log) %>%
  pivot_longer(cols = c(gdp, gdp_log),
               names_to = "series",
               values_to = "values") |>
  group_by(series) |>
  features(values, unitroot_ndiffs)

usa_gdp_diff
```

#  12.4 Moving Average model (the MA in ARIMA) 

In the time series context, we assume that the current observation is a mix of actual measurement and some “noise” denoted by <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_071.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_071.png</span> . In *<a href="Chapter_9.xhtml#h1_201" class="chapref"> Chapter 9 </a>* , we discussed **Exponential Weighted Moving Average** ( **EWMA** ) smoothing and filters based on MA.

Techniques like EWMA and MA filters reduce noise from data. On the other hand, an MA model captures and estimates the dependency pattern of a variable <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_072.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_072.png</span> through any information available in its noise. The sources of noise in a time series could be measurement errors, system imperfections, or errors made during data processing.

If the observation <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_073.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_073.png</span> depends on the past <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_074.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_074.png</span> terms of its noise, then the model is called an <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_075.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_075.png</span> model and written as:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_076.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_076.png</span>

where <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_077.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_077.png</span> is a white noise process, that is, it follows a Gaussian distribution with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_078.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_078.png</span> mean and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_079.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_079.png</span> standard deviation.

It is perhaps counterintuitive that stochastic noise, though unmeasurable, can be accurately modeled and estimated. In hindsight, it is the smartness of the ARIMA model. In real life, it is not possible to measure any data-generating process without error. It is also not feasible to build a perfect model to capture all the inter-dependencies in data. There will be some “information” left over in the error series.

When the AR terms are sufficient to capture the majority of the data dynamics, the leftover information flowing to the errors is minimal, and no MA term is required. When the autocorrelation in a variable <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_080.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_080.png</span> is weak (AR terms are not sufficient to capture inter-dependency), then more information ends up in the error series, which is then captured by the MA terms. In such situations, inter-dependency in the errors provides better information about the future path than the dependency structure in the series itself.

Let’s explain how to estimate MA terms in an ARIMA model with a non-seasonal simulated series with weak autocorrelation ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_081.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_081.png</span> ) and strong dependency on the two neighboring error terms ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_082.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_082.png</span> and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_083.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_083.png</span> ). #  12.5 General structure of an ARIMA model 

Combining three different components; AR (autoregressive), I (integration), and MA (moving average), we obtain a general structure of an ARIMA model. There are two versions, depending on the frequency in the data, non-seasonal and seasonal.

##  12.5.1 Non-seasonal ARIMA model 

A non-seasonal <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_107.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_107.png</span> model incorporates three parts:

-  **Autoregressive (AR)** : Represents the dependency of an observation on a number of lagged observations. The parameter <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_108.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_108.png</span> determines how many lagged observations are needed to explain the inter-dependency. <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_109.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_109.png</span> is known as the order of the AR part.
-  **Differencing** : Determines how many times a series needs to be differenced before it becomes stationary. The <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_110.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_110.png</span> parameter determines the order of differencing.
-  **Moving Average (MA)** : Represents the dependency of an observation on lagged residual errors. The <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_111.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_111.png</span> parameter is the order of the MA model, which indicates how many residual error terms are used in the model.

##  12.5.2 Seasonal ARIMA models 

The dependency structure in a seasonal series can be separated into the following six parts:

-  Non-seasonal differencing, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_129.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_129.png</span> : differencing order to make a series stationary by subtracting consecutive observations
-  Non-seasonal autocorrelation structure <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_130.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_130.png</span> : observations at time <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_131.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_131.png</span> , <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_132.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_132.png</span> , depend on the immediate past values <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_133.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_133.png</span>
-  Non-seasonal moving average structure, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_134.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_134.png</span> : observations at time <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_135.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_135.png</span> , <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_136.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_136.png</span> , depend on the immediate past error terms <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_137.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_137.png</span>
-  Seasonal differencing, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_138.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_138.png</span> : differencing order to make the series stationary by subtracting observations from the same season in the past years

#  12.7 Steps in fitting an ARIMA model 

The general steps involved in fitting an ARIMA model for a non-seasonal time series are summarized here.

1.   Observe the time plot and identify any unusual observations.
2.   If required, transform data to stabilize the variance using Box-Cox or log transformation.
3.   Take differences on non-stationary data to make them stationary
4.   Examine the ACF and PACF plot. A good starting point would be an <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_195.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_195.png</span> or <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_196.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_196.png</span> model.
5.   Fit your chosen model(s). If you have more than one model, choose the one with the minimum <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_197.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_197.png</span> values.
6.   Perform residual diagnostic checks on the errors generated from the chosen model. This involves examining the residual ACF and doing a portmanteau test. If the residuals are not white noise, go back to step <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_198.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_198.png</span> and try searching for a better model.
7.   Once the residual is deemed white noise, use the model for forecasts.

The number of steps and iterations is amplified while dealing with a seasonal series.

The Hyndman-Khandakar algorithm (Hyndman et al., 2008) implemented automation in steps 3-5. You need to take care with other steps to obtain better forecasting performance, even when an automated algorithm is used.

#  12.8 Automatic ARIMA algorithm 

The stepwise search enables the automatic algorithm to find the best model by fitting a bare minimum of potential models. The steps for non-seasonal data are as follows:

1.   The number of differences <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_212.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_212.png</span> is determined using repeated KPSS tests.
2.   The values of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_213.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_213.png</span> and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_214.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_214.png</span> are then chosen by minimizing the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_215.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_215.png</span> after differencing the data <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_216.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_216.png</span> times. Rather than considering every possible combination of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_217.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_217.png</span> and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_218.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_218.png</span> , the algorithm uses a stepwise search to traverse the model space.
    1.   Four initial models are fitted:
        -  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_219.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_219.png</span>
        -  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_220.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_220.png</span>
        -  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_221.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_221.png</span>

If <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_224.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_224.png</span> , an additional model is also fitted:
        -  <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_225.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_225.png</span> without a constant, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_226.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_226.png</span> .

    4.   Variations on the current model are considered:

        -  vary <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_228.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_228.png</span> and/or <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_229.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_229.png</span> from the current model by <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_230.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_230.png</span> ;

The benefit of the stepwise search is that it considerably reduces the total number of potential models to fit. In every step, the algorithm updates the “current model” according to the minimum <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_233.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_233.png</span> value, and then searches in the nearest neighbors of the current model for a better one. By default, the ` `` ARIMA() `` ` function performs a stepwise search and switches to a new “current model” as soon as a better model is found, instead of going through all the neighboring models. The ` `` greedy=FALSE `` ` option enables a full nearest neighboring search. In addition to that, some approximations are performed to further speed up the procedure. If a minimum <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_234.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_234.png</span> model is not found due to approximation, or even due to the stepwise search, users can turn off the stepwise search by setting ` `` stepwise=FALSE `` ` . There are options to use other information criteria, such as <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_235.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_235.png</span> or <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_236.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_236.png</span> , for selecting a model.

The ` `` trace=TRUE `` ` arguments print the fitted models information on console. You could experiment with various arguments and observe the difference in potential model fits with and without stepwise search option.

Apart from R packages, the auto ARIMA algorithm is implemented in a lot of proprietary software dedicated to forecasting, such as AWS forecasting algorithms (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) Algorithm-Amazon Forecast), Google Cloud’s BigQuery ML ` `` ARIMA_PLUS `` ` (Cheng et al., 2021) to name a few.

While complex modeling processes can be automated, it is equally important to understand the impact of generated forecasts in a business context. Next, we discuss the power of the ARIMA model on forecast explainability.

#  12.9 ARIMA and forecast explainability 

Forecast explainability refers to the ability to understand and interpret the underlying reasons behind forecasts generated from a model. It involves identifying and communicating the key factors and data patterns that influence the forecasted outcomes, making the forecasting process more transparent and trustworthy.

ARIMA models support forecast explainability by providing clear and interpretable components such as autoregressive terms, differencing, and moving averages that reflect the underlying patterns in data. By analyzing these components, users can understand the influence of past values, trends, and noise on future predictions, making the forecasting process more transparent and understandable.

The ARIMA model is a useful technique to generate long-term forecasts for series with a long history. The ` `` ARIMA() `` ` function is highly useful to find an optimal model from a pool of a large number of potential models. There are scenarios where even the best-fitted ARIMA model produces undesirable forecasts. Those situations would be easy to comprehend if you are aware of the impact of various parameters on forecast values obtained by ARIMA models.

##  12.9.2 Prediction intervals from ARIMA models 

The prediction intervals from ARIMA models only consider the variations in errors. Other sources, such as variations in parameter estimation and model order selection, are not accounted for. This is a reason why ARIMA-based prediction intervals tend to be narrow.

By default, the computation of prediction intervals assumes residuals are normally distributed and uncorrelated. The prediction interval may be incorrect if these assumptions are not satisfied. It is crucial to always perform residual diagnostics using residual’s ACF and histogram to validate the core assumptions and use the prediction intervals.

The prediction interval computed from ARIMA models also assumes that the past pattern in the data will continue into the forecast period. If there is a significant change at the end of the series compared to the full historical data, it is suggested to use prediction intervals with caution.

##  12.9.3 Impact of differencing orders on prediction intervals 

Generally, prediction intervals from ARIMA models widen as the forecast horizon increases. The value of the differencing order <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_251.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_251.png</span> has an impact on prediction intervals. The higher the value of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_12_252.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_12_252.png</span> , the more rapidly the prediction intervals increase in size.
