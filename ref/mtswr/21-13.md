In this chapter, we will explore several computational predictive methods for univariate time series forecasting. This field has gained substantial attention in recent literature and in applied forecasting due to the ever-rising popularity of various machine learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) based prediction algorithms. ML, which includes DL, is collectively referred to as machine learning, is an influential field within artificial intelligence that focuses on creating algorithms that allow computers to learn from data and make informed, data-driven decisions. Rather than being explicitly programmed for specific tasks, ML algorithms detect patterns within data and leverage these patterns for predictions. These algorithms have proven highly effective across various domains, from less sensitive applications, such as targeted advertising for tech giants to highly sensitive tasks such as predicting the preoperative risk of death for cardiac surgery patients.

By the time of the M5 competition, the top 50 forecasting methods were based on ML, highlighting the alignment of ML with certain problem spaces in forecasting.

#  13.1 Understanding the connection between forecasting and prediction 

Before you dive into prediction-based algorithms for forecasting, it is important to understand the difference between the terms *prediction* and *forecasting* . ML techniques primarily aim to enhance the accuracy of predictions for a variable of interest. These advanced methods leverage algorithms to make informed predictions about unknown outcomes based on known input data. The terms prediction and forecasting are often mistakenly used interchangeably. While they may seem synonymous in everyday language, they carry distinct meanings in academic literature. In time series analysis, a statistical forecast refers specifically to predicting the future behavior of an observed random variable based on its past states. This is rooted in the theory that an observed sequence is the outcome of a random process, where randomness results from the interaction of many independent factors in a linear fashion.

Dynamical systems theory, however, suggests that what may appear as random behavior could arise from deterministic systems with a few factors interacting non-linearly. This opens the possibility of reframing forecasting as a supervised learning problem, where ML techniques can be applied to capture complex non-linear relationships and enhance prediction accuracy.

Supervised learning involves building a model from a finite set of observations to establish the relationship between input and output variables. Once developed, the model can be used for one-step or multi-step forecasting. In the context of forecasting, when past values of a time series are available, the forecasting task can be framed as a standard prediction regression problem using lagged values of the time series and the predictions from the regression model can be considered as forecasts (Bontempi et al., 2013). Several approaches have been explored, and this chapter will cover two important techniques useful for univariate forecasting that have gained substantial popularity in recent applications, the neural network prediction and ML-based prediction for forecasting.

In its simplest form, **Time Series Linear Models** ( **TSLMs** ), as discussed in *<a href="Chapter_10.xhtml#h1_216" class="chapref"> Chapter 10 </a>* , explicitly measure dependencies over time by estimating deterministic trends and seasonal components. However, in supervised learning, more advanced methods, particularly those based on ML and DL, have proven extremely effective for handling tabular data, and these will be the focus of this chapter.

Forecasting through prediction involves designing an input/output process, where the output is a scalar and the input is a vector. This relies on the availability of a collection of observed pairs, typically referred to as a training set. In forecasting, the training set is derived from historical time-series data, where the input matrix, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_001.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_001.png</span> , represents the lag dependencies, and the output vector, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_002.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_002.png</span> , represents the forecasted values. #  13.2 DL forecasting: Neural network 

DL is a specialized area of ML that leverages artificial neural networks to model and solve complex problems. Inspired by the structure and functioning of the human brain, it enables computers to learn from large volumes of data. Neural networks are structured as layers of interconnected nodes (often referred to as neurons), and their purpose is to predict outcomes based on input data. These networks excel in recognizing intricate patterns within data, making them particularly effective for tasks, such as forecasting. In recent years, DL algorithms have gained significant popularity due to advancements in computational techniques that allow for faster training and convergence at scale (Bengio et al., 2017). One of the key advantages of DL over traditional ML algorithms is its ability to automatically extract complex features from vast datasets. Moreover, these complex features can be shared across different parts of the network, enhancing its ability to perform sophisticated prediction tasks.

A neural network consists of three primary layers:

-  **Input layer** : This is the first layer, where data features are fed into the network. Each node in this layer represents a unique feature from the input data.
-  **Hidden layers** : In this middle layer computations are performed, allowing the network to learn complex feature representations. Each node in a hidden layer applies a weighted sum to its inputs and processes the result through a non-linear activation function, such as the Sigmoid function. The more hidden layers a network has, the better it can capture complex relationships within the data.
-  **Output layer** : The final layer generates the network’s predictions. In regression tasks, this layer typically consists of a single neuron with a linear activation function, providing a continuous output value.

The simplest form of a neural network consists solely of an input layer and an output layer, devoid of any hidden layers. This structure is analogous to a linear regression model without interaction terms, where the relationships between variables are purely linear.

The core components required for a neural network are the weights and biases, along with the activation function. Weights are parameters that control the strength of the connections between neurons across different layers of the network. When an input is introduced to a neuron, each feature of the input is multiplied by a corresponding weight, which determines the significance of that feature in shaping the final decision. Bias, another important parameter within each neuron, allows the model to adjust the activation function curve. This bias is added to the weighted sum of the inputs before the result is passed through the activation function. The inclusion of bias enables the network to model data that doesn’t necessarily pass through the origin, essentially handling situations where the input is zero, but the output is not. Once the weighted sum of the inputs and bias is calculated, the result is passed through an activation function, such as ReLU, Sigmoid, or tanh. The activation function is crucial in introducing non-linearity to the model, allowing it to capture and represent intricate patterns within the data that would otherwise be missed by a purely linear model.

The learning process of a neural network, known as forward and backward propagation, hinges on the continual adjustment of weights and biases. In forward propagation, input data traverses through the network’s layers, where each neuron computes a weighted sum, applies an activation function, and forwards the output to the next layer. This sequence continues until the network produces a prediction. The accuracy of this prediction is evaluated using a loss function, which measures the deviation between predicted and actual values. In regression tasks, a common loss function is the **Mean Squared Error** ( **MSE** ), which penalizes larger errors more heavily.

The process of adjusting the network’s weights and biases involves minimizing the loss function. This is achieved by computing the gradient of the loss with respect to each weight and bias, and then updating these parameters using an optimization algorithm, such as gradient descent. Through this iterative process, the model gradually improves, reducing the error and enhancing the accuracy of its predictions.

Training a multi-layer feed-forward neural network for regression involves finding the set of weights, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_010.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_010.png</span> (which are equivalent to the coefficients attached to these predictors), and biases, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_011.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_011.png</span> , in the network that minimizes the difference between the network’s predicted outputs and the actual target values. The forward propagation, loss calculation, and backpropagation are repeated for a specified number of iterations (epochs) or until the loss converges to a minimum. During training, the network gradually adjusts its weights and biases to minimize the loss function, improving its predictions on the training data. The choice of random initial weights can impact the convergence and accuracy measure of the forecasts. To average out the fluctuations of random initialization the final forecasts are derived by averaging forecasts over multiple initializations of a feed-forward neural network.

An advantage of employing DL methodologies for time series forecasting lies in their capacity to automatically engineer complex features within their hidden layers that can improve forecasting accuracy. This approach is particularly effective when the underlying data exhibits sharable or transferable features. In univariate forecasting, sharable or transferable features, refer to fundamental, abstract patterns that a DL model automatically learns within a single time series. These are not specific data points but general mathematical properties, such as recognizing robust autoregressive relationships (the dependence on recent past values), identifying the general shape of a seasonality and cycle (e.g., lynx population boom every 11 years), or detecting long-term trends. By identifying these universal building blocks of time series data in its hidden layers, the model can apply this learned knowledge efficiently across different time periods, improving forecast accuracy without needing explicit human instruction for feature engineering.

##  13.2.1 Forecasting using neural networks

-  We will use data up to 1920 for training/model development, reserving the remaining data for validation. Split the training (from 1821 to 1919) and test set (from 1920 to 1934), as shown in the following code:

  ```
  lynx_train <- lynx_tsb |> filter(year < 1920)
  lynx_test <- lynx_tsb |> filter(year >= 1920)
  ```

-  It is important to note that the lynx population count is constrained by a lower bound of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_042.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_042.png</span> . To preserve this non-negative characteristic in forecasting, we will apply a square root transformation (sqrt) to the series. For the **Neural Network Autoregressive** ( **NNETAR** ) forecasting, we will use an input layer with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_043.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_043.png</span> inputs, a hidden layer with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_044.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_044.png</span> neurons, and an output layer. The ` `` NNETAR() `` ` function from the ` `` fable `` ` package automates this process. It selects the number of nodes in the input layer based on the estimated order of the linear AR model, which is chosen by minimizing the **Akaike Information Criterion** ( **AIC** ). By default, the size of the hidden layer is half the size of the input layer, rounded to the nearest integer. For seasonal time series, unless specified otherwise by the user, the default choice of seasonal lag is <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_045.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_045.png</span> .

-  A primary benefit of utilizing automated DL forecasting functions, such as NNETAR is the inherent ability to bypass the manual selection of model parameters. The function can automatically initialize these settings. Nonetheless, advanced users can leverage the algorithm’s flexibility by manually adjusting the neural network’s structure through parameter input. This design accommodates both novice users seeking a straightforward, naïve application and expert users requiring granular control over the model architecture.

##  13.2.2 Prediction interval for neural networks 

The prediction interval for NNETAR demands additional explanation as it is derived using a simulation-based approach compared to the closed-form analytical approach used in statistical forecasting models. This is due to the non-existence of an underlying probabilistic model in NNETAR. The prediction interval is derived under an assumed functional form for the NNETAR model. For the lynx example, the underlying model can be assumed as follows:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_049.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_049.png</span>

where <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_050.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_050.png</span> is a vector containing lagged values of the series, and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_051.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_051.png</span> is a neural network with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_052.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_052.png</span> hidden nodes in a single layer. The error, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_053.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_053.png</span> , series is assumed to be homoscedastic (and possibly also normally distributed).

Under this formulation, it is possible to generate future sample paths by iteratively sampling <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_054.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_054.png</span> from its assumed normal distribution or empirical distribution. By repeatedly simulating the sample path, say, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_055.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_055.png</span> times, we will be able to get an empirical distribution of the forecast for each value of the forecast horizon. The lower and upper quantiles of these forecast distributions will serve as prediction intervals for NNETAR forecasts. This is exactly what was used in our example and that is the reason behind using a seed in our code while deriving forecasts using the ` `` NNETAR() `` ` function.

However, DL methods typically operate as black boxes in forecasting applications. The presence of non-zero weights connecting the hidden layers to the output layer may present a substantial challenge in interpreting the specific influence of these auto-curated features in many business contexts. This can be a critical drawback in situations requiring actionable insights into why a particular forecast was generated. To facilitate a clearer, more interpretable understanding of the relationship between input features and the resulting forecast, one might consider exploring alternative ML approaches, which will be detailed in the following section.

#  13.3 ML forecasting 

Supervised ML is a blend of art and science that involves creative feature engineering, careful tuning of the algorithmic parameters and intelligent validation to derive the best prediction. In addition to this, top performing ML algorithms benefit from fine-tuning their configuration, often referred to as tuning hyperparameters. The list of algorithms used in ML is already huge, and that list is ever-growing. No wonder there is no R package that works like a breeze to perform forecasting using all ML algorithms. The ` `` forecastML `` ` package (Redell, 2022), inspired by Bergmeir et al. (2018), can help to curate and manage features in an efficient way. This also gives a convenient wrapper for virtually any ML algorithm available in R to be ported into forecasting. The package also provides some fine control over combining forecasts over different forecast horizons and visualization.

It is important to realize that the purpose of this section is not to serve as a user manual for a specific R package. This section also does not intend to cover the details of various ML methods and compare them. It is already well known to practitioners of ML algorithms that tabular data methods based on ensemble learning algorithms, for example, LightGBM and Random Forest, show superior predictive performance compared to a single linear model. So, forecasting based on properly tuned and validated ensemble learning algorithms is expected to perform better. However, anyone with some level of knowledge of these algorithms will realize that writing code for implementing alternative methods will be lengthy even for simple use cases. This is due to the difference in the way these algorithms use input data, and the difference in the list of hyperparameters that require tuning these algorithms. To avoid those complexities and to keep things concise, we will illustrate the core idea using a regularized regression algorithm. This will offer both an intuitive understanding and a foundation for developing forecasts using other ML algorithms with suitable customization.

The ` `` forecastML `` ` package offers direct forecasting that comprises three steps:

1.   Developing a series of horizon-specific forecast models using the chosen ML algorithm
2.   Assessing model generalization performance across a variety of holdout datasets
3.   Selecting those models that consistently performed the best at each forecast horizon and combining them to produce a single ensemble forecast

You will find a detailed example of an ML model used for forecasting next.

##  13.3.1 Application of ML in forecasting 

All ML models work better when a feature set related to the target variable needs prediction and is curated in the context of the prediction problem. Using ML models for forecasting is no exception. When using an ML algorithm for forecasting univariate time series, a feature set could be curated based on the lagged values of the series. The complexity arises when the resulting dataset needs to be prepared in a way that it is suitable for feeding into prediction algorithms to serve the purpose of forecasting as described in *Section 13.1* .

The ` `` forecastML `` ` package provides a flexible framework for building such ML pipelines tailored for time-series forecasting. All the features used for model training should be available in the forecast periods so that forecast values can be computed.     data_pred <- as.data.frame(
      predict(model, as.matrix(data)))
  }

  data_forecasts <- predict(
    model_results,
    prediction_function = list(predict_fn),
    data = feature_data )

  ```

##  13.3.2 Quantile regularized regression: A brief summary 

Quantile regularized regression combines quantile regression with Elastic Net regularization. It aims to estimate conditional quantiles of a response variable while controlling for model complexity using elastic net regularization. The core of regularized regression is explained in Zou et al. (2005) and Friedman et al. (2010), and implementation in the ` `` glmnet `` ` package (Friedman et al., 2024). Quantile prediction using regularized regression is implemented in the ` `` hqreg `` ` package (Yi, 2022). This kind of regression model is particularly useful as it can handle two common problems in the business domain:

-  **Multicollinearity** : Features are correlated among themselves and as a result estimated model parameters are volatile. At its extreme (in the case of perfect multicollinearity), standard regression model estimation will not work due to the rank deficiency in the design matrix.
-  **The** **<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_081.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_081.png</span>** **problem** : This refers to the small <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_082.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_082.png</span> (sample size) and large <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_083.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_083.png</span> (large number of parameters) problem, also known as the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_084.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_084.png</span> problem. In such a scenario, standard regression fails due to fewer data points compared to the number of parameters needed to be estimated for modeling.

In our example, the lag features were created from the observed series, year, and month columns, which have 93 rows (instances) with 118 columns. Correlations between these features often exceeding <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_085.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_085.png</span> , exhibiting multicollinearity, hence, the choice of quantile regularized regression as an ML model for forecasting, rather than other, more popular models, such as LightGBM.

Let’s see how regularized regression works. For simplicity, we will use the notion, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_086.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_086.png</span> for the outcome variable for the prediction and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_087.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_087.png</span> for all features. We will also avoid time subscript to simplify our exposition and use <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_088.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_088.png</span> as subscript to denote instance/observation as used in standard ML literature. In general, a quantile regression aims to estimate the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_089.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_089.png</span> -th quantile. The objective function for quantile regression is as follows:

#  13.4 Forecasting as a curve fitting: The Prophet method 

Prophet is an open-source forecasting tool developed by the Meta research team (Taylor et al., 2018). It is designed for time-series forecasting, particularly for data with strong seasonal patterns and historical data spanning several seasons. This model was introduced originally for forecasting daily data incorporating weekly and yearly seasonality, plus holiday effects and was later extended to cover more types of seasonal data. It works best with time series that have strong seasonality and several seasons of historical data. Some key features of the Prophet method are as follows:

-  **Additive model** : Prophet uses an additive model where non-linear trends are fit with yearly, weekly, and daily seasonality, plus holiday effects. You can get good forecasts with minimal effort.
-  **Ease of use** : It is designed to be user-friendly and fully automated, making it accessible even for those who are not experts in time series forecasting. It automatically detects and models yearly, weekly, and daily seasonality which is great for data with strong seasonal patterns.
-  **Robustness** : The model is robust to missing data and outliers, which means it can still provide accurate forecasts even if your data is not perfect. Prophet can model non-linear trends and can handle changes in trends, which is useful for data with complex patterns.
-  **Customization** : While it works well out of the box, it also allows for manual tuning by data professionals. You can add your own seasonalities, holidays, and other special events to improve the forecast.

It is worth realizing that Prophet is a curve-fitting exercise, which is inherently different from time-series models that explicitly account for the temporal dependence structure in the data.

##  13.4.1 Prophet forecasting 

Forecasting by the Prophet method can be performed using the ` `` fable.prophet `` ` package in R (O’Hara-Wild et al., 2024). For this example, we use monthly food retail turnover in Australia from the ` `` tsibbledata `` ` package. We use data from January 2000 to December 2015 for deriving forecasts until December 2018. To evaluate forecasting performance against the Prophet model, we also fit ETS and ARIMA models, as detailed in the following steps:

1.   At first, split the training and test sets for Prophet tuning and forecast evaluation using the following code:

    ```
    aus_food_retail <- tsibbledata::aus_retail |>
      filter(Industry == "Food retailing") |>
      index_by(Month) |>
      summarise(National_turnover = sum(Turnover, na.rm=T)) |>
      filter(Month > yearmonth("1999 Dec"))

    aus_food_retail_train <- aus_food_retail |>
      filter(Month < yearmonth("2016 Jan"))

    aus_food_retail_test <- aus_food_retail |>
      filter(Month >= yearmonth("2016 Jan"))
    ```

2.   The ` `` prophet() `` ` function from the ` `` fable.prophet `` ` package allows you to fit a Prophet model with some users’ input, such as the shape of growth (linear versus logistic), how seasonality is incorporated (additive versus multiplicative), and the number of terms in the partial Fourier sum when estimating seasonality, among other arguments. For our example series, we use a linear growth term and multiplicative seasonality with four Fourier series. The automated ARIMA and ETS models will be used on the same data. After fitting, use the estimated models to compute three-year forecasts. You can perform model fitting and compute forecasts using the following code:

    ```
    foodretail_fit <- aus_food_retail_train %>%
      model(
        arima = ARIMA(National_turnover),
        ets = ETS(National_turnover),
        prophet = prophet(
          National_turnover ~ growth("linear") +
          season(period = 12, order = 4, type = "multiplicative" )
          )
      )

    foodretail_fc <- foodretail_fit %>%
      forecast(h = "3 years")
    ```

#  13.5 Forecast ensemble 

So far, we’ve explored various approaches to forecasting. We started with simple naïve methods for data that has no apparent patterns. We then studied statistical models, such as ETS and ARIMA, for data with consistent discernible patterns. In this chapter, we expanded into more advanced computation-heavy techniques, such as DL methods, ML algorithms, and the Prophet method. Applying these methods across diverse datasets and comparing alternative approaches on the same data underscored an important insight: there is no single “best” forecasting model that universally applies to all situations.

For example, as shown in *Figure 13.3* , the NNETAR forecast may appear visually suitable for capturing cyclical patterns in the lynx dataset; however, it significantly deviates from the true values in short-term forecasting, both in point and interval estimates. Similarly, *Figure 13.6* illustrates that ML-based forecasts on the ` `` AirPassengers `` ` series, which exhibits strong trends and seasonality, also face challenges in producing accurate point and interval forecasts.

Differences in forecasts produced by various methods are well documented in the literature, with established explanations for why certain approaches may outperform others. Generally, these differences relate to how effectively each method leverages the available information within the data, as well as the richness of the historical data itself. Factors, such as the length of historical records and the strength of systematic components (e.g., trends or seasonality) can impact each model’s performance. As you gain expertise, you will be better equipped to choose methods that are conceptually well suited for specific forecasting scenarios and to fine-tune models for improved accuracy.

Although no single method consistently delivers the “best” forecast, you can enhance forecast accuracy by applying a simple yet powerful technique: averaging forecasts from multiple models. This may sound surprising, but combining forecasts from various approaches applied to the same time series often yields more accurate results. This ensemble technique is widely endorsed in applied forecasting. The averaging process can be implemented in various ways; here, we demonstrate it using a straightforward arithmetic mean, though more sophisticated weighted averages may also be employed to further refine forecasts.

We illustrate how to perform a forecast ensemble from multiple models on the ` `` AirPassengers `` ` series in the following steps:

1.   **Split data into training and test sets** : The ` `` AirPassengers `` ` series is organized into a ` `` tsibble `` ` object. For model training, we will use observations from January 1949 to December 1956. The remaining four years are reserved for testing:

    ```
    ap_tsb <- AirPassengers |> 
      as_tsibble() |>
      rename(month = index, passenger_count = value)
    ap_train <- ap_tsb |>
      filter(month < yearmonth("1957 Jan"))
    ap_test <- ap_tsb |>
      filter(month >= yearmonth("1957 Jan"))
    ```

2.   **Fit multiple forecasting models** : Five different forecasting models are fitted: seasonal naïve, TSLM, ETS, ARIMA, and Prophet. You can choose any number of forecasting models depending on the context of the forecasting problem at hand. As a general idea, the selection of the model should leverage the strengths and support the weaknesses of each other. Fit the models on the training data, and at the same time, you can make an ensemble of the fitted models by taking average of them, as shown in the following code:

    ```
    ap_models <- ap_train |>
      model(
        snaive = SNAIVE(passenger_count),
        tslm = TSLM(log(passenger_count) ~ trend() + season()),
        ets = ETS(log(passenger_count) ~ season("A")),
        arima = ARIMA(log(passenger_count)),
        prophet = prophet(
          passenger_count ~ growth("linear") +
          season(period = 12, order = 4, type = "multiplicative"))
        ) |>
      mutate(ensemble = (snaive + tslm + ets + arima + prophet) / 5 )

    ap_models
    ```

The ensemble forecast achieves the highest accuracy across all evaluation metrics, demonstrating a significant improvement over the second best forecast generated by the Prophet model.     It may be tempting to remove the poorest-performing forecast and rerun the ensemble. However, this is not recommended here, as visual inspection indicates that snaïve is the only approach to producing a low-biased forecast. Removing snaïve would result in all components of the ensemble being positively correlated, which would reduce the effectiveness of the ensemble. The benefit of ensemble methods arises from averaging components with lower correlations. For a more robust ensemble, consider excluding one of the positively correlated forecasting method, for example the Prophet method, shown in the following code:

    ```
    # fit model
    ap_mod_revised <- ap_train |>
      model(
        snaive = SNAIVE(passenger_count),
        tslm = TSLM(log(passenger_count) ~ trend() + season()),
        ets = ETS(log(passenger_count) ~ season("A")),
        arima = ARIMA(log(passenger_count))    ) |>
      mutate(ensemble = (snaive + tslm + ets + arima ) / 4)

    # forecast
    ap_fcst_revised <- ap_mod_revised |>
      forecast(h = "4 years") |>
      mutate(.model = str_c(.model, "_forecast"))

    # accuracy
    ap_accuracy_revised <- ap_fcst_revised |>
      accuracy(ap_tsb) |>
      select(.model, .type, RMSE, MAPE, MASE) |>
      arrange(MASE)

    ap_accuracy_revised

5.   **Generate prediction intervals** : The prediction intervals for forecast ensembles can be derived using simulation. Simulation works on model-based forecasts; this is another reason to exclude the Prophet method from our candidate models earlier while revising ensemble forecasts. We will generate <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_13_151.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_13_151.png</span> future sample paths for all forecasting which can be used to calculate distributions of the future sample path. The ` `` generate() `` ` function from the ` `` fabletools `` ` package performs the simulation while the ` `` set.seed() `` ` function ensures reproducibility. The ` `` dist_sample() `` ` function from the ` `` distributional `` ` package help summarizing the result from the simulation, which is finally stored as a ` `` tidy forecast `` ` object ( ` `` fable `` ` ), as shown in the following code:

    ```
    set.seed(98765); ap_sims <- ap_mod_revised |>
      fabletools::generate(h = "4 years", times = 1000) |>
      mutate(.model = str_c(.model, "_prediction_interval")) |>
      tibble() |>
      group_by(month, .model) |>
      summarise(
        dist = distributional::dist_sample(list(.sim))) |>
      ungroup() |>
      as_fable(index = month, key = .model,
               distribution = dist,
               response = "passenger_count")
    ```

#  Summary 

In this chapter, we explored advanced time-series forecasting techniques, including DL, ML, curve fitting (Prophet), and ensemble methods for forecasting. The examples used highlighted both the potential and limitations of these approaches.

DL is effective for various types of time-series data, provided there is sufficient historical data. A key advantage of DL is that it eliminates the need for manual feature engineering by the user.

ML approaches offer flexibility in choosing algorithms, but they require feature engineering, making them generally more time-intensive in terms of model training. Additionally, deriving prediction intervals can be more technically demanding.

DL, particularly through feed-forward neural networks, and curve fitting using Prophet are both well integrated within the tidy forecasting ecosystem. However, deriving prediction intervals using DL can be time-consuming due to its dependency on simulation.

When using ML for forecasting, the flexibility of algorithm selection must be balanced against the effort required for model tuning and code development.

It is important to note that no single method consistently outperforms others, including traditional statistical forecasting techniques. Forecasting ensemble can improve the accuracy of forecasting, and this can be achieved by simply averaging forecasts from different approaches on the same data.

This concludes our discussion on approaches useful for univariate time series forecasting. The concepts covered so far provide a solid foundation for analyzing multiple time series. The field of analyzing and forecasting multiple time series is becoming increasingly important in various business and economic applications. In the next chapter, we will explore some modern methods applied in these contexts.
