#  Anomaly Detection and Imputation 

Anomalies or outliers are events in time series that cause sudden deviation from observed systematic patterns for a usually short period. They can arise from various sources, such as equipment failures, extreme weather, or pandemics. Changepoints (discussed in *<a href="Chapter_16.xhtml#h1_346" class="chapref"> Chapter 16 </a>* ) make persistent shifts in the underlying data-generating process. Anomalies, on the other hand, are sudden deviations in a single observation or a sequence, after which the long-term systematic behavior remains unchanged.

In certain situations, replacing anomalies with plausible values is a better approach, particularly when they arise from known, uncontrollable reasons that are unlikely to repeat. Examples include a spike in customer complaints during a one-time database migration or a surge in friendship-band sales during a celebrity music tour.

Completely removing anomalies from the data is generally not advised, as they often contain valuable information about critical events or system failures. Discarding them can make decision-makers blind to genuine disruptions that might signify emerging risks or unique opportunities. Furthermore, simply excluding these observations leaves gaps in the series that make analysis challenging unless filled with prudent methods. Consequently, this chapter explores the dual challenges of detecting anomalies and imputing missing values to preserve the temporal structure of the data.

Anomaly detection in time series presents unique challenges compared to cross-sectional data because “normal” behavior evolves through trend-cycle and seasonal patterns. Effective methods need to distinguish between these expected baseline variations and genuine anomalies. This chapter covers various techniques to address these challenges, highlighting their respective advantages and limitations. Understanding the conceptual background of these methods will allow you to choose the right tools to make informed decisions and solve complex business problems.

#  17.1 Anomaly detection in time series 

Anomaly detection in time series focuses on identifying deviations from the expected behavior or defined “normality” in the context of the data collection process. The deviation may happen at a single time point or last over a sequence of time points. The identification of anomalies is essential for rigorous analysis. Their presence can distort statistical measures, compromise the extraction and modeling of trend and seasonality, and compromise forecasting performance. By detecting these outliers, we can safeguard the integrity of the analysis, reduce noise, and better understand the underlying patterns. Efficient detection transforms these potential obstacles into strategic advantages by allowing us to control for adverse effects and strengthen business decisions.

Time series anomalies generally fall into two major categories:

-  Point anomalies: Single and isolated observations that deviate from the expected pattern
-  Collective anomalies: A sequence of observations that together constitute anomalous behavior compared to the observed pattern

Effective time series anomaly detection must account for both temporal dependencies and complex underlying patterns. Defining any anomaly as a deviation from an expected baseline is inherently difficult, as the baseline itself can be distorted by the presence of anomalies. This challenge is magnified in situations where the context for what constitutes “normal” is derived solely from the data rather than established through domain-specific knowledge.

In this chapter, we cover two methods of anomaly detection:

-  Seasonal-trend decomposition with LOESS (STL) based detection: A decomposition approach that separates time series components to identify anomalies.
-  Isolation forest: An ensemble machine learning method adapted for time series anomaly detection

Each method offers distinct advantages for specific scenarios. You will learn about their foundations and practical considerations to effectively apply anomaly detection in real business problems. Let’s start with the STL-based method in the next section.

#  17.2 STL decomposition-based anomaly detection 

STL was covered in *<a href="Chapter_7.xhtml#h1_161" class="chapref"> Chapter 7 </a>* . It provides a robust framework for separating a time series into its components, such as trend, seasonality and remainder. This well-known decomposition technique can be used for time series anomaly detection, too. STL is free from underlying model assumptions, can handle any seasonality type, can capture evolving seasonal patterns over time, and, most importantly, is robust to anomalies. This method can identify anomalies in different components while respecting the underlying patterns extracted from the data.

-  It respects the natural structure of time series by separating components
-  It can detect anomalies in seasonal, trend, or remainder components separately

-  It requires the specification of the seasonality period in advance
-  It may struggle with multiple seasonal patterns at different frequencies

-  It may not capture complex inter-dependencies between components

#  17.3 Isolation forest-based anomaly detection 

Isolation forest (Liu et al., 2008) is an ensemble-based anomaly detection algorithm that is based on the principle that anomalies are often easier to isolate than non-anomalous observations. This method was originally designed for high-dimensional cross-sectional data; however, it can be adapted for time series by incorporating temporal features and contextual information in the algorithm.

This intuitively simple but clever algorithm creates a forest of random isolation trees. Each tree partitions the data randomly, and anomalies typically require fewer partitions to be isolated. The anomaly score is derived from the average path length required to isolate each observation.

For a time series <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_037.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_037.png</span> , the algorithm progresses as follows:

1.   Feature extraction: Create sequential feature vectors <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_038.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_038.png</span> to capture temporal context. Such a feature vector includes immediate prior <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_039.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_039.png</span> lagged values of the time series for a chosen width of the window <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_040.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_040.png</span> .

    In addition to sequential features, some other contextual features can be added, such as entropy (forecastability), lumpiness (variance of variances), non-linearity and so on.

2.   Build isolation trees: For each tree in the ensemble, do the following:
    1.   Select a random sub-sample or a bootstrap sample of the data.
    2.   Recursively partition the sub-sample by randomly selecting a feature and a split value.
    3.   Continue until all points are isolated or a specified depth is reached.

3.   Compute anomaly scores: For each time point <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_041.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_041.png</span> , do the following:

    1.   Pass <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_042.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_042.png</span> through each isolation tree

    2.   Compute the average path length <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_043.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_043.png</span> across all trees

    3.   Calculate the anomaly score using the following:

        <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_044.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_044.png</span>

    Here, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_045.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_045.png</span> is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree:

    <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_046.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_046.png</span>

    <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_047.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_047.png</span> is the harmonic number, approximated by <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_048.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_048.png</span> . This number helps normalize the anomaly score.

4.   Anomaly detection: Finally, flag observations with anomaly scores above a threshold (typically <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_049.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_049.png</span> to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_050.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_050.png</span> ) as anomalies.

In order to use an isolation forest on time series, we need to extract information from the time series and arrange it in a way that mimics a high-dimensional data scenario. The extracted information from the time series should encapsulate temporal and contextual information efficiently. In machine learning, such a data curation process is commonly referred to as feature engineering. It is important to understand that the distinction between a useful versus useless machine learning model can often be determined by the set of features curated in the feature engineering phase. Finding the right set of features is a common challenge in the machine learning process.

Let’s implement an isolation forest on the New York gasoline spot price data. The feature set includes lagged values along with the following features (detailed discussion available in *<a href="Chapter_8.xhtml#h1_184" class="chapref"> Chapter 8 </a>* ):

-  Crossing points: defined as the number of times a time series crosses the median line
-  Entropy: measures the “forecastability” of a time series, where low values indicate a high signal-to-noise ratio
- ` `` Holt_parameters `` ` : estimated smoothing parameter for level ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_051.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_051.png</span> ) and the smoothing parameter for trend ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_052.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_052.png</span> ) of Holt’s linear trend method
-  Lumpiness: the variance of the variances for tiled (non-overlapping) windows of a width of 10 for this data
-  Nonlinearity: takes large values when the series is nonlinear, and values around <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_053.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_053.png</span> when the series is linear
-  Stability is the variance of the means for tiled (non-overlapping) windows of width <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_054.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_054.png</span> for this data

We create a custom function to compute relevant features within a sliding window. The following code shows the custom ` `` create_features() `` ` function that takes observed series, ` `` ts_data `` ` , and the width of the sliding window, ` `` win_wid `` ` as arguments:

```
# Create features for time-series context
create_features <- function(ts_data, win_wid) {
  n <- length(ts_data)
  X <- matrix(NA, nrow = n - win_wid + 1,
                     ncol = win_wid + 7)
  for (i in win_wid:n) {
    win_data <- ts_data[(i - win_wid + 1):i]
    row_idx <- i - win_wid + 1
    # Window values
    X[row_idx, 1:win_wid] <- win_data
    holtPars <- holt_parameters(win_data)
    # Additional cross Mean, SD, Max and Min respectively
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 1] <- crossing_points(win_data)
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 2] <- entropy(win_data)
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 3] <- holtPars["alpha"]
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 4] <- holtPars["beta"]
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 5] <- lumpiness(win_data)
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 6] <- nonlinearity(win_data)
    X[row_idx, win_wid + 7] <- tsfeatures::stability(win_data)
  }
  # Assign column names to X
  colnames(X) <- c(
    paste0("lag_", (win_wid - 1):0),
    "xpoints", "entropy", "holt_1", "holt_2",
    "lumpiness",  "nonlinearity", "stability")
  return(as.data.frame(X))
}
```

```
gas_isof_anomaly <- gas_tbl |>
  mutate(
    isof_anomscore = c(
      rep(NA, win_wid-1),
      isof_pred$anomaly_score
    )
  ) |>

    anomaly_threshold = quantile(

    )
  ) |>

      is.na(isof_anomscore)  ~  NA,

        anomaly_threshold & !is.na(isof_anomscore)  ~ "Yes",
      .default = "No"
    )
  )
```

-  It does not make strong assumptions about data distribution
-  It is computationally efficient
-  It can detect both global and local anomalies
-  It performs well with limited training data
-  It can capture complex patterns when properly adapted for time series

-  It requires careful feature engineering to capture temporal dependencies
-  It may miss contextual anomalies if temporal features are poorly designed
-  Its performance is affected by the selection of hyperparameters (window size, number of trees, etc.)
-  It is not directly suited for detecting collective anomalies spanning multiple time points
-  It may struggle with extremely noisy time series or highly complex seasonal patterns

Isolation forest for time series anomaly detection is particularly valuable in scenarios where many features can be generated and collected to explain the underlying data-generating process.

It is important to handle those missing values with a proper method instead of just excluding them from further analysis and modeling time series. This leads to the topic of imputing missing values in time series in the next section.

#  17.4 Imputation in time series 

A critical question practitioners often face after detecting anomalies is how to address the unwanted influence of anomalous data points in subsequent analysis. Instead of removing anomalies, it is advisable to use robust methods to reduce the influence of anomalous observations on the analysis. Logarithmic transformation is one of the simplest but most effective ways, as it reduces the impact of anomalous observations. However, the problem might get complicated in a collaborative environment in which domain knowledge about data cleaning is not adequately shared with the analytics team. In fact, this is a common setup in modern business-to-business (B2B) analytics environments, especially where data is provided as a service. In this workflow, when anomalies are detected and removed before sharing for analysis, the important information about the anomalies might get missed, too. The subsequent analysis requires a rigorous scientific approach to impute these missing values for maintaining data integrity and performing accurate analysis.

With knowledge of forecasting, one might be inclined to use a forecasting model to fill those gaps (imputation), using the forecasting model for forecasting or backcasting (applying a forecast on a reversed time series). However, it is important to recognize that the time series imputation method needs to be model-agnostic, meaning imputation should not be done under any model assumption. If a model-based imputation is performed, subsequent analysis and models built on the imputed data will favor the model used for imputation. Keeping the caveat in mind, we adopt the linear interpolation method for imputation using a tidy workflow. The following subsections cover methodology and implementation.

##  17.4.1 Linear interpolation-based imputation of anomalies 

Linear interpolation is a method of predicting plausible values for anomalous data points using neighboring non-anomalous data points of a time series. The linear interpolation method can be used directly on non-seasonal series. For seasonal series, a robust STL decomposition is first computed. Then a linear interpolation is applied to the seasonally adjusted data, and the seasonal component is added back. The linear interpolation method is applicable to any non-linear series that can be transformed into a linear series.

The linear interpolation process (Hyndman, 2021) is a two-step process involving (i) detection of anomalies and (ii) linear imputation. The process begins by decomposing the time series into trend, seasonal, and remainder components using an additive model <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_064.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_064.png</span> . The seasonal component may contain several seasonal patterns corresponding to the seasonal periods in the data. For example, daily data might have a seasonality of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_065.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_065.png</span> and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_066.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_066.png</span> to allow for weekly and daily seasonality, which can be estimated iteratively using the ` `` feasts::STL() `` ` function.

From the resulting decomposition, the strength of seasonality is measured by calculating the quantity:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_067.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_067.png</span>

If the strength of seasonality exceeds the threshold of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_068.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_068.png</span> , a seasonally adjusted series is computed as <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_069.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_069.png</span> ; otherwise, set <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_070.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_070.png</span> .

The benefit of using a strength of seasonality threshold is to avoid over-fitting and not to forcefully estimate seasonality from noisy data when the underlying seasonality is too weak (or non-existent). Forcefully estimated seasonal components may mask anomalies by absorbing them.

In the next step, the trend component is re-estimated from <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_071.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_071.png</span> values. For non-seasonal time series (e.g., annual), this is a necessity, whereas for series with weak or non-existent seasonality, this is immensely useful, as trend estimation was not performed accurately in the initial decomposition due to uncertainty around the existence of a seasonal component. This re-estimation of the trend <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_072.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_072.png</span> is performed by applying a super smoother (Friedman, 1984) to the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_073.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_073.png</span> data, a non-parametric regression method designed to adaptively smooth data while minimizing bias and variance trade-offs.

The anomalies are identified from the estimated remainder series <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_074.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_074.png</span> , using the quantile-based technique described in the STL decomposition-based anomaly detection section for the choice <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_075.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_075.png</span> . If the distribution of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_076.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_076.png</span> , the probability of an observation being identified as an anomaly is approximately <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_077.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_077.png</span> , i.e, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_078.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_078.png</span> in <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_17_079.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_17_079.png</span> .

Anomalies identified by this method can be replaced by the linear interpolation method and repeated for identifying and imputing additional anomalies that might exist in the time series.

##  17.4.2 Tidy workflow for simultaneous anomaly detection and imputation 

The ` `` forecast `` ` package in R provides a streamlined functionality of anomaly detection using STL decomposition and its imputation using linear interpolation in time series data. Anomaly detection and its imputation simultaneously can be viewed as cleaning of time series, and its automatic implementation is provided by the ` `` forecast::tsclean() `` ` function. If the ` `` replace.missing `` ` argument set to TRUE, the function removes anomalies and impute those using linear interpolation. The function expects input data in a ` `` ts `` ` format.

We will perform the anomaly detection and linear imputation on the New York weekly gasoline spot price dataset. First, we compute the cleaned (imputed any anomalous observations) gas price and then create a binary indicator to pinpoint its time index (location) by comparing the imputed series with the original series.

The following code shows how to implement the linear interpolation to detect anomalies and replace it with imputation:

```
gas_tsclean <- gas_tbl |>
  mutate(
    clean_gas_price = tsclean(
      astsa::gas, replace.missing = TRUE)
  ) |>
  mutate(is_anomaly = case_when(
    gas_price != clean_gas_price ~ "Yes",
    .default = "No"))
```

#  17.5 Guidelines for selecting an anomaly detection method 

In this chapter, we explored two distinct anomaly detection methods, STL-based and isolation forest. The linear imputation method, which simultaneously detect and replace anomalies, also use an STL-decomposition and assessing strength of trend and seasonal components for anomaly identification, and once detected they are replaced via a linear imputation technique.

Applying these techniques to the same dataset revealed noticeable differences in performance, highlighting how method selection can significantly impact results. The implementation of each approach depends on the careful selection of certain parameters, which often involves subjective judgment. However, these parameters alone do not encompass all the factors necessary for choosing a method in practice. When selecting an appropriate anomaly detection method, the following considerations should also be taken into account:

-  Data characteristics
  -  Seasonality: STL-based methods excel for time series with strong seasonal patterns, while isolation forest may require additional pre-processing for seasonal data
  -  Trend: All methods can handle trends, but STL explicitly models the trend component, making it more interpretable
  -  Stationarity: Both STL and the adapted isolation forest can handle non-stationary data
  -  Data volume: Isolation forest scales well to large datasets, while STL decomposition may become computationally intensive for very long time series with complex seasonal patterns.
-  Anomaly types
  -  Point anomalies: All methods are effective at detecting individual outliers, with STL generally being the simplest option
  -  Collective anomalies: Isolation forest with appropriate window features can detect patterns of anomalies, while standard STL methods may struggle with this task
-  Implementation considerations
  -  Computational resources: STL-based methods are typically the least computationally intensive, followed by isolation forest
  -  Parameter tuning: All methods require parameter selection, with isolation forest often requiring the most domain expertise for effective calibration
  -  Interpretability: STL provides the most interpretable results by separating anomalies by component, while isolation forest offers the least transparent decision mechanism
  -  Online detection: Neither STL nor standard isolation forest can be used for online detection, but they are good for batch processing

In addition, the summarized criteria presented in *Table 17.1* will help you decide on a method.

|  **Criteria** |  **Selected method for treating anomalies in time series data** |
|----|----|
|  The time series is relatively well-behaved, and computational efficiency is the prime concern |  STL-based method, especially when domain expertise is not available for parameter tuning in an isolation forest |
|  The time series exhibits strong seasonal patterns |  STL-based detection to separate and analyze anomalies in different components |
|  The time series is complex with multiple interacting features or robust performance is expected with minimal assumptions |  Isolation forest with carefully engineered temporal features |
|  Multiple types of anomalies need to be detected, or uncertainty quantification is important |  Consider combining methods or using ensemble approaches that leverage the strengths of each method |
