#  9.1 Comparison of time series preprocessing techniques 

Time series decomposition techniques (e.g., STL) separate a time series into its components: trend, seasonal and random variation. Decomposition techniques do not remove any components, rather preserve them after separation. Time series smoothing removes random noise from the data to reveal underlying patterns. Time series filtering is a frequency-domain or time-domain operation that selectively attenuates or eliminates specific frequency components while preserving others.

All three techniques aim to extract meaningful information from a time series by reducing unmeasurable complexity (noise). They may seem to produce similar outcomes, but actually they have specific objectives, purposes and applications in time series analysis. *Table 9.1* summarizes some key aspects of these techniques.

|  **Aspect** |  **Decomposition** |  **Smoothing** |  **Filtering** |
|----|----|----|----|
|  Primary goal |  Separate time series into components (trend, seasonal, cyclical, irregular) |  Reduce noise to reveal underlying patterns/trends |  Remove or isolate specific frequency components |
|  Purpose |  Diagnostic |  Descriptive |  Prescriptive about what to remove |
|  Output |  Multiple component series (T, S, C, R) |  Single smoothed series |  Single filtered series or specific component |
|  Approach |  Structural breakdown into additive or multiplicative components |  Local averaging |  Frequency-selective transformation |
|  Common methods |  Classical decomposition, STL, X-12-ARIMA |  Weighted Moving Average, exponential smoothing, LOESS, kernel smoothing |  Linear filtering with Moving Average, Hodrick-Prescott filter, Kalman filter |
|  Frequency selectivity |  Component-based (separates by temporal pattern type) |  Non-selective (diminish effect of all high frequencies) |  Highly selective (targets specific frequency bands) |
|  Reversibility |  Yes, components can be recombined |  No, information loss from averaging |  Partial, depends on filter type |
|  Preserved information |  All identifiable structural components separately |  Overall shape with reduced variability |  Specified frequency components only |
|  Causality |  Non-causal; retrospective or post-processing only, need entire time series |  Usually non-causal; a symmetric operation, requires both past (before) and future (after) data points; some methods are applicable in real-time scenario |  Can be causal (uses only past and present data for real time application), or non-causal (uses future data points for symmetric filters) |
|  Use case |  Understanding structure; pre-processing for forecasting |  Quality control; fast trend visualization; noise reduction for exploratory analysis |  Signal processing; removing known interference; cycle extraction |
|  Example |  Analyzing     the impact of seasons on sale |  Annual sales review, identifying long-term climate trends, analyzing past marketing campaign |  High-frequency trading algorithms, extract business cycle from GDP, biomedical signal processing (ECG analysis), image and motion processing |

Table 9.1: Comparison of time series preprocessing techniques

In a time series context, the terms smoothing and filtering are often used interchangeably. While filter broadly refers, in the frequency domain, to analyzing signal-to-noise ratio, smoothing refers only to reducing noise from the data to reveal underlying trends. Methods based on moving average are used for both filtering and smoothing. Advanced filtering techniques, such as the Kalman filter, are commonly used to filter out random noise from complex, often non-linear time series.

#  9.2 Exponentially weighted moving average method (EWMA) 

The moving average smoothing discussed in *Section 7.6.1* and *7.6.2* , showed how to reduce random noise by taking averages over neighboring observations. In that scenario, all observations were given equal weight, irrespective of how close or far they are from the observation being smoothed. The moving average method has a few limitations, such as loss of information at the start and end of a series, and the optimal number of observations to average is more of a subjective choice.

Moving average requires both past ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_001.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_001.png</span> ,.., <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_002.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_002.png</span> ) and future ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_003.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_003.png</span> ,…, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_004.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_004.png</span> ) data points relative to the time index, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_005.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_005.png</span> . This information is not always available—especially if you would like to analyze the underlying pattern by reducing noise as soon as a period has passed. The **exponentially weighted moving average** ( **EWMA** ) method is applicable in such a scenario.

EWMA effectively removes random noise from a time series and smooths out fluctuations to reveal patterns. EWMA assigns less weight to past values, with more recent values receiving higher weights. #  9.3 Time series filtering 

In general, the word filter means to pass a liquid, gas, light, or sound through a device to remove unwanted material. In the time series context, the filtering involves passing the observed series through a mathematical operation to remove or emphasize particular components. Time series filters are commonly used to isolate patterns at a specific frequency.

Time series filtering is applied in the **frequency domain** , and the prime objective is to isolate patterns that occur at various frequency lengths. For example, separating trends that occur over a 5-10 year period from short-frequency cyclical (over 2-4 years). Usually, time series filtering is mainly used for explaining and analyzing observed phenomena. Even though some advanced filtering techniques, such as the Kalman filter, are also used in forecasting models.

Time series smoothing concentrates on removing short-term fluctuations, also known as a **finite impulse response** ( **FIR** ) filter. On the other hand, time series filtering removes long-term fluctuations, technically an infinite number of observations and known as **infinite impulse response** ( **IIR** ) filter.

Time series filtering is a sophisticated mathematical operation in which temporal dependencies are explicitly modeled with parameters.

##  9.3.1 The Nyquist frequency and sampling considerations 

In time series analysis, data is collected at regular intervals (i.e., daily, monthly, or quarterly). These intervals are known as the **sampling frequency** . In frequency domain analysis, the information that can be extracted from a time series is governed by the **Nyquist frequency** , which is half the sampling rate,

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_023.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_023.png</span>

For any discrete time series, if you wish to see any variation/pattern at a particular frequency cycle, then your data collection (sampling) frequency must be at least twice that cycle.

For example, daily time series cannot capture the intra-day cycle; at least two days of observations are needed to detect such a cycle. An annual series ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_024.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_024.png</span> ) could not provide enough information if there are quarterly patterns ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_025.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_025.png</span> ) in the data. The annual series has a slow sampling rate, so the quarterly trend would be completely missed. You must sample at a higher frequency (e.g., monthly with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_026.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_026.png</span> ) to detect any pattern that occurs at a lower frequency ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_027.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_027.png</span> ).

This requirement eliminates **aliasing** , a well-known concern in signal processing. Aliasing is the distortion in the signals that occurs when a signal is sampled at a rate lower than twice its highest frequency component.

To ensure accurate representation, it is essential to choose a sampling frequency that is sufficiently high to capture the periodic behavior present in the business cycles or economic indicators being studied. These behaviors are defined by their frequency or cycle length:

-  Lower frequencies typically refer to longer-term movements, such as trends or 2-5 year business cycles
-  Higher frequencies refer to shorter, more rapid movements, such as annual cycles found in quarterly and monthly data, which we call as seasonality

The time series filtering techniques filter out a specific range of frequencies within a time series while attenuating others. They can be characterized as follows:

-  **Low-pass filter** : A filter that dampens higher frequencies in the data and allows only lower frequencies to pass through. Ideal for separate trends.
-  **High-pass filter** : A filter that dampens low frequencies and allows high frequencies to pass, which is used to separate short-and-long term business cycles.
-  **Band-pass filter** : A filter that passes only the frequencies within a certain frequency band.

Most time series filters are based on the concept of moving averages: smooth out the white noise terms as much as possible. A variety of time series filtering techniques can be found in the literature with their own pros and cons. We include the following three that have wide application in time series analysis, especially business and economic time series contexts:

-  Linear filtering with moving average: A finite impulse response filter that separates the underlying pattern by smoothing out past noise
-  Hodrick-Prescott filter: A finite approximation of an infinite impulse response filter that separates trends from cycles and random noise
-  Kalman filter: A general and versatile filter that considers observed series as a system of the true underlying data-generating process and could handle noise arising from multiple sources.

Let’s start with linear filtering with moving average in the next section.

#  9.5 The Hodrick-Prescott filter 

Among the time series components, trends and cycles are often entangled with each other. You need longer periods to observe the true occurrence of these two components. Business cycles typically operate in 2-8 year periods, which create fluctuations around the long-term trend. On the other hand, when trends are very strong, they may dominate cyclical patterns, making it harder to detect without specific decomposition techniques.

The Hodrick-Prescott (HP) filter (Hodrick et al., 1997) separates trends and cycles from an observed series by applying the concept of frequency domain analysis. It is a high-pass filter, which means it preserves high-frequency components by damping low-frequency movement. The HP filter accomplishes this by minimizing the fluctuations from the estimated trend.

So far, we have assumed that a time series is obtained from a single observable data generating process (DGP). What if the underlying DGP is actually unobservable; what we could measure is observations generated from a state of a dynamic system mixed with a series of noise. The Kalman filter was developed to handle such situation. It is a recursive algorithm for estimating the internal state of a linear dynamic system from a series of noisy measurements. It is also used as a filtering technique and can be used for forecasting.

#  9.6 The Kalman filter 

In supply chain operations, the true customer demand for a product is “hidden” or “latent” state, because it cannot be measured accurately in real time. It is impossible to measure the exact demand until the sales are finalized over a period. On the other hand, the historical sales are measurable, but could be misleading to accurately predict product demand, as sales are often mixed with noise due to random fluctuations, promotional spikes, stock runouts and so on. In this scenario, what we observed (realized sales) is a combination of two different processes, unobservable demand and observed sales. Both simultaneously change over time and are mixed with their own noise.

The Kalman filter is a method for making accurate estimates about hidden demand using past sales. First, it predicts where the demand is likely to be based on the previous sales figures (sales forecasting). Then, in the second step, it combines this forecast with the actual new measurement (realized sales data) using a weighted average (the Kalman gain) to estimate the true underlying demand. By filtering out short-term noise from both the demand and sales systems, the Kalman filter provides a clear, continuously updated estimate of genuine market needs, which is essential for making better inventory and product decisions.

Kalman ( ` `` 1960 `` ` ) proposed this filter to estimate the actual system at discrete time steps using information hidden behind the noise. To be specific, the algorithm was first used to track the position, velocity, and orientation of the Apollo spacecraft guided by the **Apollo Guidance Computer** ( **AGC** ) in the presence of noise and uncertainty from the sensors. Kalman et al. ( ` `` 1961 `` ` ) extends the algorithm to be applicable to the state space framework of a linear model in a continuous time step.

So, what is the state space framework of a time series? The state space framework is a general and versatile representation of time series data. It represents the underlying dynamics of a system using a set of state variables and a set of measurement equations. The state variables represent the unobserved states of the system, while the measurement equations relate to the state variables of the observed data. Time series components, such as level, trend, and seasonality, can all be considered as state variables.

The state variable vector <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_09_099.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_09_099.png</span> could be one-dimensional or multi-dimensional, depending on the unobserved components or states (level, trend, seasonal) that are associated with the underlying DGP. The number of parameters in the Kalman filter varies depending on how many states you have in your system; hence, this technique incorporates an optimization routine that can be extended based on the dynamics of the system.

Kalman filtering is a vast topic. There is an array of literature that covers different notations and representations of the Kalman filter, considering one to multiple states, estimation methods under different noise characteristics (Gaussian and non-Gaussian), how to tune parameters, how to choose initial state and covariance matrices, and their impact on convergence, to name a few. No wonder there are multiple R packages that offer Kalman filtering in various settings. See Tusell (2011) for a review of R packages on the Kalman filter.

```
y <- Nile

## Set constant parameters:
dt <- ct <- matrix(0)
Zt <- Tt <- matrix(1)
a0 <- y[1] # Estimation of the first year flow
P0 <- matrix(100) # Variance of 'a0'

## Estimate parameters:
fit_fkf <- optim(
  c(HHt = var(y, na.rm = TRUE) * .5,
    GGt = var(y, na.rm = TRUE) * .5),
  fn = function(par, ...)
    -fkf(HHt = matrix(par[1]),
         GGt = matrix(par[2]), ...)$logLik,
  yt = rbind(y),
  a0 = a0, P0 = P0,
  dt = dt, ct = ct,
  Zt = Zt, Tt = Tt
)

## Filter Nile data with estimated parameters:
fkf_obj <- fkf(
  a0, P0, dt, ct, Tt, Zt,
  HHt = matrix(fit_fkf$par[1]),
  GGt = matrix(fit_fkf$par[2]),
  yt = rbind(y)
)
```

A lot of the time, in a business context, we cannot measure the “actual states”; rather, what we observe is the output of the actual dynamics mixed with various measurement noise.
