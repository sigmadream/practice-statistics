#  15.1 What is causal inference? 

Causal inference is the process of determining whether a cause-and-effect relationship exists between variables and quantifying the strength of that relationship. It goes beyond simply identifying correlation to answer fundamental questions: what causes what, and by how much? This process is central to understanding the underlying mechanisms that drive changes in outcomes, allowing decision-makers to pinpoint the true impact of one variable on another. Unlike correlation analysis, causal inference emphasizes direction and the pathways through which variables influence each other.

Correlations can offer insights into bivariate relationships, but they are prone to misinterpretation and may lead to flawed decisions if incorrect causality is assumed. For example, while a correlation analysis might reveal that increased advertising expenditure correlates with higher sales, such an association does not necessarily mean that the increased expenditure caused the sales to rise. Both variables could be influenced by a third factor, such as seasonal demand or economic conditions, commonly termed a *confounder* . Causal inference seeks to disentangle these complexities, determining whether advertising expenditure is the true driver of sales or merely coincidental. Acting solely on correlation-led analysis could result in investing resources in initiatives that do not deliver the desired outcomes or missing opportunities to address the actual drivers of change. Causation, on the other hand, provides the actionable insights necessary for strategic planning and operational execution.

Causal inference relies on a combination of theoretical principles, experimental designs, and statistical tools to isolate the causal effect of one variable on another. It is grounded in frameworks such as counterfactual reasoning, where the impact of a treatment or intervention is measured against what would have happened in its absence. Experimental methods are often considered the gold standard for causal inference because they effectively eliminate confounding factors in counterfactual reasoning through random assignment of treatment. However, in many real-world scenarios, treatment assignment is impractical or unethical, thus making the experiment unfeasible, and in such scenarios, observational data and advanced statistical techniques such as *matching, instrumental variables methods* , and *time series models* are employed to estimate the causal effect.

In the terminology of causal inference, a “treatment” refers to an intervention or initiative, such as a business decision or policy change, while an “outcome” or “response” denotes the measurable result or effect of that intervention. For businesses, this framework provides a systematic approach to evaluate the true impact of their actions, whether it involves launching a new product, implementing a marketing campaign, or optimizing internal processes.

Building upon a fundamental understanding of the core concepts and essential terminologies associated with causal inference, the subsequent section will formally introduce the foundational tools and methodologies necessary for conducting causal analysis.

#  15.2 Causal inference with experimentation 

Over the centuries, several scientific methods have been developed to identify and estimate causal relationships, each tailored to specific scenarios and data contexts. The reliability of evidence derived from these methods varies, giving rise to the concept of a hierarchy of evidence. This concept has been extensively discussed and refined in fields, such as clinical and public health research (Evans, 2003). More recently, it has begun to play a pivotal role in business decision-making processes, as organizations recognize the need for robust and reliable insights to guide their strategies.

At the top of this hierarchy lies scientific experimentation, which provides the most reliable form of evidence for causal inference. Foundational texts, such as Montgomery (2017), offer a detailed exploration of experimental designs, including those specifically crafted to uncover cause-and-effect relationships. Among these, the most fundamental and powerful design is the randomized controlled trial (RCT). In an RCT, subjects are randomly assigned to either a treatment group or a control group, ensuring that, on average, the two groups are comparable in all respects except for the treatment being studied. This randomization process minimizes the influence of confounding variables (external factors that could affect both the treatment and the outcome), thereby enabling a direct attribution of observed differences to the treatment itself.

RCTs have gained significant traction in the technology industry in recent times and are often referred to as experiments or A/B testing. At Microsoft, A/B testing was employed to substantiate claims that the choice of color for presenting search results in the Bing search engine could generate tens of millions in annual revenue (Kohavi et al., 2017). For businesses with millions or billions of users, A/B testing on the individual response serves as a reliable method for making objective decisions.

However, conducting proper A/B testing in a business scenario faces practical challenges that are highlighted in the following sub-section.

##  15.2.1 Practical challenges of conducting experiments 

While experimental tools such as A/B testing are often regarded as the gold standard for establishing causal relationships, there are many scenarios where conducting such experiments is either impractical or unfeasible. One of the common challenges is obtaining the required sample size, particularly when businesses have a limited number of customers to study. This issue is further compounded when the effect size of the intervention is small. For instance, detecting a <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_001.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_001.png</span> increase in click-through rate (CTR) demands a significantly larger sample size compared to detecting a <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_002.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_002.png</span> increase.

The challenge becomes even greater when businesses aim for a higher degree of confidence in their results. Achieving a higher level of significance of a statistical test reduces the likelihood of false positive results but necessitates a larger sample size. Similarly, when decision-makers require greater statistical power ensuring a lower likelihood of false negatives, then the sample size requirement grows further.

Variability in individual responses to an intervention also complicates matters, as higher variance inflates the sample size needed to achieve reliable results. While advanced experimental designs can sometimes address these issues by reducing the sample size and stabilizing variance, implementing such strategies requires deep insights into the population under study. In practice, many businesses do not have such an advantage. Consequently, for medium-sized or even some large businesses, conducting experiments might not be practical due to these resource constraints.

In addition to the limitations posed by sample size, not all experiments in a business setting involve individual customers or users. Often, experiments need to be designed around larger units, such as geographic regions or retail outlets. The number of such units is typically far smaller than the number of individual users, making the sample size requirement unrealistic.

Beyond these, practical constraints in several scenarios where A/B testing is inherently unsuitable are as follows (but not limited to these):

-  **Post implementation analysis** : When an intervention has already been implemented, it becomes impossible to design a randomized experiment retrospectively.
-  **Resistance to randomization** : Stakeholders or participants may resist random assignment due to concerns about the potential adverse effects of the intervention on key business metrics.
-  **Ethical considerations** : Testing certain interventions, such as subsidies or aids targeted at sub-populations, may raise equity or ethical concerns, especially if withholding treatment is perceived as unfair.
-  **Long-term follow-up** : Experiments requiring prolonged monitoring, such as studying the impact of an intervention on customer complaints over several months may be impractical due to resource or time constraints.
-  **Low incidence events** : Measuring the effect of interventions on rare but critical events, such as system failures or severe customer complaints, is challenging due to the scarcity of such events.

Due to these practical limitations, businesses often need to look for analytical tools beyond A/B testing to assess the impact of their interventions. These constraints also highlight the importance of alternative approaches, such as observational data analysis, which can provide valuable causal insights when experimentation is not feasible. We discuss the method in the following section.

#  15.3 Causal inference with observational data 

Observational studies provide a practical and reliable means of investigating causal relationships in real-world contexts where experiments are not feasible. Unlike experimental studies, observational research involves the collection of data on subjects without applying any interventions or manipulating variables in a randomized way. Instead, subjects are observed for outcomes in their natural environments, allowing researchers to analyze outcomes without directly influencing them. Depending on their design and objectives, observational studies can be categorized as either *descriptive* (focusing on characterizing phenomena) or *analytical* (aimed at exploring relationships between variables).

Though descriptive studies provide powerful insights to uncover hidden patterns, it is not the focus of this chapter. We’ll focus on analytical studies leveraging statistical modeling and inference. Two common types of analytical observational studies are as follows:

-  **Cohort studies** : These studies track a group of subjects over time to examine the relationship between exposure to specific factors and the development of an outcome. Cohort studies may be prospective, observing events as they unfold, or retrospective, analyzing past data to uncover patterns.
-  **Case-control studies** : These studies compare subjects who have a particular outcome (cases) with those who do not (controls) to identify factors that may have contributed to the outcome. Case-control studies can also capture how the outcome evolves over time.

The main focus of this book is on the application of time-series-based methodologies in a business context. Hence, we limit the discussions of cohort studies using time-series-based methodologies.

Experimental data typically focuses on unit-level responses, such as whether an individual responds positively to a treatment or not. On the other hand, observational studies can analyze both unit-level responses and macro-level outcomes. For instance, a macro-level response might measure the total number of customers contacting a mobile phone service provider about connection problems due to a cellular network fault in different geographic regions.

Establishing causality in observational studies, however, is inherently more challenging than in experimental designs due to the lack of direct control over the treatment variable. Observational studies are particularly susceptible to confounding factors—variables that influence both the independent and dependent variables, potentially biasing the results. To address these challenges, observational studies often adopt strategies to mimic experimental designs, such as quasi-experiments (e.g., propensity score matching) for observable confounders, or rely on sophisticated statistical methods to control for unobservable confounders (e.g., Heckman correction in regression) during the analysis. These approaches are extensively discussed in existing literature and are integral to ensuring valid inferences.

We will not cover discussions on the broader methodologies for establishing causality from observational data. Instead, our goal is to demonstrate how observational study methods can be utilized in scenarios where a known cause-and-effect mechanism already exists, and the objective is to estimate the impact of the causal factor on the response.

The reliability of causal effect estimates depends on the adequacy of the analytical tools employed. By applying advanced time series methods to observational data, we aim to highlight the improved robustness of these tools, ensuring that the resulting quantitative evidence is actionable, and can reliably estimate the effects of interventions or treatments. As established in earlier chapters, many time series models are fundamentally rooted in regression techniques. Therefore, prior to leveraging time series regression for causal impact estimation, it is essential to formally understand the inherent connection between hypothesis testing and regression analysis, which will be the focus of the next section.

#  15.4 Equivalence of hypothesis tests and regression 

Over the centuries, a wide array of statistical methods and models have been developed in both academic and applied research to analyze data derived from experiments and observational studies. For data collected from scientific experiments, statistical hypothesis testing has been the cornerstone of analysis. Comparing group means, for example, often relies on methods such as t-tests, z-tests, and chi-squared tests. When the comparison involves multiple groups, researchers frequently turn to F-tests or Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). These methods are deeply rooted in specific assumptions: the samples must be random, independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.); and they should exhibit approximately normal distributions. The validity of any conclusions drawn relies on the fulfillment of these fundamental assumptions.

In contrast, regression analysis offers an alternative approach, particularly useful for observational studies. The Time Series Linear Models (TSLM) presented in <a href="Chapter_10.xhtml#h1_216" class="chapref"> Chapter 10 </a> , Section 10.7, and advanced time series regression techniques, such as ARIMAX in <a href="Chapter_12.xhtml#h1_254" class="chapref"> Chapter 12 </a> , Section 12.6, provide the flexibility to incorporate multiple factors or predictors into the analysis, enabling a more nuanced understanding of the relationships within the data. However, the validity of regression-based inference also depends on satisfying critical assumptions: the relationship between predictors and the outcome must be linear, predictors must be independent of one another, residuals should have a mean of zero with constant variance, and residuals must remain independent of both the predictors and each other.

Despite their distinct origins and applications, hypothesis testing and regression analysis share a fundamental equivalence when the required assumptions for analysis are met. Indeed, many common statistical tests, such as t-tests and ANOVA, can be viewed as specific cases of inference about parameters using linear regression models. This equivalence underscores the power of regression not only for prediction but also for causal inference, provided that confounding factors are adequately controlled.

To illustrate this inter-connectedness, we turn to a classic dataset, the randomized experiment on sleep conducted by Student, the pseudonym of William S. Gosset, who developed the t-distribution. The dataset measures the effect of two soporific drugs on <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_003.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_003.png</span> patients, recording the increase in hours of sleep. The paired design of this randomized controlled trial (RCT) ensures that confounders are minimized, as each individual receives both treatments, allowing for direct comparisons. The ` `` Student `` ` dataset, available in the ` `` datasets `` ` package in R, provides an excellent example for exploring the connections between traditional hypothesis testing and regression analysis.

##  15.4.1 Paired sample 

Consider a scenario where two treatments are compared in terms of their effect on sleep as a paired design. To begin, calculate the mean and variance for two groups using the following code:

```
slpd <- sleep |>
  tibble() |>
  clean_names() |>
  mutate(
    group = case_when(
      group == 1 ~ "Trt_1",
      .default = "Trt_2"
    ) |>
    factor() |>
    fct_relevel(c("Trt_1", "Trt_2"))
  )

sleep_summary <- slpd |>
  group_by(group) |>
  summarise(
    mean = mean(extra),
    variance = var(extra)
  )

sleep_summary
```

The summary statistics for treatment groups are printed in the following output:

```
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  group  mean variance
  <fct> <dbl>    <dbl>
1 Trt_1  0.75     3.20
2 Trt_2  2.33     4.01
```

The summary suggests, on average, treatment <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_004.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_004.png</span> resulted in an additional <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_005.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_005.png</span> hours of sleep, while treatment <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_006.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_006.png</span> led to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_007.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_007.png</span> extra hours of sleep. This difference indicates that treatment <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_008.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_008.png</span> resulted in <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_009.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_009.png</span> fewer hours of extra sleep compared to treatment <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_010.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_010.png</span> . But how consistent will this difference be if you perform the same study with other response cohorts? This is the exact reason we conduct hypothesis testing—to estimate confidence and measure the consistency of obtaining repetitive results observed in a study.

Now, perform a paired t-test using the ` `` t.te `` ` ` `` s `` ` ` `` t() `` ` function with the option ` `` paired = TRUE `` ` . The results will provide p-value and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_011.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_011.png</span> confidence intervals that can be used to evaluate the difference between treatments. ```
pair_ttest <- t.test(
  extra ~ group, data = slpd,
  paired = TRUE, conf.level = 0.95)

pair_ttest_tidy <- tidy(pair_ttest) |>
  select(method, alternative, estimate, p.value,
         conf.low, conf.high)

pair_ttest_tidy
```

The following tidied output shows the results from the paired t-test:

```

The paired t-test shows p-value <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_012.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_012.png</span> , indicating a statistically significant difference between the two treatments at a <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_013.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_013.png</span> significance level. To interpret the p-value, recall that it represents the probability of observing a difference as extreme as <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_014.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_014.png</span> under the null hypothesis that assumes no difference between the means. While a paired t-test can technically be performed without explicitly computing the difference between measurements obtained under alternative treatments, the underlying statistical calculation inherently operates on difference. Difference in measurement is also needed to demonstrate the use of a linear model on this data. To validate the consistency between approaches, we will first run a one-sample t-test on the calculated difference data to ensure that the results align with those derived from the paired t-test applied earlier. The following code computes the difference between two treatments and then performs a one-sample t-test:

```
slpdiff <- slpd |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = group,
              values_from = extra) |>
  mutate(diff_group =  Trt_1 - Trt_2)

onesamp_diff_ttest <- t.test(
  slpdiff$diff_group,
  conf.level = 0.95)

onesamp_diff_ttest_tidy <- tidy(onesamp_diff_ttest) |>
  select(
    method, alternative, estimate,
    p.value, conf.low, conf.high)

onesamp_diff_ttest_tidy
```

The following output confirms that the one-sample t-test on the difference between two treatments provides results identical to the results obtained from the paired t-test:

To achieve the same results using linear regression, we fit a linear model on the ` `` diff_group `` ` column with no independent variable. Coefficient estimates from this model will essentially make inferences about the mean of ` `` diff_group `` ` (difference in treatment responses). You can fit the linear model using the ` `` lm() `` ` function, tidy the model object, and compute confidence intervals using the ` `` confint() `` ` function as shown in the following code:

##  15.4.2 Two independent samples 

Now, let us shift the focus to the scenario of two independent response groups. Suppose the same data is reinterpreted as two independent samples rather than paired. We conduct a two-sample t-test, assuming equal variance. To maintain simplicity, we have opted not to perform formal statistical testing for the equality of variance. Instead, we rely on the established guidance that a two-sample t-test assuming equal variance remains robust even when sample variances differ by as much as a fourfold factor. In our data, the ratio of large and small sample variances, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_020.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_020.png</span> , is indeed less than <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_021.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_021.png</span> . Hence, the equal variance assumption holds.

To perform a two-independent-samples t-test, pass the formula specification ` `` extra ~ group `` ` into the ` `` t.test() `` ` function with the option ` `` var.equal = TRUE `` ` . The following code shows you how to perform the test and organize the results:

```
twosamp_ttest <- t.test(extra ~ group, data = slpd,
                        var.equal = TRUE, conf.level = 0.95)

twosamp_test_tidy <- tidy(twosamp_ttest) |>
  select(method, alternative, estimate,
         p.value, conf.low, conf.high)

twosamp_test_tidy
```

The following results are observed:

```
# A tibble: 1 × 6
  method          alternative estimate p.value conf.low conf.high
  <chr>           <chr>          <dbl>   <dbl>    <dbl>     <dbl>
1 Two Sample t-t… two.sided      -1.58  0.0792    -3.36     0.204
```

The p-value <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_022.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_022.png</span> is higher than <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_023.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_023.png</span> , and showed no statistically significant difference between the two treatments at a <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_024.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_024.png</span> level of significance. The confidence interval ranges from <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_025.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_025.png</span> to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_026.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_026.png</span> , which includes the null value zero, reflecting greater uncertainty in the estimated difference. This discrepancy arises because the standard error is inflated under the assumption of independence, as opposed to the paired design. This distinction highlights the importance of adopting an alternative experimental design in impact measurement; a paired-sample design has better control for the confounders than a two-independent-samples design.

So far, we have shown the equivalence of hypothesis testing and regression methods in on-sample and two-sample scenarios. Next, we will check the equivalence in a more general scenario, such as having three or more response groups.

While a t-test evaluates intervention on a single group or exactly two groups, the F-test serves as a general test that can assess multiple treatments or factors simultaneously to detect any statistically significant differences. The F-test is primarily employed in Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to compare means across three or more treatments, and in regression to assess if a complex model fits the data significantly better than a simpler, intercept-only model.

We check the equivalence by fitting a linear model, ` `` extra ~ 1 + group `` ` , and perform an F-test as part of a two-sample ANOVA analysis. Like previous examples, the ` `` Trt_2 `` ` is considered as the reference treatment. Let’s perform the ANOVA analysis using the ` `` aov() `` ` function and extract results, as shown in the following code:

```
twosamp_anova <- aov(
  extra ~ 1 + group,
  data = slpd |> 
    mutate(
      group = relevel(group,ref = "Trt_2")))

tidy(twosamp_anova)
```

The results presented in the following output show that the p-value from the F-test is identical to what was obtained from the two-sample t-test earlier:

```
# A tibble: 2 × 6
  term         df sumsq meansq statistic p.value
  <chr>     <dbl> <dbl>  <dbl>     <dbl>   <dbl>
1 group         1  12.5  12.5       3.46  0.0792
2 Residuals    18  64.9   3.60     NA    NA  
```

When two or more treatments are compared, a pairwise comparison, such as Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) can be applied to identify which two treatments have different means. Even though there are only two treatments in the ` `` sleep `` ` data, we can still apply the HSD for illustration to identify which treatment group is significantly different. Before applying Tukey’s HSD, one treatment should be kept as a reference. Throughout the prior examples, we used treatment 2 as a reference. The ` `` TukeyHSD() `` ` function requires an ANOVA output to test pair-wise differences. Pass the ` `` twosamp_anova `` ` object, as shown in the following code:

```
tukeyhsd_tidy <- tidy(TukeyHSD(twosamp_anova)) |>
  select(contrast,estimate, adj.p.value,
         conf.low, conf.high )

tukeyhsd_tidy
```

The result is presented in the following output:

```

#  15.5 Interrupted Time Series (ITS) analysis 

Interrupted Time Series (ITS) analysis is a robust quasi-experimental method used to evaluate the impact of an intervention or policy change by comparing outcome metrics across distinct time segments. Unlike multi-group comparisons, a standard ITS focuses on a single population, analyzing time-series data to determine whether an intervention “interrupted” the pre-existing level or trend, or both. By examining shifts in both level (an immediate change) and trend (a change in the rate of increase or decrease), ITS provides a statistically rigorous way to attribute observed variations to a specific intervention.

A recent large-scale review of 200 academic studies involving 230 time series, conducted by Turner et al. (2021), summarizes the methods commonly used for ITS analysis. The most widely adopted approaches are regression-based methods estimated using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), either with or without adjustments. Adjustments are critical when time series properties, such as autocorrelation, are present, as these properties violate key OLS assumptions. Autocorrelation, for instance, can bias regression coefficient estimates and render conventional statistical tests invalid due to incorrect standard error estimates.

Several methods are available to address the challenges posed by autocorrelation in ITS analysis, each offering varying degrees of sophistication. They are as follows:

-  **OLS without adjustments** : This approach assumes no autocorrelation in the data, which can lead to biased estimates and unreliable statistical tests if the assumption is violated.
-  **OLS with the Cochrane-Orcutt method** : This technique iteratively estimates the autocorrelation parameter and transforms the regression model to remove first-order autocorrelation (AR(1)) effects. The process continues until convergence is achieved.
-  **Generalized Least Squares (GLS) using the Prais-Winsten method** : This method improves upon Cochrane-Orcutt by retaining the first observation in the dataset, resulting in more precise estimates for models with first-order autocorrelation (AR(1)).
-  **OLS with Newey-West standard errors** : This adjustment accounts for both heteroskedasticity (non-constant variance) and autocorrelation in the error terms, ensuring more reliable statistical inference for time-series data.
-  **Time-series regression models** (e.g., ARIMA with exogenous variables): These models explicitly account for the time series structure of the data, making them suitable for more complex scenarios involving autocorrelation and non-stationarity.

While a comprehensive discussion of these methods and their mathematical foundations is beyond the scope of this book, readers interested in the theoretical underpinnings of OLS adjustments can refer to the seminal work by Judge et al. (1991). This chapter will instead focus on the practical application of these methods to compare results, demonstrating how the concepts introduced in earlier chapters can be used to measure the impact of initiatives through ITS analysis.

By applying ITS methods, this chapter will also highlight the quality of evidence that can be expected when using different approaches to analyze observational data. The core objective is to equip you with the essential tools and insights necessary for the rigorous evaluation of intervention impacts. This includes understanding the inherent strengths and limitations of ITS methods within the context of real-world time series data.

To illustrate the concept of ITS, we will use simulated data throughout this chapter. Simulations are particularly useful for demonstrating ITS methods, as real-world business data used for measuring the impact of an initiative often remains confidential due to its sensitive nature. Businesses are generally hesitant to disclose the specifics of their initiatives, which are often designed to solve industry-wide problems or gain a competitive edge. Simulated data also allows us to benchmark the estimated benefits, as the true impact is known in advance. For this chapter, we will leverage the forecast package, which provides functionality for simulating time-series data under statistical model assumptions. While countless scenarios exist for simulating data, this chapter will focus on common and straightforward scenarios to serve as foundational building blocks for tackling more complex business cases.

#  15.6 ITS on stationary time series 

The stationarity in the response metric is one of the most commonly assumed properties when analyzing data using experimental tools. When there is an interruption in the time series resulting in a level shift attributable to a business intervention, the resulting series covering the pre-intervention and post-intervention periods is no longer stationary. However, the metric can be stationary in the pre- and post-intervention periods separately. Consequently, a before-and-after comparison of the metric using experimental tools would violate the identically distributed assumption necessary for conducting statistical tests of significance.

To avoid this violation of assumption and to control bias due to confounders, experiment-based methods rely on comparisons between treatment and control groups to measure the impact of the intervention. Thus, in our data generation process, we adhere to these principles to ensure that a valid comparison can be made.

##  15.6.3 Statistical power analysis in regression 

Power analysis is a vital statistical technique used to determine the minimum sample size required for an experiment. By balancing the risk of making a false positive error (significance level) against the need for sufficient strength to detect a real effect (statistical power) and the expected effect size, it ensures the study includes enough data to detect a true effect. This method helps confirm that the results of an experiment or survey are statistically significant and not simply due to random chance. For standard experimental designs, such as A/B testing, it is critical to calculate the required sample size upfront.

The concept of statistical power calculation extends to observational data modeling involving regression. In observational data modeling, generally, we don’t have any flexibility in collecting additional data besides what is already collected. This leaves only the option to check whether the minimal sample size requirement was met for regression under the hypothesized effect. A higher effect size indicates that the mean difference is large relative to the overall variability.

The ` `` pwr `` ` package provides functions for performing power analysis for regression. This package allows researchers to calculate sample sizes for hypothesized effects at a specified significance level and power. In a regression setting, the effect size is defined as a ratio of between and within the sum of squares, computed as <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_055.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_055.png</span> , where <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_056.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_056.png</span> is the coefficient of determination of the regression model. Cohen’s effect size thresholds (Cohen, 2013) are <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_057.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_057.png</span> , <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_058.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_058.png</span> , and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_059.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_059.png</span> , corresponding to small, medium, and large effects respectively, for regression. Now, compute the minimum sample size required for a linear regression model to confidently measure the observed effect of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_060.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_060.png</span> with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_061.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_061.png</span> power at a <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_062.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_062.png</span> significance level. The value of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_063.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_063.png</span> indicates degrees of freedom or the number of independent variables excluding the intercept in the model:

```
pwr_analysis_lm <- pwr.f2.test(
  u = 1, f2 = 0.35,
  sig.level = 0.05,
  power = 0.80)

min_sample_size <- ceiling(
  pwr_analysis_lm$u + pwr_analysis_lm$v + 1)
```

The result can be used to determine the minimum sample size required for a regression with one predictor, as shown in the following output:

```

# [1] 25
```

In the formula for calculating the minimum sample size, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_064.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_064.png</span> represents the residual degrees of freedom calculated by the power test. It accounts for the sample data leftover after all parameters are estimated. The additional ` `` + 1 `` ` term accounts for the intercept (i.e., the constant term) in the regression equation.

In the simulation, we kept <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_065.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_065.png</span> observations after intervention for measuring impact. This is sufficient as it is higher than the recommended ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_066.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_066.png</span> ) to observe a large effect size ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_067.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_067.png</span> ) with <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_068.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_068.png</span> power and <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_069.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_069.png</span> level of significance.

Computing the achieved effect size is crucial because it quantifies the measured effect from the observed data. This also allows businesses to determine whether the regression model is practically meaningful, rather than the coefficients being just statistically significant. It is expected that the effect size should be at least as large as the effect size assumed in the power analysis. If the achieved effect size is smaller than the pre-specified effect size, then it is an indication that the model’s estimated impact might not be reliable.

```

#  15.8 ITS on time series with autocorrelation 

In earlier sections, we emphasized that when data is time-stamped but lacks strong time series properties such as serial dependency, a linear model produces results comparable to traditional experimental analysis tools, such as t-tests. However, those examples represent only a small subset of business problems. In practice, business metrics are often serially dependent, influenced by their past values. This dependency can arise for various reasons, such as the following:

-  Customer behavior: In subscription services or repeat-purchase industries, a customer’s past purchase behavior can significantly influence future purchases.
-  Website traffic: Past website traffic can affect current traffic through mechanisms, such as user engagement, repeat visits, and viral trends.
-  Production dynamics: Past production levels can shape current production due to factors such as capacity planning and supply chain constraints.

In this section, we explore options for ITS analysis using data with serial correlation to address such scenarios effectively. To illustrate, we simulate data from an ARIMA(1,0,0) process to ensure first-order autocorrelation with a moderate value of the AR parameter, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_090.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_090.png</span> . ##  15.8.2 Adjustments in the linear model for AR(1) process 

Theory of regression analysis tells us that the presence of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_101.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_101.png</span> in the residuals provides invalid inference unless the effect of autocorrelation is adjusted properly. In particular, autocorrelation can result in an inefficient estimate of model parameters by underestimating standard errors, which leads to false significance of the estimated coefficients.

To address this issue, three approaches are widely used:

-  the Cochrane-Orcutt method
-  the Prais-Winsten method
-  the Newey-West adjustment.

These methods help to isolate the effect of autocorrelation and bring validity to the statistical tests. In R, packages such as ` `` desk `` ` , ` `` prais `` ` , and ` `` lmtest `` ` (in combination with sandwich) can be used to implement these adjustments.

Now, we will move on to the time-series-based approaches for measuring impact from intervention data as they exhibit time series characteristics, such as autocorrelation. Let’s perform the adjustments and examine the results using these methods.

We will apply the Cochrane-Orcutt method to start with. The ` `` cochorc() `` ` function from the ` `` desk `` ` package is used to perform the adjustment on the linear model object. After that the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_102.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_102.png</span> intervals are extracted as shown in the following code:

```
# Adjustment by Cochran-Orcutt
lm_CO_adj <- desk::cochorc(ar_lm, iter = 100)

# Derive 95% confidence interval
lm_CO_adj_int <- tibble(
  bind_rows(
    lm_CO_adj$results[,"coef"] -
    qnorm(0.975)*lm_CO_adj$results[,"std.err."],
    lm_CO_adj$results[,"coef"] +
    qnorm(0.975)*lm_CO_adj$results[,"std.err."]
  )
) |>
  t() |>
  `colnames<-`(c("2.5 %",    "97.5 %"))

lm_CO_adj
```

The adjusted standard error increases to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_111.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_111.png</span> (Newey-West adjustment) from <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_112.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_112.png</span> (unadjusted). Consequently, the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_113.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_113.png</span> confidence interval expands to range from <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_114.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_114.png</span> to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_115.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_115.png</span> , which now contains the true impact of the intervention.

So far, the results demonstrated that when intervention data is autocorrelated, standard hypothesis tests and linear regression models overestimated impact and become unreliable. Because autocorrelation is a fundamental time-series component, it is best addressed through integrated time-series methodologies rather than a two-step ‘regress-then-adjust’ process. By applying robust time-series-based method directly on autocorrelated intervention data, we can obtain valid and reliable impact measures that account for the data’s temporal structure.

Next, we will discuss pure time-series-based approach for measuring intervention impact.

##  15.8.3 ARIMAX for measuring the impact from an AR(1) process 

Time series models are particularly well suited for autocorrelated data, as they explicitly account for lag dependencies. When an intervention is incorporated as an exogenous regressor in such models, its estimated impact is naturally adjusted for autocorrelation. To achieve this, a dummy variable can be used to distinguish between the pre- and post-intervention periods. In this discussion, we focus exclusively on ARIMA models discussed in *<a href="Chapter_12.xhtml#h1_254" class="chapref"> Chapter 12 </a>* , due to their ability to include exogenous regressors.

You might wonder why this topic appears later in the chapter, given that the book primarily focuses on time-series analysis in R. While the sequence may seem unconventional, it is intentional. It highlights the unique value that time-series models bring to scenarios involving autocorrelated data. By now, you should feel confident that linear models, when applied to observational data, serve as a robust alternative to hypothesis testing in experimental settings. Moreover, you have seen how linear models provide flexibility to incorporate adjustments, enabling improved inferences when assumptions are violated. To maintain a coherent narrative, all comparisons in this chapter were conducted on time series data with a known data generation process (the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_116.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_116.png</span> process). In real-world applications, however, this level of clarity is rare, and determining the correct lag order becomes essential to efficiently adjust for autocorrelation.

In the literature, ARIMAX is often termed regression with ARIMA errors or dynamic regression. Theoretically, this is because the model combines a linear regression component (the exogenous variables, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_117.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_117.png</span> ) that captures time dynamics in the autocorrelated error terms through an ARIMA structure.

We will leverage the automated model selection capability of the ARIMA model and let it identify the correct autocorrelation structure. The ` `` ARIMA() `` ` function from the ` `` fable `` ` package by default uses ` `` stepwise=TRUE `` ` , which is optimized for forecasting model selection. On the contrary, we want ARIMA to identify an optimal model for the attribution perspective. That means the optimal model should have the lowest possible information criteria (either <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_118.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_118.png</span> , <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_119.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_119.png</span> , or <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_120.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_120.png</span> ) among all the plausible models. That is why the ` `` stepwise=FALSE `` ` option is used in automated model selection. Now fit the model, extract its coefficients, and compute the confidence intervals of the coefficients. The code progresses as follows:

```
aa_trt <- wd_ar |>
  as_tsibble(index = tidx) |>
  model(arima = ARIMA(y_ar1_trt ~ period, stepwise=FALSE))

aa_trt_coef <- tidy(aa_trt) |>
  mutate(
  lower_95 = estimate - qnorm(0.975) * std.error,
  upper_95 = estimate + qnorm(0.975) * std.error )|>
  mutate(across(where(is.numeric), \(x) sprintf("%.3f", x)))

#  15.9 ITS on time series with AR(1) and trends 

A/B testing is a well-established tool for measuring the impact of initiatives in business settings. Throughout this chapter, we have focused on types of data where experimental tools can be effectively applied and interpreted. In earlier examples, we primarily examined whether equivalent statistical tests yielded significant results and whether confidence intervals captured the true value of the impact. In this section, however, we explore a scenario where the traditional A/B testing approach falls short: measuring the impact on a slope parameter.

Businesses are often interested not only in changing the level or mean of a metric but also in altering its slope. This scenario arises when the goal is to accelerate growth or slow decline in a particular metric. For example, understanding changes in the sales growth rate is critical for evaluating the effectiveness of marketing campaigns, product launches, or seasonal promotions. A specific use case could be measuring the increase in the slope of sales growth after a new advertising campaign to determine its success. Similarly, monitoring changes in the rate at which customers leave (churn) is essential for assessing customer retention efforts. In such cases, a business might aim to decrease the slope of the churn rate after introducing a new customer loyalty program to gauge improvements in retention.

In the preceding practical examples, it is not possible to “split” the experience of all users into two groups to test the impact of the loyalty program, keeping everything else the same, such as brand inconsistency, demographic attributes, and so on. Hence, a traditional RCT is not feasible. Even when the underlying user base changes as time progresses, the aggregated metric should reflect any change due to intervention. This relaxes the requirements of having control and treatment groups to measure the intervention impact. The impact is measured from the behavior of the group that received the intervention/treatment, while the subjects belong to that group may change. Confounding variables handle situations where the underlying group composition changes significantly, and are easy to pass as exogenous variables in time-series models.

For illustration, we will use data with the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_128.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_128.png</span> structure used in the previous example, and add a trend component to it. We will assume the metric showed a <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_129.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_129.png</span> growth before the intervention ( ` `` trn_coef_pre=0.5 `` ` ), and the post-intervention growth of <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_130.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_130.png</span> ( ` `` trnd_coef_post=1 `` ` ). We will disregard a fixed impact size because, with non-parallel trends, the interpretation of the coefficient for the time-period dummy differs from the simpler cases discussed earlier. Instead, we will introduce a secondary time index, ` `` tidx2 `` ` , to account for the incremental trend in the post-intervention period.

Since observational studies frequently lack control groups, we have excluded them from our simulation to better reflect typical research conditions. The data is generated using the following code:

```
set.seed(3)

  mutate(tidx2 = c(rep(0,(n_obs - n_trt)), 1:n_trt)) |>
  mutate(
    y_slope_trt = case_when(
      tidx <= (n_obs - n_trt) ~ y_ar1_trt  + trnd_coef_pre*tidx,
      tidx > (n_obs - n_trt) ~ y_ar1_trt + trnd_coef_pre*tidx +
                                trnd_coef_post*tidx2))

##  15.9.1 ARIMAX for measuring the impact from AR(1) data with trends 

Traditional statistical tests are not suited for measuring the impact of an intervention that introduces a trend (in other words, a slope parameter), because it violates the crucial assumption of sampled subjects being i.i.d. Previous examples also showed that regression-based models require proper adjustment when post-intervention treatment observations have autocorrelation. These leave the time-series-based approach as an important candidate to properly capture the intervention impact in such a scenario.

To measure the slope change (that is, trend) caused by the intervention, we need to pass the time indicator variable and the period as exogenous regressors in the ARIMA model. We also need to estimate the interaction term between the time indicator and intervention period ( ` `` tidx:period `` ` ) variables.

Like the previous implementation, the stepwise selection in automated ARIMA should be FALSE to allow searching among all possible model combinations and finding the best one by minimizing the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_131.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_131.png</span> , as shown in the following code:

#  15.10 Flexibilities of ITS-based inference 

In order to present a compelling case for the application of time series models for estimating causal impact from time series data, we utilized simple cases, specifically restricting ourselves to <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_142.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_142.png</span> in the data simulation process. This choice enabled us to use linear models as alternative tools, given that certain methods allow for valid inference if the residuals from a linear model follow an AR(1) process. Our chosen setting also facilitated the comparison of results from linear models with standard statistical tests used in A/B testing. However, real-world data might originate from a different <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_143.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_143.png</span> process. In such a scenario, time-series models become the sole viable method among other alternatives available.

Some key features that highlight the flexibility and suitability of ITS in practice are as follows:

-  **Measure impact from only the treatment group** : In contrast to A/B testing, ITS estimates impact exclusively from the treatment group. They do not compare responses from the treatment group against the control group. Rather, the intervention impact is actually measured from the subjects who received the intervention (the treatment group). This approach also allows for the intervention to be applied to a larger cohort, eliminating the need for a control group. By following the same cohort over time, the risk of confounding bias in the impact estimate is reduced. As a regression-based method, it offers the flexibility to adjust for confounders by including them as exogenous regressors in the model. Additionally, it is possible to normalize the outcome variable using other influencing variables, such as the size of the cohort, especially when the cohort is defined by criteria that allow units to be included as time progresses.
-  **Allows sample subjects to change over time** : ITS puts no restriction that the subject/items in the treatment group should be exactly the same throughout the intervention period. For example, a major global change has been implemented on a website, such as a major UI redesign or a site-wide navigation update. In this case, the traditional RCT is not feasible because you cannot split the experience of all users without risking technical bugs or brand inconsistency. Crucially, data collected from the sample subjects are not identical across the timeline; the unique visitors arriving at the site before the intervention are different from those visiting after the change. By measuring the impact of the aggregated metric rather than following individual users’ experience, ITS provides a robust measure of impact even when the population is constantly changing. If, in rare scenarios, the underlying sample drastically changes over time, ITS normalizes that effect by adding related confounders as exogenous regressors in the model.
-  **Allows** **seasonality** : Seasonal patterns in the observed metric can be handled by ITS methods by design. The observed data needs to be seasonally adjusted before proceeding with measuring the impact of it. A decomposition method based on non-parametric smoothing, such as LOESS discussed in <a href="Chapter_7.xhtml#h1_161" class="chapref"> Chapter 7 </a> , can be adopted. This approach ensures that seasonal variations are accounted for, allowing for more accurate estimation of the causal impact. By leveraging these techniques, you can obtain robust and reliable results from time series data, making a strong case for the application of time series models in causal impact estimation.

Based on the discussions covered in this chapter so far, we can summarize the appropriate approaches to measure impact under various intervention data structures in *Table 15.1* :

|  **Data Characteristics** |  **Groups in Study** |  **Applicable Methods** |
|----|----|----|
|  Stationary (shifts level only) |  Treatment and control |  Two-sample t-test, regression model |
|  Trend |  Treatment and control |  Two-sample t-test, regression model (may need adjustments if not adequate enough) |
|  Autocorrelated, such as AR(1) |  Treatment and control |  Regression method with ITS adjustments (Cochrane-Orcutt, Prais-Winsten, Newey-West), time-series model with regressors (ARIMAX) |
|  Autocorrelated (AR(1)) with trend |  Treatment |  Regression-based method with ITS adjustments, time-series model with regressors (ARIMAX) |
|  Autocorrelated (AR(1)) with trend and seasonality |  Treatment |  Time-series model with regressors (ARIMAX) after seasonal adjustments |

In intervention analysis, monitoring a guardrail metric is essential to ensure that improvements in the primary business metrics do not come at the expense of long-term business goals or unintended operational trade-offs. In the next section, we discuss how you can incorporate monitoring guardrail metrics in ITS.

#  15.11 Monitoring guardrail metrics in ITS 

Traditional A/B testing advocates for checking guardrail metrics. A guardrail metric is a type of secondary metric used in A/B testing to ensure that any changes implemented do not adversely affect important aspects of the user experience or business performance. While primary metrics focus on the main goals of an experiment, such as conversion rates or revenue, guardrail metrics serve as safety checks to monitor potential negative impacts. The importance of checking guardrail metrics in A/B testing cannot be overstated, as they help to identify unintended consequences of experimental changes, ensuring that improvements in primary metrics are not achieved at the expense of other critical factors. For instance, an increase in conversion rates might coincide with a decrease in customer satisfaction, or discounts offered to customers might reduce the overall revenue, and so on.

Several options exist for checking guardrail metrics in an A/B test. By analyzing these metrics alongside primary metrics, researchers can gain a holistic understanding of the experiment’s impact. Statistical methods such as t-tests or confidence intervals can be applied to guardrail metrics to assess their significance. Additionally, visualizations such as time series plots or control charts can help identify trends and anomalies. Implementing guardrail metrics in A/B testing ensures that experiments are not only driving desired outcomes but also maintaining or improving overall user experience and operational stability.

When guardrail metrics themselves are non-stationary, t-tests lose their statistical validity. Time-series models can again come to the rescue but have their own caveat. In the realm of time-series analysis, “spurious regression” occurs when two or more non-stationary time series are regressed against each other, leading to a misleadingly high <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_15_144.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_15_144.png</span> value and significant t-statistics, despite there being no meaningful relationship between the variables (i.e., primary and guardrail metrics). This phenomenon is a consequence of the underlying time-series data being integrated, which means they possess a unit root and exhibit random walks or trends over time. The primary consequence of spurious regression is that it can lead researchers to draw false conclusions about the relationships between variables, potentially informing incorrect business decisions or policy interventions.

To avoid spurious regression, it is crucial to ensure that the time-series data is stationary before performing regression analysis. Stationarity implies that the statistical properties of the series, such as mean and variance, do not change over time. Common techniques to achieve stationarity include differencing the data (detrending). Additionally, performing unit root tests, such as KPSS test, as discussed in *<a href="Chapter_8.xhtml#h1_184" class="chapref"> Chapter 8 </a>* , can help verify stationarity. By addressing non-stationarity, you can avoid the pitfalls of spurious regression and derive more accurate and reliable insights from the observed time series.

Nevertheless, assessing guardrail metrics in isolation might fail to capture the bi-directional relationship between the primary and guardrail metrics. The time-series-based methods provide a natural solution to this issue. The Vector Autoregression (VAR) modeling discussed in *<a href="Chapter_14.xhtml#h1_291" class="chapref"> Chapter 14 </a>* offers a robust framework to better understand and analyze these interdependencies. Through VAR modeling, it is possible to account for the dynamic interactions between multiple time series, providing a more comprehensive insight into how changes in the primary metric can affect guardrail metrics over time.
