#  7.1 Summary of differences: Adjustments, transformations and decompositions 

Adjustments, transformations and decompositions serve fundamentally different analytical purposes in time series analysis.

Adjustments are about ensuring the data accurately reflects the underlying characteristics by correcting for measurable, external influences (e.g., holidays, trading-day effect).

Transformations are mathematical functions applied to the data to improve statistical properties (i.e., reducing volatility) required by further modeling.

Decomposition methods are procedures applied to the data to separate inherent time series components (trend, seasonality, and remainder) for better structural understanding, modeling and forecasting.

A brief comparison of time series adjustment, transformation, and decomposition techniques is presented in *Table 7.1* . You will learn the usage of these techniques and when to apply them in an analytical framework:

|  **Criteria** |  **Adjustments** |  **Transformations** |  **Decomposition** |
|----|----|----|----|
|  Primary goal |  Data cleaning: Remove external biases or normalize scale |  Model prep: Stabilize variance and normalize distribution for modeling assumptions |  Analysis: Break series into inherent components (trend, season, remainder) |
|  Nature of change |  Value modified by external factors (e.g., day counts, population size) |  Mathematical function applied to values (e.g., log, power function) |  Statistical model that separates deterministic parts of the series |
|  Methods included |  Calendar, population, indexing |  Logarithmic, Box-Cox |  MA-based methods, classical, X-13-ARIMA, STL |

Table 7.1: Comparison of time series adjustment, transformation, and decomposition methods

In summary, while each of these three techniques manipulates data differently, they collectively form an essential analytical toolkit in the time series analysis framework. Depending on the problem at hand, you may need to apply only one technique or all three combined.

Let’s look at scenarios where adjustments and transformations are required for time series data.

#  7.2 Rationales for time series adjustments and transformations 

Adjustments and transformations make time series data suitable for analysis and modeling. The purposes of time series adjustment and transformations are the following:

-  **Removing known sources of variation** : Some data variation can occur solely due to calendar effects. Calendar adjustments are needed before identifying any other existing pattern in the data.
-  **Handling outliers and anomalies** : Outliers are large measurement errors, and anomalies are data points that do not belong to the assumed data distribution. They can distort the analysis and modeling of time series data, and may be caused by errors in data collection or unusual events. Adjustments may involve identifying and handling outliers by winsorizing or applying time series smoothing techniques.
-  **Transforming data to a stationary state** : Stationarity is a property of time series data that means the mean, variance, and autocorrelation structure do not change over time. The validity of many analyses and models depends on the stationarity of the input data. Differencing or detrending is used to make non-stationary data stationary.
-  **Seasonality adjustment** : Seasonality is a pattern in data that repeats at fixed intervals. Often this masks the magnitude of other components. Seasonal differencing and seasonal decomposition are two common techniques for removing seasonal patterns from data, making it easier to analyze underlying trends and irregular components.
-  **Missing observations** : Missing observations are a common scenario you will face when dealing with business time series data. Imputation and interpolation methods are used to fill in missing data points, ensuring a complete series for modeling.
-  **Data non-normality** : Many statistical analyses and inferences assume that the input data follows a normal distribution. If the observed data does not follow normality, then a logarithmic or Box-Cox transformation is applied to make the data more normally distributed.
-  **Heteroscedasticity** : Heteroscedasticity refers to the changing variability of residuals from a time series model over time. It affects the reliability of statistical analyses. This will also happen when non-linear data is passed into a modeling framework that expects a linear series. A logarithmic function is the most common way to transform data in such a scenario. Square root or cube root functions are also used, depending on the nature of non-linearity. It is important to apply a reverse transformation on the analyzed or model output to bring data back to its original scale.
-  **Noise-reduction** : Too much noise or random variability in the data obscures underlying patterns. Time series smoothing or filtering methods are used to reduce noise and highlight underlying components.
-  **Comparability** : When multiple time series are compared or data is combined from different sources, adjustments are often needed to ensure that the data is on a common scale and unit of measurement.
-  **Interpretability** : Adjustments and transformations often simplify complex patterns or relationships, which makes the data more interpretable. This helps in making informed business decisions.
-  **Improving forecast accuracy** : Sometimes, adjustments and transformations are performed solely to improve the forecast accuracy of a model. These adjustments are based on business rules and largely depend on the problem at hand.

Now that you have learned about the reasons for applying adjustments and transformations to time series data, let’s look at some common adjustment techniques.

##  7.3.1 Calendar adjustments 

These adjustments are used to remove the effects of known calendar events, such as holidays and trading days. For example, you are analyzing the monthly total number of visitors to a website over 2 years, and February is expected to be lower than the other months solely because of the smaller number of days. You can adjust this variation by using the average monthly visitors rather than the totals. This is known as calendar adjustments. There may be other reasons why visitors in February could be lower. Calendar adjustments also help you separate known facts from the unknown when analyzing data.

For illustration, we will use a synthetic time series of total daily visitors to a website. The daily website visitor count is generated using a Poisson distribution with an average of 950 visitors per day. The ` `` total_visitor `` ` and ` `` avg_visitor `` ` are computed per month using the following code:

```
# Set the seed for reproducibility
set.seed(3302)
visitors <- tibble(visit_date = seq(as.Date("2021-01-01"),
                                    as.Date("2022-12-31"),
                                    by = "1 day")) |>
    mutate(visitor = rpois(n = n(), lambda = 950)) |>
    as_tsibble() |>
    group_by_key() |>
    index_by(year_month = ~yearmonth(.)) |>
    summarize(total_visitor = sum(visitor),
              avg_visitor = mean(visitor))
```

##  7.3.2 Population adjustments 

Population adjustments may require any data that is impacted by changes in the population. A large number of metrics are always reported after population adjustments, such as birth and death rates, crime rates, school enrollment rates, unemployment rates, inflation rates, and so on. Population adjustments are usually applied to raw measurements, such as total births, deaths, and number of unemployed, which would increase with a larger population.

Let’s illustrate the concept with a simulated scenario where you are analyzing the clicks of a certain product on a website to assess its popularity. The simulation runs as follows:

1.   Simulate a base time series ( ` `` stable_clicks `` ` ) for a stable population ( ` `` base_visits `` ` ).

2.   Introduce an increasing population bias: 20% annual growth in raw visits.

3.   Compute daily raw clicks and population-adjusted clicks normalized to the average population level.

4.   Compute monthly unadjusted raw clicks ( ` `` unadjusted_clicks `` ` ) and population adjusted clicks ( ` `` adjusted_clicks `` ` ) by taking the monthly average to accommodate calendar adjustment and population adjustment at the same time.

    Let’s see a full breakdown of the code:

    ```
    set.seed(3302)
    days_in_sim <- 730
    clicks_data <- tibble(
      visit_date = seq(as.Date("2021-01-01"),
                       as.Date("2022-12-31"),
                       by = "1 day"),
      stable_clicks = rpois(n = days_in_sim, lambda = 100),
      base_visitors = rpois(n = days_in_sim, lambda = 5000)) |>
      # introduce annual population growth
      mutate(
        date_index = as.numeric(visit_date - min(visit_date)),
        raw_visits = base_visitors * (1 + 0.2 * (date_index/365))
      ) |>
      mutate(
        raw_clicks = stable_clicks * (1 + 0.2 * (date_index/365)),
        pop_adjusted_clicks = raw_clicks/raw_visits * mean(raw_visits)
      ) |>
      as_tsibble() |>
      index_by(year_month = ~ yearmonth(.)) |>
      summarize(
        unadjusted_clicks = mean(raw_clicks),
        adjusted_clicks = mean(pop_adjusted_clicks)
      )
    ```

5.   Plot the results to visually highlight the differences. *Figure 7.2* presents time plots of the two series in one graphical window:

    <figure class="mediaobject-one">
     <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_7_2.png" data-original-image-title="">Image 2</span> 
    </figure>

    Figure 7.2: Effect of population and calendar adjustments to a simulated product click data

    The visual comparison of ` `` unadjusted_clicks `` ` and ` `` adjusted_clicks `` ` clearly illustrates the importance of population adjustment in time series analysis. The unadjusted series (dashed line) exhibits a strong upward trend, reflecting the 20% annual visitor growth on the website. On the other hand, the adjusted series (solid line) removes this growth bias, remaining stable and flat over a two-year period. Based on the unadjusted clicks, some might interpret that the product has become popular, whereas the adjusted clicks tell the opposite story.

    Population adjustments are important because they isolate the true underlying behavior of observed data, free from the confounding influence of external population changes, and ensure that the data is comparable over time.

##  7.3.3 Base adjustments 

Base adjustment is the process of designating a specific observation within a given time as the base, and then subsequently expressing all other observations relative to that base. Base adjustments are also known as reference point adjustments.

For example, you compare sales before, during and after a promotion trial. To identify the impact of the promotion, it is important to adjust all observations to a reference point. You might use a day’s sales as the base before your analysis period begins. If there is strong weekly seasonality in your data, then you can use seven days’ sales (one for each day of the week) as a base instead of a single day’s figure and adjust the data using the corresponding weekdays’ base value. This would allow you to compare the actual impact of promotion, even if the population size or the sales rate had changed.

Base adjustments can be done using a variety of methods, including but not limited to the following:

-  **Indexing** : This is the most common method for base adjustments. It involves dividing each data point by the value of the data point in the base period and then multiplying by 100. Indexing transforms all series to a common scale (base=100), which makes comparisons among series simpler.
-  **Deflating** : By deflating, you can remove inflation from the data. This can be done by dividing each observation by a price index. The **Consumer Price Index** ( **CPI** ) and the **Produce Price Index** ( **PPI** ) are commonly used for adjusting inflation in economic data.
-  **Chaining** : Chaining involves linking together successive periods of data by calculating the percentage change in each period relative to the previous period. This technique is commonly used in business to compare changes over time, as percentage changes can be more informative than measuring changes in actual values.

We will explain how to perform indexing on time series data using the Switzerland quarterly total employment data for females and males. The data are downloaded from <a href="https://www.dataseries.org" style="text-decoration: none;">  www.dataseries.org  </a> using the ` `` dataseries `` ` package and filtered to have full-year data from 2000 to 2019. Here, the yearly total employees are calculated for both groups and adjusted using their respective values in 2000 as a base:

```
swiss_employment <- cbind(
  male = ds("ch_comb_emp.ttl.m.tot", "ts"),
  female = ds("ch_comb_emp.ttl.f.tot", "ts")
) |>
    as_tsibble() |>
    filter_index("2000 Q1" ~ "2019 Q4") |>
    index_by(year = ~year(.)) |>
    group_by_key() |>
    summarize(employee = sum(value)) |>
    group_by_key() |>
    mutate(employee_base_2000 = round(100 * employee/employee[1]))
```

#  7.4 Time series transformations 

Mathematical transformations are performed when time series data exhibits heteroscedasticity; this refers to when the variance of the data changes along with the level of the data over time. These types of transformations also bring non-normal data to normality—an important assumption to hold for statistical analysis and modeling.

We will look at logarithmic transformations first.

##  7.4.1 Logarithmic transformation 

A logarithmic (in short, log) transformation can be used in multiple scenarios. The two most useful cases are to transform data when it is not normally distributed, and to stabilize the variance.

Logarithmic transformations are widely used in practice because they make it easy to understand the relative changes in a set of data. In base-10 logs, an increase of 1 unit corresponds to a 10× increase in the original value. This makes it easy to evaluate how the data is changing over time, even if the original values are very large or very small. However, log transformation cannot be used when there are zero or negative numbers in the data.

If the original observations are denoted as, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_001.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_001.png</span> then you can obtain the log transformed series as <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_002.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_002.png</span> .

The auto selection, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_028.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_028.png</span> , reduces the exponential nature of the data without compromising the actual nature of the series, that is, variability increased along with trend. This is crucial for performing proper analysis as the transformation did not hide the actual pattern in the data.

If Box-Cox transformations are used, it is important to apply inverse Box-Cox transformations on forecast values to bring them to the original scale to ensure that the forecasts are interpretable and comparable.

#  7.6 Decomposition using MAs 

The MA takes the average of a fixed number of observations by moving the time horizon. The number of observations is fixed, and observations leave and enter as the time horizon changes. The MA removes noise in the data and reveals underlying trends, trend-cycles, or seasonal components. That is why the technique is known as a time series smoothing technique than a decomposition method.

The MA method has a parameter, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_034.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_034.png</span> , which denotes the number of observations of the averaging window. For example, a 3-MA uses three data points to calculate the average. The MA value is aligned with the center of the window (for odd <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_035.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_035.png</span> value). Naturally, there are no MA values at the beginning or at the end of a series. The higher the <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_036.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_036.png</span> value, the larger the information loss is. Some allow a shorter window of observations at the end of the series. Even if there is information loss, the technique works really well for short-to-medium series to depict any underlying components.

Next, we’ll see how to decompose a series with trends using MA smoothing and then we’ll decompose a series with seasonality using the same technique.

##  7.6.1 MA smoothing of data with trends 

You will find an example of smoothing annual takeaway food retail turnover with a 3-MA and a 5-MA. The helper functions from the ` `` slider() `` ` package ( ` `` Vaughan, 2022 `` ` ) provide an easier way to implement MAs in your code:

```
smooth_turnover <- takeaway_turnover |>
  index_by(year = ~year(.)) |>
  summarise(turnover = sum(turnover)) |>
  mutate(`3-MA` = slide_dbl(.x = turnover,
                            .f = mean,
                            .before = 1,
                            .after = 1,
                            .complete = FALSE),
         `5-MA` = slide_dbl(.x = turnover,
                            .f = mean,
                            .before = 2,
                            .after = 2,
                            .complete = FALSE ))
```

Now, visualize the two MA series with the original series to inspect the effect of smoothing. Both the original and the MA series are plotted in *Figure 7.9* :

Figure 7.9: MAs of annual Australian takeaway food services turnover 2015-2022

The parameter of MA controls the amount of noise reduction. There is a possibility of introducing new noises if the data is over-smoothed. As we see in *Figure 7.9* , the 3-MA series has smoothed the annual series to reveal the trend, whereas the 5-MA series changes the magnitude of the trend at the end by over-smoothing observations around 2020.

##  7.6.2 MA smoothing of data with seasonality 

For seasonal series, it is best practice to use an MA that matches the frequency of the seasonal data; for example, using a 12-MA for a monthly series. As you will notice, it is not possible to align MA values exactly to a middle observation when the sliding window is even. Taking a further 2-MA on the 12-MA helps mitigate the issue as shown in the following code:

```
turnover_monthly_ma <- takeaway_turnover |>
  mutate(`12-MA` = slide_dbl(.x = turnover,
                             .f = mean,
                             .before = 5,
                             .after = 6,
                             .complete = TRUE),
         `2x12-MA` = slide_dbl(.x = `12-MA`,
                               .f = mean,
                               .before = 1,
                               .after = 0,
                               .complete = TRUE))
```

The smoothed data should reveal the underlying trend or trend-cycle component, if exists. Some data points are lost as we used the ` `` .complete=TRUE `` ` argument while calculating MAs.

#  7.7 Trend and seasonal decomposition with the classical method 

The classical decomposition method uses MA smoothing to estimate trends, and seasonal components using de-trended values.

In *<a href="Chapter_4.xhtml#h1_113" class="chapref"> Chapter 4 </a>* , we saw that time series components can be combined either additively or multiplicatively. Classical decomposition is of two types based on how components are presented in the data. Additive decomposition is used when the seasonal component is constant from year to year, while multiplicative decomposition is used when the seasonal component varies over time. The technique follows the steps described next.

1.   The trend-cycle ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_037.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_037.png</span> ) is estimated using MA smoothing. The smoothed values are used to de-trend the observed value ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_038.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_038.png</span> ). The de-trended series contains the seasonal ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_039.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_039.png</span> ) and remainder ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_040.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_040.png</span> ) terms. Depending on how the components are combined, one of the following two equations is used to de-trend:
    -  Additive: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_041.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_041.png</span>
    -  Multiplicative: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_042.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_042.png</span>

2.   The seasonal indices <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_043.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_043.png</span> are obtained by taking the average of the de-trended value at each seasonal period <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_044.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_044.png</span> :

    -  Additive: The seasonal indices are adjusted so that <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_045.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_045.png</span>
    -  Multiplicative: The seasonal indices are adjusted so that <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_046.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_046.png</span>

    The seasonal components <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_047.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_047.png</span> are obtained by chaining these seasonal indices and repeating for each year.

3.   The remainder component is then obtained as follows:
    -  Additive: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_048.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_048.png</span>
    -  Multiplicative: <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_049.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_049.png</span>

You can apply the preceding method using the ` `` classical_decomposition() `` ` function from the ` `` feasts `` ` package. The ` `` type `` ` argument allows you to pass which method of decomposition should be used, additive or multiplicative.

#  7.8 Model based decomposition methods 

Many financial and economic time series require proper seasonal adjustment. For policy makers it is crucial to compare seasonally adjusted data to evaluate existing policies and design new ones if needed. From that need, model-based decomposition methods, such as X-11 and Seasonal Extraction in ARIMA Time Series (SEATS) were developed and implemented in software. The X-11-ARIMA method was first developed by the US Census Bureau in 1965 and has evolved in multiple versions, the most recent one being X-13-ARIMA. The SEATS method was developed by the Bank of Spain and implemented in two kinds of software. Many government agencies, statistical bureaus, and financial institutions around the world widely use these model-based decomposition methods. However, detailed discussion on these topics is out of the scope of this book. For further reference see Dagum et al. ( ` `` 2016 `` ` ).

Advantages of these model-based decomposition techniques include, but not limited to, the following:

-  No information loss due to MA smoothing
-  Better seasonality estimates in quarterly and monthly data (cannot handle weekly or daily seasonality, though)
-  Handling of trading day variations
-  Handling holiday effects and outliers robustly
-  Use of external predictors

Both the X-11 and SEATS methods for seasonal decomposition are implemented in the feasts package via the ` `` X_13ARIMA_SEATS() `` ` function. This function calls the seasonal package, which offers further controls and helper functions.

You can apply the methods to the takeaway food turnover data using the following code:

```
turnover_model_decomp <- takeaway_turnover |>
  model(x11 = X_13ARIMA_SEATS(turnover ~ x11()),
        seats = X_13ARIMA_SEATS(turnover ~ seats()))
```

Extract the components estimated using X-11 and make a decomposition plot as follows:

```
turnover_model_decomp |>
  select(x11) |>
  components() |>
   autoplot(linewidth = 1) +
  labs(x = "")
```

Let’s examine the X-11 decomposition on the takeaway turnover presented in *Figure 7.12* :

</figure>

Figure 7.12: X-13-ARIMA decomposition on monthly Australian takeaway food services turnover 2015-2022

If you are looking for a versatile and robust decomposition method that considers all seasonal frequencies, unlike the model-based methods, or you are wishing for a fully automated method with flexible user controls, then you should use STL decomposition. You have already seen STL mentioned in several sections. Now, you will learn more about it in the next section.

#  7.9 STL decomposition 

**Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess** ( **STL** ) was introduced by Cleveland et al. ( ` `` 1990 `` ` ). The method uses **Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing** ( **LOESS** ) techniques in the estimation of the time series components. It overcomes all the major disadvantages of the classical and model-based decomposition methods, as follows:

-  Handles all types of seasonal frequency ranges, from daily to monthly
-  Captures variations in seasonal components over the years
-  More user control over trend-cycle smoothness, which reduces the possibilities of over-smoothing
-  Outliers are estimated via a robust method

However, STL assumes an additive model by design and cannot automatically handle trading-day variation or holiday effects. The STL method can be extended to allow the multiplicative nature of time series after proper transformation.

The method is implemented in the ` `` forecast::stl() `` ` and ` `` feasts::STL() `` ` functions. We will use the latter version as it fits with the tidyverse ecosystem. STL decomposition uses multiple parameters under three broad categories. The defaults are set for each parameter, which works well for the majority of the series. As a user, you may need to control the following parameters depending on the observed series:

- ` `` trend(window=?) `` ` : Determines how many nearest observations are to be considered in the LOESS method to estimate the trend-cycle component. A larger window value allows for greater smoothness. For monthly data the default is 21. Defaults are calculated for each seasonal frequency. Should always be an odd value.
- ` `` season(window=?) `` ` : How many consecutive years to be considered in seasonal indices estimation. The default value is 13 and should be an odd value. To consider a consistent seasonal pattern across the years use ‘periodic’ instead.
- ` `` lowpass(window=?) `` ` : The number of observations used in the low-pass filter for each subseries. Uses an odd number matching the seasonal period of the data by default.

The robust option also handles data with outliers. We will use the same takeaway turnover data as we used before to compare across several decomposition techniques. Let’s extract time series components from monthly turnover using STL:

```
turnover_stl <- takeaway_turnover |>
  model(
    stl_dcomp = STL(
      turnover ~ trend(window = 12) +
                 season(window="periodic"),
      robust = TRUE 
    )
  )
```

Earlier in *Section 7.5* , we showed the use of ` `` components() `` ` function to extract the estimated components from an ` `` STL `` ` object and generate a decomposition plots from an STL fit. You can experiment by passing different parameter values in the ` `` STL `` ` function and then compare the outputs by generating decomposition plots.

By default, STL assumes components are combined additively in the observed series. You can use STL to decompose data even when components are combined multiplicatively after transforming data either using a logarithmic or Box-Cox transformation. Taking the logarithmic route makes the data to an additive model:

<span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_050.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_050.png</span>

Box-Cox transformation provides greater flexibility as it enables robust decomposition when the data has issues, such as non-constant variance. When the optimal transformation parameter, <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_051.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_051.png</span> , is between 0 and 1 ( <span class="image placeholder" data-original-image-src="../Images/B21040_07_052.png" data-original-image-title="">B21040_07_052.png</span> ), the series must first be transformed before applying the ` `` STL() `` ` function. It is crucial to back-transform the resulting decomposed series to the original unit of measurement for valid interpretation.

We have covered four different time series decomposition techniques. Each of them has its own strengths and limitations, applied widely in current business practices. In the next section, a summary of these techniques is presented.

##  7.9.1 Summary of decomposition techniques 

Decomposition is an integral part of analyzing time series data. Time series decomposition methods, ranging from simple to complex, are discussed with examples in *Table 7.3* :

|  **Method** |  **Assumptions** |  **Strengths** |  **Limitations** |  **Use case** |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  MA |  Seasonality is strictly stable/constant (additive or multiplicative) |  Simple calculation; intuitive understanding; good for quick visualization |  Highly sensitive to outliers; information loss at start/end; cannot handle moving seasonality |  Simple, stable data for quick, exploratory analysis |
|  Classical decomposition |  Seasonal component and trend component are constant/stable over time |  Simple and fast; provides clear separation of components |  Cannot handle moving seasonality; poor outlier handling |  Historical economic data with very stable patterns; initial exploration |
|  X-13ARIMA / SEATS |  The     underlying time series can be adequately modeled by an ARIMA model structure |  Industry standard in official     statistics (e.g., Census Bureau); handles outliers and trading days automatically |  Complex and resource-intensive; black box for many users; difficult to integrate automated pipelines |  Official government statistics (e.g., GDP, unemployment rates) where high accuracy is required |
|  STL |  Seasonality can move slowly over time (LOESS smoothing handles evolution) |  Very robust to outliers; handles all types of seasonal frequencies |  Default is additive decomposition (requires pre-transformation for multiplicative data); does not handle trading days/holidays automatically |  Applicable to most time series analysis |

Table 7.3: Summary of time series decomposition methods
