## Biserial Correlations

Pearson devised the **biserial correlation** in 1909\. This is related to the product-moment correlation (in which both variables are continuous), with one difference.

![image](./images/00221.jpeg)

The value of one of the variables is an artificial dichotomy, such as pass/fail in a test. The cut-off point for pass/fail may be determined differently by various teachers.

As we will see later, the biserial correlation is also similar to Student’s t-test and Fisher’s analysis of variance.

The **point-biserial correlation** is related to Pearson’s biserial correlation, but one variable is continuous and the other is a “true dichotomy”, such as male/female. This is an estimate of what the product-moment correlation would be if the dichotomous variable were replaced by a continuous variable instead.

These two methods are used quite extensively by psychometricians for test-item analysis in the construction of various intelligence and aptitude tests. The biserial is commonly used to determine the correlation between the score of a test item and the total scores on the test.

![image](./images/00222.jpeg)

When the point-biserial is used to measure the correlation between the score on the item and the score on the test,… …this becomes a statistical measure of the “internal consistency” of test items.

**Pearson’s triserial correlation** is similar to the biserial, where one variable is continuous, but the second variable is a trichotomy (e.g., low, medium and high).
