## Abstract

This chapter introduces the chi-square family of statistical tests, focusing on their application in categorical data analysis using R. Using both a hypothetical planetary dataset and the CPS85 dataset, readers learn to construct contingency tables, compute observed and expected frequencies and apply the chi-square equation to test hypotheses about associations between categorical variables. The chapter covers both the Goodness of Fit test for single categorical variables and the Test of Independence for two-variable analyses, illustrating step-by-step calculations and interpretation. Key statistical concepts are addressed, including null and alternative hypotheses, _p_\-values, degrees of freedom, and statistical significance. Additionally, assumptions of chi-square tests and effect size measures, such as _Phi_, Cramér’s V, and contingency coefficients, are explained. Readers are guided through implementing these analyses in R, generating tables and plots, calculating effect sizes, and preparing results for reporting, providing a practical foundation for performing and interpreting chi-square tests in research.

## 6.1 Astronaut Scenario

An astronaut just returned to earth and has an interesting finding to report. On her travels to both Mars and Venus, she noticed that they also have variations of light and dark eyes. However, she does not know if the proportion of eye colors is different between Mars, Venus, and Earth. On her travels, she randomly sampled hundreds of Martians and Venusians and noted their eye colors, categorizing them as dark (shades of brown or hazel) and light (shades of green or blue). Now that she has returned to Earth, she will randomly sample Earthlings for the same classification, thus generating a dataset with two variables: (a) Planet of origin (Venus, Earth, or Mars), and (b) Eye shade (light or dark). From this dataset, she generates the following research question: I_s there a difference in eye shades given planet of origin_? The null hypothesis (_H_0) is that there is no difference in eye shade meaning that the proportions of eye shade are equal across planets. This leaves several alternative hypotheses with several different combinations as shown in Table [6.1](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter). As shown in the table, we have concocted eight different alternatives to the null hypothesis to answer this research question. We could generate even more if we were more specific. For example, we could say Earth has more light Eyes than Venus, but not more light eyes than Mars, but Mars has more dark eyes than Venus, etc. However, this is not necessary because we can simply test it and see what the data tells us.

## 6.2 Conceptual Background

The chi-square (_χ_2) test is a fundamental statistical method for determining whether one category differs across another category (Agresti, [2018](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)). In our example, we are interested to see if eye shade category differs across the planet category. In a sense, this also tests to see if there is a relationship between the independent and dependent variable. Like I stated earlier, differences and correlations (relationships) are separate sides of the same coin. If we find there is a difference in eye shade given planet, we can also say that there is a relationship between eye shade and planet, or that there is some predictability of planet of origin given eye shade.

The tricky part in understanding chi-square is that we compare the frequencies that we observe to the frequencies we would expect. Thus, we compare what is known as the expected count to the actual count. If the expected count and the actual count are statistically equal (or _p_ \> .05) then we accept the null. If the actual count is different than the expected count (_p_ < .05) then we reject the null and accept an alternative hypothesis depending on the counts from our observed data (Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)).

Chi-square consists of two approaches. The first type, the Goodness of Fit Test, is used when you only have one category across a population (Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)). For example, you want to see if all Earthlings (and only Earthlings) have equal proportions of light and dark eyes. This is the simplest type of chi-square test. The second approach, the Test of Independence is used when you want to examine two variables (independent and dependent) that are categorical on a contingency table. A contingency table consists of rows and columns, the rows being the categories of one variable and the column being the categories of the other. For example, as shown in Table [6.2](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter), we construct a contingency table with two rows (eye color: light, dark) and three columns (planet of origin: Earth, Venus, Mars) to represent our astronaut’s research question about eyes and planets of origin. The symbol _n_ represents the count of individuals in each category. Subscripts indicate category combinations; for example, _n_ _el_ represents the number of individuals with light-colored eyes on Earth.

In this case, our astronaut must accept the null hypothesis that there is no difference between eye shade given planet of origin _χ_2(2) = 3.73, _p_ \= .155\. Notice that we report the statistics with the degrees of freedom in parentheses, followed by the _p_ value.

### 6.2.1 Statistical Assumptions of Chi-Square Tests

Most statistical tests come with assumptions. If these assumptions are not met, then the test will not work, or the results will be problematic. The assumptions of chi-square are simple. First, both the i**ndependent and dependent variables should be nominal or interval (categorical)**. Using scale level data will result in a contingency table with one column or row per score on the variable. For example, if you had a test worth 0–100 points, they would be one row for 0, one for 1, one for 2, etc. Therefore, in chi-square tests both variables should be categorical and should have a limited number of categories per variable. Second, **observations are independent meaning that one value does not affect the other and each row of data should be unduplicated** (e.g., each person should only have one row) on each variable. This way different categories are not overcounted. **Third, expected values should be 5 or greater**, which indicates that we should have a large enough sample size to detect any difference if it exists (Field, [2018](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Spatz, [2019](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)).

### 6.2.2 Effect Size in Chi-Square Tests

Effect size is a standardized number that quantifies how strong the relationship between the independent or dependent variable is and is usually reported if statistical significance is found. Most statistical tests have an effect size statistic, which you will learn about as we explore them. Chi-square-related effect sizes are reported on a number between 0 and 1\. The closer to 1 the effect size, the stronger the difference or relationship. When we run chi-square in R, you will notice we get three types of effect sizes, all of them close in results: (a) Phi (_Φ_), (b) The contingency coefficient (_C_ or _C\*_), and (c) Cramér’s _V_. These can all be used for 2 × 2 tests, but V is also applicable to any size of table (for example 2 × 3, which is what we have). Thus, I typically report on Cramer’s _V_. There are other effect size measures for contingency tables worth noting. Kendall’s _Tau-b_ is typically used with ordinal data as does gamma (_γ_). Tschuprow’s _T_ can be used for tables larger than 3 × 3 (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)).

For _V_ and other related effect sizes, Table [6.7](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter) provides a way to interpret the values for the most common effect sizes used in the chi-square family. As you can see, the interpretation is the same across all measures. Other effect sizes could be different, so it is advisable to check the literature (Field et al., [2012](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter); Leech et al., [2013](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)).

__Table 6.7Interpreting chi-square related effect sizes__
| Effect size             | 0.00–0.10               | 0.10–0.30                 | 0.30–0.50            | 0.50–0.70          | 0.70–1.00               |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| Cramér’s V              | Very weak or negligible | Small or weak association | Moderate association | Strong association | Very strong association |
| Phi (2 × 2 tables)      | Very weak or negligible | Weak association          | Moderate association | Strong association | Very strong association |
| Kendall’s Tau-b         | Very weak association   | Weak association          | Moderate association | Strong association | Very strong association |
| Contingency coefficient | Very weak or negligible | Weak association          | Moderate association | Strong association | Very strong association |

## 6.3 Running Chi-Square on R

Running a chi-square test in R is straightforward, but it requires the dataset to be properly structured and converted into a contingency table. It is now time to run some inferential statistics. We are going to shift from planetary data to the CPS85 dataset on R from the _MOSAIC_ package (Pruim et al., [2023](A668427_1_En_6_Chapter)) to run some chi-square statistical tests.

### 6.3.1 Getting the Dataset

The first step is to get the dataset ready on R. We start by loading the _mosaic_ library with _library(mosaic)_ and then entering _view(CPS85)_ in the console to examine the dataset. Upon examination, there are several categorical variables. We can explore which ones we want by entering _unique(CPS85$race)_, replacing “race” with any of the variables. This will list their unique values. In this case, we see there is a “W” for white and “NW” for not white. We can also see how many levels each variable has by entering _n\_distict(CPS85$race)_ (or any variable we want). This will tell us how many distinct (non-duplicated) categories of the variable there are. For race, there are two.

For this exercise, we will perform a 2 × 2 chi-square test to answer the following research question: Is there a difference in union membership given a worker’s race? Each “2” in the “2 × 2” refers to the number of categories each variable contains. In this case race contains two (as explained above) and union contains two with “Not” for not union and “Union” for union.” The null hypothesis is that there is no difference in union membership given race. Remember, we are most interested in observed count versus expected count, so proportions matter more than sheer numbers.
