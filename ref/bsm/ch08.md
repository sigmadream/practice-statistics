A classic Bayesian analysis strives for a fairly simple statistical model with a handful of interpretable parameters. This is ideal for testing specific hypotheses or understanding the main drivers of the process of interest. This approach requires careful model building and checking to ensure that the chosen model fits the data well and collaboration with domain experts to ensure the model is scientifically plausible. Due to this need for supervision, it is difficult to deploy at scale.

As a concrete example, consider the linear regression model in [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2). The multiple linear regression model for response _Y_ _i_ and covariates Xi1,...,Xip is Yi\=∑j\=1pXijβj+εi, where the errors are εi∼iid Normal(0,σ2). Fitting this model requires first selecting the response variable and the _p_ covariates to include and then verifying four key assumptions:

1. Linearity: The mean of Yi|Xi is linear in Xi1,...,Xip.
2. Equal variance: The residual variance (σ2) is the same for all _i_.
3. Normality: The errors _ε_ _i_ are Gaussian.
4. Independence: The errors _ε_ _i_ are independent.

In real analyses, most if not all of these assumptions will be violated to some extent. Minor violations will not invalidate statistical inference, but glaring model misspecifications should be addressed.

244\. Machine learning (ML) is a branch of statistics that attempts to automate this process by introducing robust statistical procedures that are less dependent on expert knowledge and make fewer statistical assumptions that require empirical verification. In this chapter, we discuss ML approaches to generalizing the Bayesian linear regression model. The first step in fitting a linear model is to select the covariates to be included. A classical analysis proceeds by fitting models sequentially and comparing their fits in combination with graphical checks. ML alternatives presented in Chapter 8.1 gather a large collection of covariates and fit a single Bayesian model that simultaneously selects important covariates and estimates their effects. This is generalized in Chapter 8.2 to identify the relationships between a large number of variables using graphical models. The linearity assumption is relaxed in Chapter 8.3 with a collection of methods that specify a prior for the mean response that is potentially a non-linear functions of the covariates. Moving from the mean to the residuals, Chapter 8.4 introduces models for the residual distribution that are non-Gaussian and depend on the covariates.

These ML methods are closely related to Bayesian nonparametrics \[[62](./19-ref01.md#refbib62)\]. The classic model is called a parametric model because it assumes the only unknowns are a small number of parameters, and all other aspects of the model including linearity, Gaussian residuals, etc. are given. To avoid making invalid assumptions, a Bayesian nonparametric analysis specifies priors on functions (i.e., infinitely-many parameters) rather than the few parameters that determine the functions. For example, rather than assuming the residuals follow a Gaussian distribution with unknown variance _σ_2, a nonparametric analysis specifies a model for the residual distribution function that allows the distribution to be any valid continuous distribution function, encompassing left-skewed, right-skewed or even multi-modal distributions.

Combined with large datasets, Bayesian ML/nonparametric models often give better fidelity to the data and more accurate out-of-sample predictions than parametric models. However, a consequence of selecting a complicated approach is that the results are less interpretable. In the extreme, the statistical model becomes essentially a “black box” that produces accurate predictions but does not enhance scientific understanding of the relationships between variables. To close this gap, Chapter 8.5 provides several techniques to extract scientific meaning from fitted ML models.

Armed with these Bayesian ML methods, Chapter 8.6 demonstrates several applications ranging from causal inference to equation learning. In most of these applications, the ML methods are not used merely as black-box prediction algorithms, but rather they are incorporated in analyses to uncover information about biological or physical systems such as determining the causal effect of a treatment or the most important drivers of extreme hurricane winds.

Bayesian ML/nonparametric methods offer several advantages over optimization-based ML. These are largely the same advantages discussed in previous chapters for parametric models. The most important advantage is uncertainty quantification. Frequentist uncertainty quantification (p-values, confidence intervals) often relies on asymptotic arguments with the sample size far exceeding the number of parameters, which does not apply to nonparametric methods with infinitely-many parameters. Bayesian uncertainty quantification methods (posterior probabilities, credible intervals) maintain their validity even in nonparametric problems. Another advantage is the potential for incorporating prior information. ML methods are flexible but require large datasets for training for their potential to be realized. Prior distributions that center ML models on simple parametric models, or incorporate information from related datasets (see transfer learning methods in Chapter 8.6.4) can supplement ML methods when training data is lacking. One limitation of Bayesian ML methods is computation. Therefore, in this section we largely move away from JAGS in favor of tailored ML packages.

## 8.1 245\. High-dimensional linear regression

In classic linear regression, the sample size _n_ is much larger than the number of covariates _p_. In this case, the likelihood overwhelms the prior, and Bayesian results mirror those from least squares (Chapter 4.2). However, many modern applications use high-throughput data and have _p_ on the order of _n_ or even much larger than _n_. As an example, consider the data from \[[94](./19-ref01.md#refbib94)\] that can be downloaded from the course webpage. In this study, n\=60 mice (31 female) were sampled, and the physiological phenotype stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) is taken as the response to be regressed onto the expression levels of 22,575 genes. Following \[[19](./19-ref01.md#refbib19)\] and \[[172](./19-ref01.md#refbib172)\], we use only the p\=1,000 genes with highest pairwise correlation with the response as predictors in the model. Even after this simplification, this leaves a high-dimensional problem with p\>n.

The regression coefficients cannot all be estimated in high-dimensional problems without strong assumptions. In a Bayesian analysis, assumptions are encoded in the prior distribution. A common assumption is sparsity, which presumes that many of the regression coefficients are equal to or at least close to zero, and thus only a small subset of the covariates are needed to predict the response. In Chapter 4.2.2.3 the assumption of sparsity was induced by the Bayesian LASSO prior. This prior is a continuous PDF with a peak at the origin. While this prior gives probability zero to the regression coefficients being exactly zero, the peak shrinks parameter estimates towards zero. The Stochastic Search Variable Selection (SSVS) prior in Chapter 6.2 goes further by adding discrete point-mass distribution so that the regression coefficients have positive probability of being exactly zero. In this chapter, we more thoroughly discuss continuous and discrete shrinkage priors for high-dimensional regression.

**Continuous shrinkage priors**: The multiple linear regression model is

Yi|β,σ2∼Normal(∑j\=1pXijβj,σ2).

Denote the prior for the regression coefficients as βj∼π(β) for j\=2,...,p. Continuous shrinkage priors assume that _π_ is a continuous function of _β_, but with a large peak at zero to encourage sparsity and heavy tails to allow signals to emerge. One example is the double exponential prior in [Figure 8.1](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_1), which leads to the posterior mode being the LASSO solution (4.15).

The double exponential prior is just one example of a shrinkage prior with peak at zero and heavy tails. The horseshoe prior \[[30](./19-ref01.md#refbib30)\] is

βj|τj,σ2∼Normal(0,σ2τ02τj2) and τj∼iidHC(1)(8.1)

where τ02 is global variance common to all regression coefficients and _τ_ _j_ is a local prior standard deviation specific to _β_ _j_. The local standard deviations are given half-Cauchy priors (i.e., Student's t-distribution with location 0, scale 1 and one degree of freedom, restricted to being positive). The prior derives its name from the special case with Yi|βi,σ2∼Normal(βi,σ2) for i∈{1,...,p} and τ0\=1. In this case, the posterior mean given _τ_ _i_ is E(β|Yi)\={1−κ(τi)}Yi for κ(τi)\=1/(1+τi2). Shrinkage is controlled by κ(τi), with κ(τi)\=0 giving E(β|Yi)\=Yi and no shrinkage and κ(τi)\=1 giving E(β|Yi)\=0 and complete shrinkage. Under the half-Cauchy prior for _τ_ _i_, κ(τi)∼Beta(1/2,/1/2) which is shaped like a horseshoe with mass near zero and one, allowing for no shrinkage and complete shrinkage.

246\. The inclusion of local shrinkage parameters allows the global-local skrinkage prior the flexibility to shrink null coefficients towards zero by having small variance while the true signals to have uninformative priors with large variance. The marginal distribution of _β_ _j_ over _τ_ _j_ has a complicated expression,

πj(β|σ2,τ0)\=∫0∞ϕ(β,0,σ2τ02τj2)h(τj)dτj

where _ϕ_ is the Gaussian PDF and _h_ is the half-Cauchy PDF. However, [Figure 8.1](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_1) shows that the marginal horseshoe prior gives more (actually infinite) mass at zero and in the tails than the Bayesian LASSO prior.

The Dirichlet–Laplace prior \[[18](./19-ref01.md#refbib18)\] gives even more shrinkage towards zero by supplementing the Bayesian LASSO with local shrinkage parameters and a Dirichlet prior on the shrinkage parameters. The R2D2 prior \[[172](./19-ref01.md#refbib172)\] is another global-local shrinkage prior for the regression coefficients that is constructed so that the model's coefficient of determination (i.e., R-squared) has a beta prior as in (4.18). The R2D2 prior has the most mass at zero and heaviest tail among these priors.

## 8.2 249\. Graphical models

In contrast with linear regression where one variable is designated as the response variable of interest and the others are deemed covariates, in a graphical model, the objective is to uncover relationships between _p_ variables without prespecified roles. In this sense, graphical models are less structured and thus truer to the machine learning paradigm than linear regression.

Let Y\=(Y1,...,Yp)T be the vector of outcomes; e.g., **Y** may include the activity levels of _p_ brain regions, and the objective is to uncover functional relationships between the regions. If the interest is primarily in region _j_, then we would perform a linear regression with outcome _Y_ _j_ and p−1 covariates _Y_ _k_ for k≠j,

Yj|Yk for k≠j∼Normal(∑k≠jYkβjk,σj2).

However, in a graphical analysis, the goal is to uncover relationships among all the variables. This could be accomplished with _p_ regressions, one with each variable as the outcome and the other as the responses. However, this might lead to incoherent results, that is, since the regressions are fit separately, there may not be a valid joint distribution for all _p_ variables that gives the fitted conditional distributions.

The simplest graphical model is the multivariate normal model Y∼Normal(0,Σ). Since the goal is to study relationships between the _p_ variables, we focus on the p×p covariance matrix Σ and assume the data have been centered to have mean zero. This coherent joint model gives interpretable conditional distributions in terms of the precision matrix Ω\=Σ−1 with (j,k) element Ωjk,

Yj|Yk for k≠j∼Normal(∑k≠jYkbjk,τj2),

where bjk\=−Ωjk/Ωjj and τj2\=1/Ωjj. The conditional mean is linear in the other _Y_ _k_ with bjk determining the effect of _Y_ _k_ on _Y_ _j_ after accounting for the effects of the other p−2 variables. Therefore, fitting a multivariate normal model to **Y** is effectively equivalent to fitting _p_ linear regressions but with restrictions that the models are coherent.

The interpretation of the elements of Σ and Ω in the Gaussian graphical model are subtly different. If the (j,k) element of Σ is zero, then _Y_ _j_ and _Y_ _k_ are marginally independent; i.e., they are independent when ignoring the effects of the other variables. In contrast, if the (j,k) element of Ω is zero, then _Y_ _j_ and _Y_ _k_ are conditionally independent; i.e., they are independent after removing the effects of the other p−2 variables. Formally, _Y_ _j_ and _Y_ _k_ are said to be conditionally (on the other p−2 variables) independent if and only if Ωjk\=0, so testing whether elements of Ω are zero gives a test of conditional independence.

Under some conditions, conditional dependence can be interpreted as causal relationships (see Chapter 8.6.1) rather than simply correlation. To see this, say a particular brain activity is caused by region 1 sending a signal to region 2 that is then passed to region 3 then region 4\. This could be encoded in the model Y1∼Normal(0,1) and Yj|Yj−1∼Normal(Yj−1,1) for j∈{2,3,4}, giving covariance and precision matrices

Σ\=\[1111122212331234\] and Ω\=\[ 2−1 0 0−1 2−1 0 0−1 2−1 0 0−1 1\].

250\. Since there are no zeros in Σ, all four regions are correlated. However, the pattern of zeros in Ω reflects the causal pathway through the four regions. Therefore, viewing the variables as nodes that are connected by edges if the corresponding elements of Ω are non-zero produces a graph of the relationship between the variables, hence the term “graphical model”.

While the Gaussian graphical model provides a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between variables than a linear regression model, this comes at the expense of having to estimate the p(p−1)/2 elements in the covariance matrix as opposed to the _p_ linear regression coefficients. Therefore, a prior distribution that encodes sparsity with many elements of Ω shrunk towards zero to avoid over-fitting is even more critical here than in linear regression. The conjugate prior for Ω is the Wishart prior ([Section 2.1.7](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1_7)); however, this does not encode sparsity. The ingenious prior of \[[161](./19-ref01.md#refbib161)\] for Ω implies a Bayesian LASSO prior on the off-diagonal elements of Ω and thus encourages sparsity and permits Gibbs sampling updates for the elements of Ω.

## 8.3 Nonparametric regression

Linear regression is one of the most successful tools in all of statistics owing to its stability, ease of implementation and convenient interpretation. However, when the linear assumption is grossly violated, the linear model can suffer from poor prediction and inferential properties. In this chapter, we introduce a rich collection of models for the mean of the response distribution. The model is assumed to be

Yi\=g(Xi)+εi.(8.3)

As before, we use Gaussian errors εi∼iid Normal(0,σ2). However, the mean response, denoted as E(Y|X)\=g(X)\=g(X1,...,Xp), is now allowed to be an arbitrarily complex function of the covariates including non-linear effects and interactions between covariates. We describe the models for _g_ for an arbitrary covariate vector X\=(X1,...,Xp) but continue to use Xi\=(Xi1,...,Xip) to describe a specific covariate vector for a specific observation.

Of course, the linear model remains appealing for its simplicity and transparency, and thus it is important to consider simple modifications to the linear model before launching into a more sophisticated analysis. For example, if a linear model is found to be unsatisfactory, an alternative could be the second-order model

g(X)\=∑j\=1pXjβj+∑j\=1pXj2αj+∑j<kXjXkγjk(8.4)

that includes linear (with effects _β_ _j_), non-linear/quadratic (with effects _α_ _j_) and interaction (with effects γjk) terms.

For small _p_, the second-order model is tidy and stable. However, for even a moderately-sized problem with p\=10, the full second-order model has 65 parameters, and interpretation with quadratic and interaction effects is not straightforward. It is possible to trim the model by adding only the non-linear and interaction terms that are motivated by exploratory analysis or scientific insights, but this is somewhat subjective. It is also possible to employ statistical model building tools to prune the model, such as stochastic search variable selection (Chapter 8.1), but this is now of comparable sophistication to the non-parametric regression models that are the focus of this chapter.

251\. A fully nonparametric model allows for any continuous function _g_. That is, the target changes from a finite number of parameters (e.g., β) in a parametric analysis to a function (e.g., a curve or surface _g_) in a nonparametric analysis. A model this flexible requires infinitely many parameters. We will primarily focus on semiparametric models that specify the mean function in terms of a finite number of parameters in a way that increasing the number of parameters can approximate any function _g_. For example, if there is only p\=1 covariate (_X_), we could fit a Jth order polynomial function

g(X)\=∑j\=0JXjβj.(8.5)

This model has J+1 parameters, and by increasing _J_ the polynomial function can approximate any continuous function _g_.

We explore many Bayesian semiparametric/nonparametric regression models, including regression splines \[[36](./19-ref01.md#refbib36)\], Gaussian process regression \[[126](./19-ref01.md#refbib126)\], Bayesian adaptive regression trees \[[33](./19-ref01.md#refbib33)\] and neural networks \[[114](./19-ref01.md#refbib114)\]. While generalizations to non-Gaussian data are available (using similar tools to generalized linear mixed model in Chapter 4.5), we restrict our attention to the Gaussian case in (8.3). In this case, twice the negative log likelihood function is an irrelevant constant plus

1σ2∑i\=1n{Yi−g(Xi)}2.

Therefore, point-estimators, particularly the maximum a posteriori estimator, have similarity with optimization-based machine learning algorithms that use squared-error loss as the objective function. However, as with connection between LASSO and Bayesian LASSO regression in Chapter 4.2, the interpretation and uncertainty quantification of the Bayesian methods differs from optimization-based approaches.

**Hurricane wind forecasting application:** We illustrate and compare nonparametric regression methods using the data in \[[6](./19-ref01.md#refbib6), [35](./19-ref01.md#refbib35)\]. The data consist of forecasted and observed tropical cyclone intensity, defined as the maximum 1-min average 10-m wind speed (knots), for 120 storms in the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans from 2014 to 2017\. Two baseline methods are available for comparison. The first is the Hurricane Weather Research and Forecasting (HWRF) prediction. HWRF is a physics-based numerical model that takes current conditions as inputs and predicts the path and intensity of the storm. The second baseline is the forecast from the National Hurricane Center (NHC). Both predictions are made for lead times (hours) l∈{3,6,...,48}, i.e., the prediction made on one day for the same time the following day is the 24-hour lead-time prediction. Excluding missing observations, across storm and lead time there are 37,207 observations. [Figure 8.4](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_4) plots the mean absolute error ∑i\=1n|Yi−Y^i|/n for each baseline method and lead time; as expected the error increases with lead time.

In spline regression, we construct non-linear functions of the original covariates and use these constructed covariates as the predictors in multiple linear regression. Selecting the number of basis function, _J_, is a critical step in fitting a spline model. By increasing _J_, any smooth mean function can be approximated as a linear combination of the B-spline basis functions, but large _J_ risks over-fitting. One approach to selecting _J_ is to assume uninformative priors, βj∼iidNormal(0,σ2τ2) with _τ_2 a large fixed constant, and then compare several _J_ using model selection criteria such as cross-validation, DIC or WAIC. Another approach is to set _J_ to be large, e.g., J\=n/2, and allow the prior distribution for the _β_ _j_ to prevent over-fitting via τ∼HC(1). Especially when _J_ is large, over-fitting can be guarded against by specifying a prior distribution that smooths across the splines coefficients,

βj|βj−1∼Normal(ϕβj−1,σ2τ2)

where ϕ∈(0,1) and _τ_2 are both given prior distributions to allow the data to determine the amount of prior shrinkage.

**Motorcycle example**: To fit the mean curve to the data plotted in [Figure 8.5a](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_5), we use J\=10 B-spline basis functions and priors βj∼Normal(0,τ2σ2) and σ2,τ2∼InvGamma(0.1,0.1). The model is fit using MCMC with the code in [Listing 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#list4_2). In this code, the basis functions have been computed using the bs package in R and passed to JAGS as Xij\=Bj(Xi). For each iteration, we compute g(Xi) for all i\=1,...,n as a function of that iteration's posterior sample of β. This produces the entire posterior distribution of the mean function _g_ for all _n_ sample points (or any other _X_ we desire), and [Figure 8.5a](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_5) plots the posterior median and 95% interval of _g_ at each sample point. The fitted model accurately captures the main trend including the valley around X\=0.4.

254\. **Monotonic regression:** Nonparametric regression is used when the true form of the mean function is unknown. However, it can also incorporate partial knowledge. For example, if _X_ is the dose of drug and _Y_ is the response, then we might expect the dose-response curve E(Y|X)\=g(X) to be an increasing function of _X_. Monotonicity is easily accommodated using Bayesian splines regression. Using a B-spline basis expansion for _g_, the mean curve is increasing so that g(X+ϵ)\>g(X) for all _X_ and ϵ\>0 if (though not only if) the regression coefficients are increasing, β1<...<βJ. This restriction is straightforward to implement by reparameterizing the coefficients as βj\=βj−1+δj for j\>1 and specifying a prior distribution for the _δ_ _j_ with positive support, e.g., a gamma or truncated normal distribution. A Bayesian analysis including uncertainty quantification proceeds as usual, although depending on the prior distribution for _δ_ _j_ a Metropolis-Hastings step may be required. Other shape-restricted regression models are available \[[105](./19-ref01.md#refbib105)\] to model curves that are decreasing, convex, unimodal, etc.

**Generalized additive models:** There are many ways to generalize spline regression to the multivariate case with p\>1 covariates. The most straightforward to implement and interpret is the generalized additive model (GAM) \[[166](./19-ref01.md#refbib166)\]. The main-effects-only GAM model represents the mean as the sum of _p_ nonlinear functions,

g(X)\=β0+∑j\=1pgj(Xj)(8.6)

where _g_ _j_ is a non-linear function the jth covariate. The non-linear function is interpreted analogously to linear regression coefficients; with all other covariates fixed, the mean response increases following gj(Xj) as _X_ _j_ increases.

Each curve can be modeled using a spline basis expansion,

gj(Xj)\=∑k\=1JBjk(Xj)βjk(8.7)

where Bj1,...,BjJ are spline basis functions and βjk∼indepNormal(0,σ2τj2) are the basis coefficients. Inserting (8.7) into (8.6) gives a standard Bayesian linear regression with constructed covariates Bjk(Xj) and regression coefficients βjk. The covariate-specific variance components, τj2, allow for a differential amount of smoothing by covariate and can be given inverse gamma or half-Cauchy prior distributions. If _p_ is large, Bayesian variable selection methods can be applied to simplify the model by shrinking irrelevant covarates. For example, \[[162](./19-ref01.md#refbib162)\] use a continuous shrinkage priors with differential shrinkage by covariate.

The main-effects-only GAM model includes non-linear effects, but not interactions. Following (8.4), the GAM model can be extended to include interactions

g(X)\=β0+∑j\=1pgj(Xj)+∑j<lgjl(Xj,Xl)(8.8)

where gjl is the interaction function for covariates _j_ and _l_. The interaction function is a surface, rather than a curve, because it returns a value for any combination of the two covariates. The interaction surfaces can be modeled as outer products of spline basis functions

gjl(Xj,Xl)\=∑k\=1J∑h\=1JBjk(Xj)Blh(Xl)βjlkh

255\. with prior distribution βjlkh∼Normal(0,σ2τjl2). As the number of basis functions increase, any smooth two-dimensional surface can be approximated using this representation. Extensions to three-way interactions and higher are possible, but are computationally demanding.

With the basis functions serving as covariates, both the main-effects or interactions models can be fit using standard MCMC algorithms including Gibbs updates. Posterior uncertainty about the curves _g_ _j_ naturally follows from MCMC by computing the _g_ _j_ at covariate values of interest at each MCMC iteration, and then summarizing the samples as in [Figure 8.5](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_5). However, computation can be challenging for even moderate _p_. First, the number of parameter can be large, with roughly _pJ_ parameters for the main effects and on the order of p2J2 for interactions. The computational burden can be eased by reducing the number of basis functions for the interaction terms or including only a subset of covariates for the interaction terms. Another complication is separating the effect of different covariates. If the basis functions are fixed at zero for some value of the covariates (see [Figure 8.5b](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_5)) then technically the curves can be identified. However, concurvity, a functional generalization of collinearity, makes estimation difficult. For example, if _J_ is large than either _g_1 or _g_2 can effectively interpolate the observations. Therefore, even if the correlation between _X_1 and _X_2 is moderate, g1(X1) and g2(X2) compete to explain the same signal in the data and so their posterior distributions can be highly correlated.

### 8.3.2 256\. Gaussian process regression

The spline regression model Yi|β∼indep Normal{g(Xi),σ2} with g(Xi)\=∑j\=1JBj(Xi)βj and βj∼iidNormal(0,τ2) resembles the random effects model with the _β_ _j_ serving as random effects. As discussed in Chapter 4.4, the random effects induce dependence between observations. Averaging over the prior distribution of the _β_ _j_ with p\=1 covariate,

Cov(Yi,Yk)\=τ2∑j\=1JBj(Xi)Bj(Xk).(8.9)

Therefore, spline regression can be viewed as a model for the dependence between observations with different covariate values, and the choice of basis functions determines the form of the covariance. As an example, if Bj(X)\=exp{−(X−vj)2} for a sequence of fixed values _v_ _j_ covering the range of _X_, then the sum in (8.9) is

Cov(Yi,Yk)∝∑j\=1Jexp{−(Xi−vj)2}exp{−(Xk−vj)2},

which for large _J_ converges to

Cov(Yi,Yk)∝∫exp{−(Xi−v)2}exp{−(Xk−v)2}dv∝exp{−η(Xi−Xk)2}

257\. Figure on this page. 258\. for some constant _η_. The correlation between two observations thus decays with the distance between their covariate values.

Gaussian process (GP) regression \[[126](./19-ref01.md#refbib126)\] views _g_ as a stochastic process over covariate space. That is, it is not approximated using a finite number of basis functions, but rather it can be viewed as the process that arises in the limit as the number of basis function tends to infinity for a specific sequence of basis functions. This is accomplished by modeling its covariance directly as in Chapter 4.6\. The function _g_ is modeled as a GP, defined as a process such that for any _N_ covariate values X1,...,XN the random vector g\={g(X1),...,g(XN)}T follows a multivariate normal distribution. The definition that a GP applies to any set of locations is key because in practice we will define a joint model for the _n_ observed data points and an unknown set of N−n prediction points.

The Gaussian process is defined by the mean and covariance functions used to determine the mean vector and covariance matrix of **g**. The simplest mean function is E{g(Xi)}\=μ for all Xi, although it can also be a linear function of the covariates. Similar to the spatial and temporal covariance functions in [Section 4.6](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_6), the covariance functions is defined by distance between the covariate vectors of two observations, dik\={∑j\=1p(Xij−Xkj)2}1/2. Since this distance weights all _p_ covariates equally it is said to be isotropic; an anisotropic extension is dik\={∑j\=1pwj(Xij−Xkj)2}1/2 where wj\>0 are weights and can be given prior distributions to allow the data to determine the best weighting scheme. The simplest covariance function is the Gaussian (squared exponential) covariance function

Cov{g(Xi),g(Xk)}\=τ2exp{−(dik/ϕ)2},

where _τ_2 is the variance (since τ2exp{−(d/ϕ)2}\=τ2 if d\=0) and ϕ\>0 determines the decay of the correlation as a function of the distance between Xi and Xk. Other covariance functions are available including the Matérn function \[[38](./19-ref01.md#refbib38)\].

The mean and covariance functions define the joint distribution of **g**. Assuming the constant mean and isotropic Gaussian covariance function (as we do throughout), the marginal distribution of g(Xi) is Normal(μ,τ2) for all _i_ and the joint distribution is

g|μ,Σ∼ Normal(μ,Σ)(8.10)

where μ\=(μ,...,μ)T is the mean vector of length _n_ and Σ is the n×n covariance matrix with (i,k) element equal to τ2exp{−(dik/ϕ)2}.

The Bayesian model is completed with priors for hyperparameters. The mean and variance parameters can be given prior distributions as in linear mixed models, e.g., μ∼Normal(0,c2) for large scale parameter _c_ and σ,τ∼HC(1) or σ2,τ2∼InvGamma(ϵ,ϵ). The prior for the spatial range parameter _ϕ_ depends on the scale of the dik. That is, if the units of distance change, so should the prior for _ϕ_. One way to account for this is the prior ϕ∼Uniform(0,dmax) where dmax\=max{dik} is the maximum of the observed distances between observations.

Given the likelihood Yi|g∼indepNormal{g(Xi),σ2} and prior in (8.10), MCMC can be used to sample from the posterior distribution of **g** and the parameters θ\=(μ,σ2,τ2,ϕ). Assuming conjugate priors, the updates for **g**, _μ, σ_2 and _τ_2 are simple Gibbs updates; however, since _ϕ_ appears inside the covariance matrix there is no conjugate prior and a Metropolis step is required. While the algorithm used to draw samples is familiar, MCMC is notoriously slow for GP regression even with moderate _n_ (say n\>1,000) because each step requires manipulating the covariance matrix Σ. This bottleneck can be alleviated by fixing _ϕ_ (e.g., using maximum likelihood methods) in advance so that all matrix manipulation can be done outside of the MCMC algorithm. Even after this concession, GP regression is slow and requires large memory allocation for large _n_ (say n\>10,000).

259\. In some cases, the objective of the analysis is to estimate the mean **g** and MCMC can return samples of **g** for this purpose. However, if the goal of the analysis is to estimate the parameters θ to make predictions, then **g** can be marginalized over to give the distribution of the observed data Y\=(Y1,...,Yn)T given θ as the likelihood function. The marginal distribution of **Y** is

Y|θ∼Normal(μ,ΣY)(8.11)

where ΣY\=Cov(Y|θ)\=σ2In+Σ. MCMC then proceeds using (8.11) as the likelihood function and gives the same posterior for θ but often converges faster than the algorithm that includes **g** since there are fewer parameters to estimate.

One of the main advantages of GP regression is provably optimal predictions. Denote _Y_0 as the prediction for an observation with covariate X0. Then the mean and covariance functions can be used to define the n+1 dimensional multivariate normal distribution for _Y_0 and **Y**, and the properties of the multivariate normal distribution give the prediction distribution

Y0|Y,θ∼Normal{μ+Σ0TΣY−1(Y−μ),σ2+τ2−Σ0TΣY−1Σ0},(8.12)

where Σ0 is the vector of length _n_ with ith element equal Cov(Y0,Yi)\=τ2exp{−(di0/ϕ)2}. A similar expression gives the posterior distribution for only the mean at X0,

g(X0)|Y,θ∼Normal{μ+Σ0TΣY−1(Y−μ),τ2−Σ0TΣY−1Σ0},(8.13)

which is identical to (8.12) except the error variance _σ_2 is excluded from the prediction variance. The mean of the prediction distribution in (8.12) is known as the Kriging prediction, and is the best linear unbiased predictor assuming the mean and covariance functions are correct. Of course, in practice the mean and covariance functions will be unknown and so there is no guarantee the predictor is optimal, but this result at least gives a path forward: using exploratory analysis or model comparisons we try to identify a reasonable covariance function and then use Kriging for theoretically-justified prediction.

[Figure 8.8](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_8) plots a synthetic dataset with p\=1 covariate and n\=25 observations along with GP predictions and prediction intervals (connected by lines) for a sequence of X0 covering the range of the observed Xi. Here the parameters are set to μ\=10, σ2\=0.2, τ2\=2 and ϕ\=0.05. The predicted values effectively smooth over the observed responses, and the prediction intervals are wider where there is less data. In a real data analysis, the parameters θ would be unknown and rather than make prediction for one fixed value of θ we would make a prediction for each MCMC sample of θ, thereby averaging over parametric uncertainty and giving the posterior predictive distribution p(Y0|Y).

### 8.3.3 Bayesian additive regression trees

The GAM approach of Chapter 8.3.1 represents the regression function _g_ as a linear combination of simple spline basis functions. However, to capture non-linearity and interaction effects requires a huge number of basis functions which is prohibitive for large datasets. Bayesian adaptive regression trees (BART) \[[33](./19-ref01.md#refbib33)\] also represents _g_ as the sum of simple functions, but with BART these simple functions are trees with parameters that adapt to the data so that fewer terms are required to give a sufficient approximation.

The fundamental unit of BART is a regression tree. Trees are relatively simple structures that can capture non-linear relationships and interaction effects. The covariate space is partitioned into intervals (“terminal nodes”) and each interval has a different mean response. 260\. For example, the tree on the left of [Figure 8.9](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_9) is defined mathematically as

tree(X;T,μ)\={μ1x3<c1 and x7<c2μ2x3<c1 and x7\>c2μ3x3\>c1.(8.14)

![Two regression trees are drawn side by side. Each tree splits observations using threshold rules on covariates X with cut points C. The left tree first splits on X₃ at C₁, then divides the left branch on X₇ at C₂, producing terminal nodes µ₁ and µ₂, with µ₃ on the right branch. The right tree splits on X₉ at C₁, then on X₂ at C₂ on the left branch, and on X₄ at C₃ and X₂ at C₄ on the right branch, yielding five terminal means µ₁ through µ₅.](./images/fig8_9.jpg)

Figure 8.9 **Regression trees.** Two hypothetical regression trees to be used in BART. [Return to text.⏎](chapter8)

The tree is a function of the tree structure, _T_, that includes the number of branches (m\=3) and the variables (_x_3 and _x_7) and cutpoints (_c_1 and _c_2) used to define each branch, and the values at each of the _m_ terminal nodes, μ\=(μ1,...,μm). Although the notation to precisely define _T_ is complicated, the ideas is that the function is defined sequentially through simple “splits” determined by a covariate and a cutpoint, such as x3\>c1. Each split results in two “branches”, and when splits are defined sequentially the branches form the tree. When a covariate vector **X** is evaluated, based on the splits it falls in one of the terminal nodes where it is a assigned a value from μ. To fit the data well, both _T_ and μ must be learned from the data.

Unlike GP regression or even GAMs, the tree structure is appealing because the entire mean function can be written in a simple form and visualized as in [Figure 8.9](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_9). However, it is unlikely that a single tree provides a good fit for complex data, i.e., the tree above takes only m\=3 values which is too simple for practical application. However, it has been shown that taking the average over several trees (“weak learners”) gives a rich modeling framework. The most common approach for aggregating trees in the machine learning literature is random forest regression; below we describe BART.

261\. The BART model for the mean response function aggregates over _J_ trees

g(X)\=∑j\=1Jtree(X;Tj,μj),(8.15)

where _T_ _j_ is the structure of the jth tree and μj is the collection of mean values for each of the _m_ _j_ terminal nodes in _T_ _j_. BART is a fully-Bayesian model and thus requires prior distributions for _T_ _j_ and μj. The prior distributions are independent across the _J_ trees. The prior for the number of branches is defined sequentially; as the tree grows, the probability that each branch splits into two is α(1+d)−β where _d_ is the depth of the tree and _α_ and _β_ are hyperparameters. The covariate that defines each split has Uniform({1,...,p}) prior and the cutpoint _c_ _j_ has uniform prior over the observed covariate values. Finally, the mean parameters μj have Gaussian priors with mean zero and variance chosen based on the scale of the data.

BART is fit using MCMC with _T_ _j_ and μj as the unknown parameters. An advantage of using MCMC is that posterior draws can be used to obtain posterior credible intervals for g(X) and prediction intervals that account for both the error variance _σ_2 and uncertainty in g(X). BART also performs variable selection in a similar fashion as stochastic search variable selection (Chapter 6.2). A variable is excluded from the model if it is not included in any of the splits for any of the trees, and MCMC returns an approximation of the posterior probability of this event. An alternative is the proportion of the trees that include the variable, and MCMC also returns the posterior distribution of this variable importance measure.

### 8.3.4 Deep learning

Deep learning has revolutionized ML with its ability to process large datasets and analyze and generate complex data structures such as images and text \[[65](./19-ref01.md#refbib65)\]. In this section, we will introduce the basic concepts of deep learning through the nonparametric regression problem in (8.3). The common deep learning model for this task is a feed-forward neural network (FFNN) (also known as multi-layer perceptron). After introducing the FFNN, we focus on 263\. Bayesian implementation and its advantages \[[114](./19-ref01.md#refbib114)\] including proper uncertainty quantification for prediction and variable importance. We then very briefly discuss extensions to more complex data structures such as text and images.

One way to approach DL is as a generalization of GAMs in [Section 8.3.1](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_3_1). In the main effects GAM model in (8.6) is g(X)\=β0+∑j\=1pgj(Xj). The functions _g_ _j_ are interpretable as the effect of _X_ _j_ with all other covariates held fixed. While interpretable, this makes the tenuous assumption that the mean response is additive in the covariates and breaks down with large _p_ because estimating _p_ non-linear functions requires many parameters.

A simple way to avoid the curse of dimensionality is the single-index model \[[29](./19-ref01.md#refbib29)\]. As in linear regression, the covariates are reduced to a single index, b+∑j\=1pXjWj, that enters the mean through a non-linear function _A_,

g(X)\=β0+A(b+∑j\=1pXjWj)(8.16)

where _A_ can be modeled using spline basis functions as in a GAM. This is efficient because adding an additional covariate requires adding only one additional element to W\=(W1,...,Wp). Obviously, if _A_ is the identity function, then the model reduces to Bayesian linear regression with regression coefficients _W_ _j_.

Despite its efficiency, the single-index model has limitations. The first is identifibility caused by needing to estimate both _A_ and **W**. For example, doubling **W** and replacing A(x) with A(x/2) gives the function g(X). This is usually resolved by restricting the shape and scale of _A_ (via restrictions on the spline basis function coefficients as discussed in [Section 8.3.1](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_3_1)). More critically, this model is not nonparametric because not all mean functions can be expressed in terms of a single index.

A multiple-index model generalizes the single-index model to have _L_ indices each with a different set of regression coefficients

Zl(X)\=Al(b1l+∑j\=1pXjW1lj).

Since it is difficult to estimate all _L_ non-linear functions, they can all be taken to be the same, Al\=A for all _l_. Further, to avoid complications in estimating _A_ with (restricted) spline regression, it can be taken as a simple known function, e.g., the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) function A(x)\=(x)+, i.e., A(x)\=x if _x_ is positive and A(x)\=0 otherwise ([Figure 8.11](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_11)). The mean is then linear in the indices to allow them to have differential importance,

![The left plot shows three activation functions A b plus W X with different slope and intercept combinations, each forming a piecewise linear shape that stays near zero for negative X and increases linearly afterward. The right plot compares the true function g X with approximations using L equal to 5 and L equal to 15 basis functions; both approximations follow the overall nonlinear pattern, with L equal to 15 tracking the true curve more closely.](./images/fig8_11.jpg)

Figure 8.11 **Deep learning activation functions.** ReLU activation function A(b+WX)\=(b+WX)+ for several _b_ and _W_ (left) and approximation to the true curve g(X)\=(1+X)cos(2πX) using a linear combination of _L_ neurons. [Return to text.⏎](chapter8)

g(X)\=b2+∑l\=1LZl(X)W2l(8.17)

Zl(X)\=A(b1l+∑j\=1pXjW1lj) for l∈{1,...,L}(8.18)

This is in fact a single-layer feed-forward neural network.

An interpretation of this model is that the original covariates _X_ _j_ are transformed to _Z_ _l_ before entering the linear model. This interpretation resembles the arguments for spline 264\. basis functions Bj(X) in (8.6) and (8.7). A key difference is that in the FFNN the Bayesian model simultaneously learns the transformed variables (via the b1l and W1lj) and their effect (via _b_2 and W2l), making the FFNN more efficient for high-dimensional problems.

Terminology is often a stumbling block in connecting neural network and statistical models. In statistical linear regression, the parameters _b_2 and W2l in (8.17) can be referred to as the intercept and slopes, respectively. However, when non-linear activation functions are introduced as in (8.18), this linear terminology is misleading, and so the terms (in both layers) _b_ are referred to as biases and the terms _W_ are referred to as weights. Also, in a statistical model _A_ and _L_ might be referred to as the basis function and the number of basis functions, but in deep learning they are called the activation function and number of neurons. This stems from a neuroscience analogy. If we take the activation function to be the ReLU function then we can think of Zl(X) as the output of a neuron that fires in response to a particular stimulus, and the information from this stimulus in the data as measured b1l+∑j\=1pXjW1lj. If the information is negative, the neuron does not fire and Al(X)\=0, but once the information exceeds zero, the neuron fires with strength proportional to the information.

According to the universal approximation theorem \[[81](./19-ref01.md#refbib81)\], under general conditions the model in (8.17)-(8.18) can approximate any smooth function g(X) for large enough _L_, and is thus a nonparametric model. This is illustrated by [Figure 8.11](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_11), which plots the true mean function g(X)\=(1+X)cos(2πX) by single input _X_ (so p\=1). This smooth curve is approximated by a deep FFNN with _L_ neurons and ReLU activation function. For illustration, the input layer weights are set to Wlj\=1, and biases are a grid of _L_ values spanning \[−1,1\], so −1\=b1<...<bL\=1, and the output layer parameters _b_0 and W0l are set to least squares estimates; of course, in reality all of these parameters would be estimated using MCMC. The fitted curves are piecewise linear functions, and it is clear that increasing 265\. _L_ and selecting the weights and biases appropriate any smooth curve can be approximation by a curve with form (8.17)-(8.18). Further, for large _L_ and assuming the weights W0l are Gaussian, arguments similar to those below (8.9) suggest that _g_ approximates a Gaussian process with covariance function that depends on the activation function \[[95](./19-ref01.md#refbib95)\].

While a single-layer FFNN in (8.17)-(8.18) is nonparametric in that it possesses the universal approximation property, it has been found that empirical performance can be improved by adding more layers, making it a _deep_ learning model. This is a familiar concept in Bayesian methods where layers are often added to hierarchical models to add flexibility (Chapters 2.2 and 7.1). Adding another layer gives

g(X)\=b3+∑k\=1L2Z2k(X)W3k(8.19)

Z2k(X)\=A(b2k+∑l\=1L1Z1l(X)W2kl) for k∈{1,...,L2}(8.20)

Z1l(X)\=A(b1l+∑j\=1pXjW1jl) for l∈{1,...,L1}.(8.21)

In this model, (8.21) is called the input layer because it is the initial transformation of the inputs, **X**. The final layer (8.19) is called the output layer because it gives the final output of the network. The middle layer (8.20) is called the hidden layer because it does not directly involve either the covariates or response. Additional hidden layers can be added in the obvious way.

As with any Bayesian analysis, the model parameters must be given prior distributions (for an extensive review, see \[[50](./19-ref01.md#refbib50)\]). Selecting informative prior distributions for the weights and biases is challenging because these parameters not interpretable. For example, in (8.19), the weights W3k resemble linear regression effects of the covariates Z2k, but the Z2k are actually obscure combinations of the original _p_ covariates. Further, the parameters are invariant to label switching; e.g., if Z21(X) and _W_31 are exchanged with Z22(X) and _W_32, the function g(X) is unchanged.

Given that the individual parameters are not of interest, but rather represent a distribution of weights, it is natural to model them (and similarly for the biases) as random effects (Chapter 4.4): W3k∼iidNormal(0,τ32), W2kl∼iidNormal(0,τ22) and W1jl∼iidNormal(0,τ12). Although the individual weights are not interpretable, it is still possible to perform Bayesian variable selection because covariate _j_ is removed from the model if W1j1\=0 for all _l_. This motivates a grouped discrete or continuous shrinkage priors such as the horseshoe prior \[[63](./19-ref01.md#refbib63)\] with W1jl∼iidNormal(0,τ1j2) and τ1j∼HC(1). Discrete priors can be constructed similarly, with τ1j having a prior with positive mass at τ1j\=0. Variable selection priors can also be used to select the number of neurons per layer, since if W3k\=0 for some _k_, then the effective number of neurons in the output layer is reduced by one. However, using cross-validation to select the number of layers and neurons is more common.

Computation is also challenging for neural networks due to the large number of parameters and label-switching. Fortunately, the gradient of the posterior with respect to the parameters is straight-forward to compute. This enables Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Appendix 9.4) for moderately-sized datasets. For large datasets, more advanced methods are required such as stochastic gradient MCMC and variational Bayesian methods (discussed briefly in Chapter 3.4.4).

Despite these computational challenges, the Bayesian approach has advantages over frequentist approach in terms of uncertainty quantification. Prediction uncertainty follows 266\. trivially from MCMC output, as each iteration produces a different posterior sample of _g_ and draw of the response given _g_, and thus the posterior predictive distribution accounts for both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty. Also, with the full posterior distribution of _g_, it is possible to quantify the relative importance of the each covariate and make careful probabilistic statements about which variables are the most important (see [Section 8.5](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_5)). It is also possible to incorporate information from other fitted networks \[[1](./19-ref01.md#refbib1)\], although this is more complicated than for parametric models.

Our discussion has been limited to FFNN for the classic nonparametric regression case with a scale response variable and _p_ scalar predictors. As we have seen, there are connections between the FFNN and GAM and GP regressions, and so there is no clear choice between these methods in practice. However, deep learning uniquely excels in settings where the data are not scalars and have more complex structure such as images or paragraphs of text. In these cases, different layers of the network are able to extract and combine features of the data in a computationally efficient manner. These neural networks are sophisticated with many layers and ingenious ways to process information, but the basic approach to applying Bayesian methods to analyze them is similar to the FFNN, and so we leave this as future reading.

## 8.4 Models for the residual distribution

The focus of nonparametric regression is a flexible model for the mean response. If the objective is merely a point prediction, then an accurate estimate of the mean function is likely sufficient. However, if the objective is to perform statistical inference on parameters or provide valid prediction intervals, then a model for the residual distribution is required. Checking that the assumed residual distribution fits well is part of any standard analysis. If a plot of the residuals from the estimated mean function are clearly non-Gaussian, then a transformation of the response could be performed or a non-Gaussian error distribution (e.g., Student's t-distribution) could be fit. In the spirit of machine learning, this chapter discusses two approaches to automating this process. In Chapter 8.4.1, we extend the nonparametric repression approach from the mean function to the Gaussian variance function. Chapter 8.4.2 dispenses with the Gaussian, or any other parametric error distribution, in favor of modeling the error distribution semiparametrically.

### 8.4.1 Heteroskedastic models

The standard linear regression analysis in [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2) assumes a homoskedastic variance Var(εi)\=σ2 for all _i_. A more flexible heteroskedastic model allows the covariates to affect both the mean and the variance. A natural approach is to build a linear model for the variance as a function of the covariates so that Var(εi)\=σ2(Xi). Since the variance is positive, we must transform the linear predictor to be positive before linking to the variance. For example,

σ2(Xi)\=exp(∑j\=1pXijαj).(8.22)

The parameter _α_ _j_ determines the effect of the covariate _j_ on the variance and must be estimated. JAGS code for this model is in [Listing 3](./16-chapter8.md#list8_3). The variance model does not need to be a log-linear function of covariates, and any of the nonparametric regression models for the mean can be applied to the variance. For example, in the example below we use spline regression for the variance.

### 8.4.2 Bayesian density estimation

Most Bayesian regression models assume Gaussian errors, but more flexible methods are easily constructed. For example, to accommodate heavy tails, the errors could be modeled using a Student's t- or double-exponential (Laplace) distribution. To further allow for asymmetry, generalizations such as the skew-t and asymmetric Laplace distributions would be used. [Listing 4a](./16-chapter8.md#list8_4) provides code for regression with Student's t-distribution errors.

In most analyses, a suitable parametric distribution can be found. However, this process is subjective and difficult to automate. Just as a mixture of conjugate priors ([Section 2.1.8](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1_8)) can be used to approximate virtually any prior distribution, the mixture-of-normals distribution can be used to approximate virtually any residual distribution. The mixture-of-normals density for _ε_ is

f(ε)\=∑k\=1Kπkϕ(ε;θk,σ2),(8.24)

where _K_ is the number of mixture components, πk∈(0,1) is the probability on mixture component _k_, and ϕ(ε;θ,τ2) is the Gaussian PDF with mean _θ_ and variance _τ_2. This model is equivalent to the clustering model where

Yi|gi∼Normal(∑j\=1pXijβj+θgi,σ2)(8.25)

and gi∈{1,...,K} is the cluster label for observation _i_ with Prob(gi\=k)\=πk. By letting the number of mixture components increase to infinity, any smooth distribution can be approximated, and by selecting priors that are Gaussian, inverse gamma, and Dirichlet priors for _θ_ _k_, σ2,τ2 and (π1,...,πK), respectively, all full conditional distributions for all parameters are conjugate permitting Gibbs sampling ([Listing 4b](./16-chapter8.md#list8_4)).

The most common model is the Dirichlet process mixture model that has infinitely many mixture components and a particular model for the mixture probabilities.

**Density regression:** The most flexible model for a residual distribution combines the heteroskedastic and mixture-of-normal models, giving Bayesian density regression (e.g., \[[46](./19-ref01.md#refbib46)\]). The idea is to develop a framework so that the distribution can deviate arbitrarily from normality and across the covariates. For instance, the distribution might be left-skewed for one range of the covariates but right-skewed for another. If this model can be fit, then it provides a complete understanding of the covariates' effects on the response distribution.

270\. The mixture-of-normals model in (8.24) has three types of parameters: mixture probabilities (π), means (θ) and variances (_σ_2). Any or all of these parameters could theoretically depend on the covariates, but conceptually the easiest approach is to allow the covariates to affect the mixture means

f(ε|X)\=∑k\=1Kπkϕ{ε;θk(X),σ2}.(8.26)

The mean functions _θ_ _k_ can be modeled using linear regression, θk\=Xαk, with different regression coefficients (αk) in each mixture component or using nonparametric regression methods such as spline regression. If the mean is sufficiently modeled in density regression, then the overall mean g(X) can be removed.

## 8.5 271\. Interpretable machine learning

Machine learning models are designed to be flexible and when trained with large datasets often provide more accurate predictions than classical statistical methods. However, in many cases, accurate predictions alone are insufficient, and understanding of factors that that drive the predictive model are required in order to understand and trust the prediction algorithm. More fundamentally, understanding the relationships between variables is how science advances. Balancing predictive power with understanding the fitted model is the objective of interpretable machine learning (e.g., \[[108](./19-ref01.md#refbib108)\]).

One approach to interpretable machine learning is to select a transparent model such as linear or logistic regression. However, if the transparent model fits poorly, the resulting analysis is questionable. Another approach is to fit a robust machine learning model and post-process the fitted model to elucidate the covariate effects. This is the topic of the chapter. The Bayesian approach is natural for this task. MCMC produces samples from the model parameters, and these parameters can be transformed to any quantity that summarizes the fit of the model and automatically gives the posterior distribution of this quantity. Viewed this way, interpetable machine learning is simply the analyst using the fitted Bayesian model to creatively examine relationships between variables.

Before discussing general methods for interpreting machine learning models, we first examine the trade-off between fitting a simple but incorrect linear regression model versus a complicated but robust machine learning model. Consider the synthetic data in [Figure 8.15](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_15) (left). The data are generated with a cubic relationship between the single covariate and the response, but is only slightly non-linear and perhaps close enough to linear that a simple linear regression model is an appealing choice. Fitting this model results in a one-number summary of the covariate effect (the slope) and a simple test of statistical significance 272\. (e.g., a Bayes factor). Alternatively we could fit a spline regression model which would likely give a better fit, but require tuning (selecting the number of basis functions), and results in a model with many parameters and a curve, rather than a scalar, to describe the covariate effect. However, with some post-processing of the posterior, we can still describe the covariate effect with a single value, such as the slope of the mean curve evaluated at the two endpoints, Δ\=\[g(1)−g(0)\]/\[1−0\]. In a Bayesian analysis, MCMC produces the full posterior of this interpretable quantity without having to assume the true model is linear. So, which approach to choose, fit a linear model or fit a non-linear model and summarize the posterior using interpretable quantities?

We elaborate on this bias/variance trade-off in [Chapter 9](./17-chapter9.md), but for now note that fitting the incorrect linear model has poor statistical properties.

As with selecting the prior distribution, there is no single best way to summarize the fit of a machine learning model. One general approach is to use properties of the fitted model to summarize covariate effects, such as the proportion of the trees that include each covariate in BART. While these model-specific summaries are valuable, here we focus on model-agnostic summaries that view the mean function g(X) as a black box and can thus be applied to virtually any nonparametric regression model.

**Accumulated local effects and variable importance:** The accumulated local effects (ALE) method \[[8](./19-ref01.md#refbib8)\] summarizes the effect of a covariate using the partial derivatives of the mean function g(X). Let the partial derivative of g(X) with respect to the jth covariate equal

dj(X)\=∂∂Xjg(X).

Under the linear model, g(X)\=∑j\=1pXjβj, dj(X)\=βj reduces to the usual summary of a covariate effect in linear regression.

If g(X) is non-linear or has interactions, then dj(X) is interpreted as the local (in covariate space) linear effect of _X_ _j_. That is, with all other variables fixed _a_t **X**, dj(X) is the effect of slightly increasing _X_ _j_. Since the local effect of _X_ _j_ depends on the value of all other covariates, dj(X) is a complex and uninterpretable function. To reduce the complexity of this function, the local effect (LE) simply average of these local linear effects across the distribution of the other covariates,

LEj(x)\=E{dj(X)|Xj\=x}

where the expectation is with respect to the distribution of **X** given Xj\=x. A plot of LEj(x) versus _x_ summarizes the importance of _X_ _j_; if LEj(x)≈0 for all _x_ then _X_ _j_ on average does not affect the mean response. A limitation of this summary measure is that if _X_ _j_ has a positive effect for some **X** and a negative effect for others, then these effects could offset and LEj(x)≈0 even though _X_ _j_ impacts the mean response.

To plot the covariate effects on the original scale rather than the derivative scale, the local effects are integrated (accumulated) giving the accumulated local effect (ALE)

ALE\~j(x)\=∫ljxLEj(x′)dx′,

where the support of _X_ _j_ is \[lj,uj\]. Since the ALE is a function of only _g_'s derivative, it is invariant to an additive constant. Therefore, the ALE is centered around zero by subjecting the mean given the final, centered, version

ALEj(x)\=ALE\~j(x)−E{ALE\~j(Xj)}(8.27)

where the expectation is with respect to the distribution of **X**. A plot of _X_ _j_ versus ALEj(Xj) summarizes the main effect of _X_ _j_ on the mean response; if dj(x)≈0 and ALEj(x) is flat, then this indicates that covariate _j_ is, averaging over covariate space, null. The ALE summarizes the main effect of covariate _j_ on the mean response, similar to the main effect in the GAM model in Chapter 8.3.1\. A conceptual distinction is that the ALE plot does not build a model assuming an additive structure; rather it summarizes the mean function fitted from a flexible model using the idea of a main effect.

274\. ALE can also be computed for categorical predictors and interactions. Quantifying interaction effects using ALE is very similar to the main effects. The local effect of the interaction between _X_ _j_ and _X_ _k_ is

djk(Xj,Xk)\=∂2∂Xj∂Xkg( X).

This local effect is then converted to the ALEjk(xj,xk) by taking its expected value with respect to **X** given Xj\=xj and Xk\=xk, then using bivariate integration and centering. The ALE can then be viewed as a surface over _X_ _j_ and _X_ _k_. Higher-order interaction terms can be computed following similar steps, but are difficult to compute and interpret. For categorical variables, derivatives are replaced with the difference between g(X) evaluated for different categories.

While the ALEs are useful measures of covariate effects, they are curves or surfaces, and it useful to further distill this information down to a single number to rank variables in terms of their overall importance. Intuitively, if ALEj(x) is variable across _x_ but ALEk(x) is flat, then _X_ _j_ is a more important predictor than _X_ _k_. This intuition leads to using the standard deviation of ALEj(x) as a measure of variable importance \[[68](./19-ref01.md#refbib68)\],

VIj\=SD{ALEj(Xj)}(8.28)

where the standard deviation is computed with respect to the distribution of _X_ _j_. A similar formula applies to interaction effects, but for categorical predictors the standard deviation is replaced by one fourth of the range across categories.

Computing the ALE and VI measures require differentiation and integration, which is challenging for black-box prediction methods and large datasets. Fortunately, \[[8](./19-ref01.md#refbib8)\] derive an efficient algorithm to approximate the ALE and VI, and the methods are available in the R package ALEplot \[[7](./19-ref01.md#refbib7)\]. While the details of this approximation are beyond the scope of this chapter, a key idea is that a Bayesian implementation can quantify posterior uncertainty about ALEj and VIj. The mean function _g_, and thus the derived quantities ALEj and VIj, are functions of unknown parameters (e.g., the basis function coefficients in GAM). Therefore, posterior uncertainty about ALEj and VIj can be quantified using MCMC output. As with any summary of the parameters, if ALEj and VIj are computed at several MCMC iterations, then these samples approximate their posterior distributions. These samples can then be used to give credible bands for each ALEj and credible intervals and posterior rankings of the VIj.

## 8.6 276\. Applications of Bayesian machine learning

The core machine-learning/nonparametric methods introduced above are designed to make predictions and quantify prediction uncertainty. However, these powerful methods have been creatively used for many other statistical tasks. Below we give a flavor of these applications and emphasise the strengths of the Bayesian approach in terms of quantifying uncertainty and ease of interpretation.

### 8.6.1 Causal inference

The ultimate goal of most scientific inquiry is to understand causal relationships between variables. Only a causal understanding leads to a reliable intervention strategy. The gold-standard approach for establishing causality is a controlled experiment that often relies on randomization. In a carefully-designed experiment, methods such as the Bayesian two-sample t-test (Chapter 4.1.2) are sufficient, and there is little need for advanced statistical modeling. However, due to ethical or financial considerations, not all questions can be answered with controlled experiments, and in these cases observational data is used in lieu of an experiment. This introduces many sources of potential bias and can never result in a definitive causal conclusion. However, methods from causal inference can be used to reduce bias and clearly state the assumptions needed to interpret a relationship as causal. This does require advanced statistical analysis, often incorporating machine learning methods. In this role, machine learning is not used as a black-box prediction tool; on the contrary, it is a key component of the foundational task of understanding causal mechanisms.

In this chapter, we largely follow \[[96](./19-ref01.md#refbib96)\] to give an overview of causal inference via the Rubin causal framework \[[132](./19-ref01.md#refbib132)\] and Bayesian machine learning. This framework begins with potential outcomes. Each unit (e.g., patient) is associated with a different potential outcome under two treatments that we refer to as treatment and control. For unit i\=1,...,n with covariates Xi, let Yi(0) be the potential outcome if the unit is given the control and Yi(1) be the potential outcome if the unit is given the treatment. If both of these potential outcomes were observed, then the average of Yi(1)−Yi(0) over the _n_ units would be the causal effect estimate,

Δ\=∑i\=1n{Yi(1)−Yi(0)}/n.

However, we only observe one of the two potential outcomes and the challenge is estimating the causal effect using the available data, denoted as outcome _Y_ _i_ and treatment indicator Ai∈{0,1}, where Ai\=0 indicates control and Ai\=1 indicates treatment.

There are several quantities of interest in a causal analysis. Ideally, we would estimate the treatment for individual units through the individual treatment effect ITEi\=Yi(1)−Yi(0) because this could guide interventions. This is generally too difficult because only one potential outcome is observed, so instead we might average over units with the same covariate values giving the conditional (on the covariates) average treatment effect

CATE(x)\=E{Yi(1)−Yi(0)|Xi\= x}\=g1(x)−g0(x)

where ga(x) is the expected value of Yi(a) given Xi\=x. Similar to nonparametric regression, _g_0 and _g_1 are complex functions that might be difficult to interpret. A one-number summary of the overall treatment effect is the population average treatment effect

PATE\=E{CATE(X)},

277\. where the expectation is with respect to the distribution of the covariates **X** and can be approximated as the mixed average treatment effect MATE\=∑i\=1nCATE(Xi)/n.

To connect potential outcome with standard Bayesian methods, say m\=n/2 observations are assigned to the treatment group and the rest to the control group. These data could be analyzed using the Bayesian two-sample t-test in Chapter 4.1.2\. The posterior mean estimator in (4.8) is the difference between group means, Y¯1−Y¯0 for group means Y¯1\=∑i\=1nAiYi/m and Y¯0\=∑i\=1n(1−Ai)Yi/m. To connect this estimator back to the potential outcomes, we assume that the observed treatment assignment dictates the potential outcome that is observed; i.e., if Ai\=0, then Yi\=Yi(0), and if Ai\=1, then Yi\=Yi(1). Then (recalling 1/m\=2/n)

Y¯1−Y¯0\=2n∑i\=1nAiYi(1)+(1−Ai)Yi(0).

Further assumptions are needed to relate this estimator to the causal effect.

The most robust way to estimate the casual effect is a randomized trial. Say the _m_ units given the treatment are a complete random sample from {1,...,n} so that E(Ai)\=1/2 for all _i_. Then the simple difference in sample means is an unbiased estimator of the causal effect because the expected value (where the treatment allocation A\=(A1,...,An) is the random variable) of the difference is

E(Y¯1−Y¯0)\=Δ\=∑i\=1n{Yi(1)−Yi(0)}/n.

The difference in means estimator does not use the covariates, Xi. The precision of the estimator can be increased by incorporating covariate information, but randomization breaks the dependence between covariates and treatment assignment, so simply treating the covariates as unobserved error is sufficient to obtain an unbiased estimator.

Treatments are not randomized in observational studies, and this can lead to systematic bias. Consider the extreme scenario that units are given treatment Ai\=1 if Yi(1)\>Yi(0) and Ai\=0 otherwise. This obviously induces positive bias in the estimation of the treatment effect. While this is a pathological case, similar factors may be lurking in a study where _A_ _i_ is an indicator that subject _i_ has a particular diet and _Y_ _i_ is a measure of their health. In an observational study, the subject chooses their own diet, and they may have experimented with different diets and settled on the one they found personally optimal, so that _A_ _i_ is more likely to be one if Yi(1)\>Yi(0). The goal of causal inference is to mitigate biases in observational studies to the extent possible.

Mitigating bias requires some information about its source. The most common method for dealing with bias is to assume it is explained by measured confounding variables in Xi. A variable is a confounding variable if is correlated with both the treatment variable and the response. In this approach, a causal inference can only be achieved if all confounding variables are included in the model, and thus careful consideration of the sampling design and biasing mechanism is crucial. Therefore, the first and most important step in a causal inference is to understand the problem and select appropriate confounding variables. Given that the appropriate confounding variables have been identified, they can be included in the statistical model as covariates in a linear regression or machine learning models, as described below.

To illustrate the effects of confounding variables, the two datasets in [Figure 8.17](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_17) are generated from the models:

To link the observed data and the potential outcomes and establish the role of confounding variables requires assumptions about the data-generating model. Common assumptions include:

* (A1)**Stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA)**: For each unit, the observed response equals the potential outcome dictated by the observed treatment assignment, Yi\=Yi(Ai).
* (A2)**No unmeasured confounders**: Given Xi, the treatment _A_ _i_ is independent of the potential outcomes Yi(0) and Yi(1).
* (A3)**Covariate overlap**: The propensity score e(x)\=Prob(Ai\=1|Xi\=x) is bounded away from 0 and 1 for all _i_, 0<e(Xi)<1.

Assumption A1 seems trivial, but it is key to relating the potential outcomes to the observed data. A common violation is interference/spillover, which occurs if the response for unit _i_ is affected by the treatment status of other units, such as another unit that is close to unit _i_ in space. Assumption A2 states that the observed covariates are sufficient to explain all non-random sampling of the treatment variable, so that if the covariates are incorporated correctly the results can resemble the results of a randomized experiment. Assumption A3 is required for estimation of the CATE; e.g., if e(x)\=0, then all observations with Xi\=x have Ai\=0, so g1(x) cannot be estimated without extrapolation. Generally, Assumptions A1 and A2 are not verifiable with observed data, but provide a basis to evaluate the validity of causal claims; Assumption A3 can and should be checked using the observed data.

A special case of Assumption A2 that we adopt throughout is

Yi(a)|Xi∼indepNormal{ga(Xi),σ2},

so that confounding variables determine the mean but not the variance or other aspects of the potential outcome distribution. With Assumption A1 and this special case of Assumption A2, the model for the observed data is

Yi|Ai,Xi∼indep Normal{(1−Ai)g0(Xi)+Aig1(Xi),σ2}.(8.29)

Assumption A3 implies that both g0(Xi) and g1(Xi) can be estimated because both Ai\=0 and Ai\=1 are possible for all observed Xi. Therefore, under these assumptions, if we fit a Bayesian model to estimate the parameters in (8.29), these estimates relate directly to the treatment effects CATE and MATE defined by the potential outcomes. ### 8.6.2 Precision medicine

Assuming a causal relationship is established, either through a randomized experiment or a careful observational study, then the results can be used for decision making. If a study 281\. establishes that the MATE of a treatment versus control is statistically significant, then one might recommend using this treatment. Rather than a single decision applying to the entire population, precision medicine \[[93](./19-ref01.md#refbib93)\] devises targeted recommendations (medication, dose, frequency, etc.) for specific patients or subgroups of patients defined by covariates, e.g., treatment if the patient is over 65 years old and control otherwise.

The target for precision medicine is a decision rule that recommends an action based on the covariates. Let D(X) be the decision rule for a patient with covariate **X**. The rule can be either deterministic or stochastic. If there are two treatments, and the rule is deterministic, then D(X)\=1 assigns the patient to treatment, and D(X)\=0 assigns the patient to control. If the rule is stochastic, then D(X) could be the probability of assigning the patient to the treatment. The decision rule differs from the distribution of the treatments assigned in the training data, A1,...,An. In causal inference, it is assumed that the covariate effects on treatment assignment are captured in the propensity score, Prob(Ai\=1|Xi)\=e(Xi). In precision medicine, we use these training data to estimate the causal effect, and then assign future treatment following the optimal decision rule, i.e., Prob(An+1\=1|Xn+1)\=D(Xn+1).

The treatment effect for a patient with covariates **X** is summarized by the conditional average treatment effect, CATE(X), introduced in Chapter 8.6.1\. Assuming a large value of the response variable is desirable, then all things being equal (cost, side effects, etc.), the treatment is beneficial compared to control for a patient with covariates **X** if CATE(X)\>0 and not beneficial if CATE(x)≤0. In the hypothetical nonlinear example in [Figure 8.17](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_17) (bottom right), the posterior mean of CATE(X) is positive for all **X**, but the treatment appears to be more beneficial for patients with **X** between 0.5 and 0.8\. This Bayesian analysis powered by MCMC returns not only an estimate of the CATE, but also the full posterior distribution including the posterior probability that CATE(X) is positive, denoted pCATE+(X).

The decision rule that assigns the treatment most likely to have the optimal CATE is D0(X)\=1 if pCATE+(X)\>0.5 and D0(X)\=0 otherwise. This rule is optimal in the sense that it maximizes the probability that patients are given the optimal treatment. Thompson sampling (Chapter 5.3) is a stochastic version of this rule and assigns treatment 1 with probability pCATE+(X). With a single covariate, the CATE (and thus these decision rules) can be visualized as in [Figure 8.17](./16-chapter8.md#fig8_17). However, if the treatment depends on multiple variables and is fit using a machine learning model such as BART, then it is difficult to understand and implement such a complex treatment rule.

Interpretable machine learning can be used explain the operating characteristics of a complicated decision rule, or to develop a new and simpler rule that approximates the complicated rule. Since the decision rule D(X) is a function of the covariates just like the nonlinear mean function g(X) in Chapter 8.3, methods such as the accumulated local effect in Chapter 8.15 can be used to understand the effects of individual covariates on the decision rule and rank covariates in terms of importance. An alternative to interpreting a complex rule is to devise a simpler and more interpretable rule. For example, a regression tree (Chapter 8.3.3) might approximate the complicated decision rule _D_0, e.g.,

D0(X)≈DT(X)\={1x3<c1 and x7<c20Otherwise(8.30)

where the thresholds _c_ _j_ and variables to include in the rule (_x_2 and _x_7) are selected to give the best approximation. One way to fit the tree is to compute D0(X) for the _n_ training observations and then regress D0(X1),...,D0(Xn) onto X1,...,Xn using the rpart package \[[153](./19-ref01.md#refbib153)\] in R. The decision rule can also be approximated with logistic regression to give a linear treatment rule.

282\. **Markov decision process:** Thus far we have considered a simple case where each patient in the training data produces a single data point and there is a single decision to be made for each potential patient. Optimal decision making is more challenging when data are collected and decisions are made longitudinally. This occurs routinely in medical settings where a patient visits a doctor periodically to decide whether to switch medications or change the dose of their current medication.

To formalize the problem, say training patient i∈{1,...,n} enters the study with covariates Xi that do not evolve over time. They return for regular visits, and at visit number _t_ their health response is Yit, and they are assigned treatment Ait. Treatment assignment for the training data could be observational and influenced by the covariates or the patient's health status and past treatments. The patient's history going back _m_ time points is denoted as Hit\={Xi,Yit−m,...,Yit,,Ait−m,...,Ait−1}.

The decision problem is to devise a rule to assign treatment given Hit. This is an example of a Markov decision process \[[152](./19-ref01.md#refbib152)\], where decisions are made based on available data. A Bayesian analysis begins with fitting a model for future responses given the history Hit and the current treatment Ait using the training data. The model could be

Yit+1|Hit,Ait∼indep Normal{g(Hit,Ait),σ2},(8.31)

where _g_ is modeled using a machine learning model such as the causal inference model with BART in Chapter 8.6.1\. This model can be fit using standard machine learning techniques. Because the distribution of Yit+1 depends on Yit, this accounts for longitudinal dependence. Assuming _m_ is sufficiently large, this model can even capture treatment lag effects where the treatment at time _t_ has no immediate effect but does impact future health responses. Further complicating the problem is that future performance depends on the allocation of future treatments, which may depend on yet-to-be-observed changes in health status.

Policy search is a general strategy for optimizing a treatment assignment rule that maximizes performance across the population \[[116](./19-ref01.md#refbib116)\]. Although not necessary, an interpretable approach is a parametric policy that depends only on a few tuning parameters. For example, D(Hit)\=1 with probability η(Hit) and

logit{η(Hit)}\=α0+∑j\=1PHitjαj

where Hitj are the _P_ elements in Hit and α\=(α0,...,αP) are the parameters that determine the policy. We assume that all treatments, both at the current time and at all future time steps, are assigned following this rule. This assumption makes it possible to compute the expected health response in the future and for unobserved patients.

With the assumed parametric model established, policy search boils down to finding the value of α that optimizes future health status. Defining an “optimal” policy requires defining a metric of success, i.e., the reward function. This is of course case- and user-specific, but we give an example below. Say the data consist of _n_ patients, each measured at times {1,2,...,T}. Then we might want to select the policy α that maximizes the average value over the next _L_ time steps

R(α)\=E(1nL∑i\=1n∑t\=T+1T+LYit)

where the expectation is with respect to future realizations of Yit from the posterior predictive distribution with treatment assigned according to the policy parameters α. That is, 283\. we envision realizations of the (8.31) with Ait assigned following the policy determined by α.

The reward function R(α) generally cannot be computed analytically, but, as we have seen repeatedly (Chapter 1.5), sampling from the posterior predictive distribution is straightforward using MCMC, so a Monte Carlo approximation of R(α) can be computed for any α. The optimal policy then searches for the value of α that maximizes the reward function. However, since the reward function is approximated using Monte Carlo (stochastic) sampling for each candidate α, methods from stochastic optimization \[[73](./19-ref01.md#refbib73)\] must be used to find the optimal α.

### 8.6.3 Quantile regression

Multiple linear regression models the effect of covariates on the mean response. While this leads to beautiful mathematical results that form the basis of our understanding of statistical methods, restricting the analysis to the mean response could miss important trends including covariate effects on the spread of the distribution or the likelihood of extreme values. The heteroskedastic model (Chapter 8.4.1) extends mean regression to allow covariates to affect the variance, but quantile regression goes even further. Quantile regression allows for a potentially different relationship between the covariates and each quantile of the response distribution. For example, a covariate can have a positive effect on the median (the 0.5 quantile) but a negative effect on the spread (the interquartile range is the difference between the 0.75 and 0.25 quantiles) and extreme values (the 0.99 quantile). Therefore, quantile regression offers a systematic and comprehensive way to study covariate effects.

For τ∈(0,1), the _τ_ quantile given covariates **X** is defined as the value q(τ;X), so that

Prob{Y<q(τ;X)}\=τ.

For instance, setting τ\=0.5 gives the median of _Y_ given **X**. Linear quantile regression assumes q(τ;X)\=Xβτ, so that βτ gives the effects of the covariates on the _τ_ quantile, which is allowed to vary by quantile level. A typical analysis estimates the regression coefficients separately for several quantile levels, e.g., τ∈{0.1,0.2,...,0.9} to study how the covariate varies by quantile level.

Classical linear quantile regression (e.g., \[[92](./19-ref01.md#refbib92)\]) is framed as an optimization problem. The estimator for quantile level τ∈(0,1) is the minimizer of the check loss

β^τ\=argminβ∑i\=1nρτ(Yi−Xiβ)(8.32)

where ρτ(x)\=τx if x≥0 and ρτ(x)\=(τ−1)x if x<0. An informative special case is median regression with τ\=0.5, which simplifies to

β^0.5\=argminβ∑i\=1n|Yi−Xiβ|.(8.33)

Analogous to the robustness of the median compared to the mean as an estimator of the center of a distribution, quantile regression can be shown to be more robust to outliers than mean regression.

This idea can be extended to the Bayesian setting \[[170](./19-ref01.md#refbib170)\] by assuming the observations follow the asymmetric Laplace distribution (see [Appendix A.1](./18-appA.md#secA_1)). The model is

Yi|βτ∼indepASL(Xiβτ,σ,τ),(8.34)

284\. where βτ and σ\>0 are parameters to be estimated and _τ_ is fixed. This distribution is a convenient choice for Bayesian quantile regression because both the mode and _τ_\-quantile level are Xiβτ. The connection with classical quantile regression is that assuming a flat prior, the maximum a posteriori estimator of βτ is exactly (8.32). However, unlike the classical estimator, this Bayesian approach specifies a full likelihood, and therefore it is important to check the fit of the model before trusting the results.

Applying the model in (8.34) for several values of _τ_ can give the posterior distribution of several βτ and can shed light on the covariate effects at different quantile levels. However, it is unlikely that the asymmetric Laplace distribution fits well for all quantile levels (it is right skewed for τ<0.5 and left skewed for τ\>0.5), and a separate analysis by _τ_ does not preserve quantile ordering that Xβτ1<Xβτ2 if τ1<τ2. Another option is to fit the quantile function simultaneously for all quantile levels with Xβτ and βτ modeled using splines or a Gaussian process that assures quantile ordering (\[[127](./19-ref01.md#refbib127), [169](./19-ref01.md#refbib169)\]). However, this leads to computational challenges, and assuming all quantiles are linear in the covariates is likely too restrictive.

Rather than assume a linear quantile regression model, the Semi-Parametric Quantile Regression (SPQR) approach of \[[167](./19-ref01.md#refbib167)\] fits a density regression model similar to (8.26) that makes no assumptions about the structure of the quantiles, and then uses ideas from interpretable machine learning (Chapter 8.15) to estimate quantile effects. This approach gives a flexible model that separates model fitting from interpretation and automatically preserves quantile ordering. This approach is appealing because it avoids strong assumptions about the data-generating models and provides a comprehensive study of covariate effects across quantile levels with full Bayesian uncertainty quantification.

The density regression model is

f(y|X)\=∑k\=1Kπk(X)Bk(y)(8.35)

where the the mixture weights _π_ _k_ are modeled using a neural network and the _B_ _k_ are m-spline basis functions. The simplest network is a one-layer FFNN for the probabilities πk(X),

πk(X)\=exp{b2k+∑l\=1LW2klZl(X)}∑h\=1Kexp{b2h+∑l\=1LW2hlZl(X)}

with ReLU activation function Zl(X)\=(b1l+∑j\=1pW1ljXj)+. This formulation ensures that the probabilities are positive and sum to one, ∑k\=1Kπk(X)\=1, and since the m-spline basis functions are proper density functions, f(y|X) is a proper density for any value of the weights and biases. Letting the number of basis functions _K_ and the width of the neural network _L_ increase, this density function can approximate any continuous density function of Y|X. This gives an arbitrarily flexible model from the quantile function q(τ;X), which can be computed from (8.35).

The effects of coviarates on the quantile function are measured using the accumulated local effects and variable importance metrics introduced in Chapter 8.15\. For a given quantile level _τ_, these measures are computed for q(τ;X) (i.e., g(X)\=q(τ;X) is taken as the function to be summarized). This is repeated for several quantile levels of interest, giving estimates with posterior uncertainty of each covariate's effect on each quantile level.

### 8.6.4 Transfer learning

Machine learning via nonparametric regression provides greater flexibility than traditional parametric modeling, but often requires large datasets for adequate training. Transfer learning (TL) is a possible remedy. In TL, the information from a related analysis (or analyses) with ample data (called the source) are leveraged to improve the analysis of interest 286\. Figure/Table on this page. 287\. with sparse data (called the target). Canonical examples include using data from a large metropolitan hospital to supplement data from a small rural hospital, or using classifiers trained on images from ImageNet \[[43](./19-ref01.md#refbib43)\] to assist in tumor classification for a rare type of cancer. The Bayesian framework is natural for transfer learning. Informative priors are an obvious way to incorporate the results from related analyses, and hierarchical models for multiple datasets are a means of borrowing information. In this section, we briefly define TL notation and terminology and discuss some methods for TL with Bayesian deep learning.

Although TL can be used for other tasks, for simplicity we restrict our discussion to regression with a single source dataset. We adopt the notation of \[[149](./19-ref01.md#refbib149)\], who define a regression of **X** onto **Y** by its domain and task: the domain, D\={X,P}, consists of the support X so that X∈X and the distribution of **X**, _P_; the task, T\={Y,g}, consists of the support of **Y** so that Y∈Y, and _g_ is the true regression function g(X)\=E(Y|X). TL borrows information from the source domain and task, D1\={X1,P1} and T1\={Y1,g1}, to the target domain and task, D0\={X0,P0} and T0\={Y0,g0}.

The simplest example is linear regression with the same domains but different regression functions, gj(X)\=Xβj, with β1 and β0 the true regression coefficients in the source and target distributions. An obvious two-stage approach is to use the source data to estimate β1 and then use this estimate as the prior mean for _β_0; \[[2](./19-ref01.md#refbib2)\] show that adopting a continuous shrinkage prior centered on the source-task estimator can lead to improvements over using only the target data.

TL is more complex when the domain and/or task differ between the source and target. Returning to the hospital example, say the metropolitan and rural hospitals have the same _p_ covariates, so X0\=X1\=Rp, but the metropolitan hospital has a continuous measure of the health outcome, so Y1\=R, and the task is linear regression g1(X)\=Xβ1, but the rural hospital has a binary response, so Y0\={0,1}, and the task is logistic regression logit{g0(X)}\=Xβ0. How to combine these two disparate data sources? There is not a single path forward, and the analyst must use subject-matter knowledge and model-building techniques to arrive at a suitable approach. One possibility is a stochastic search variable selection model (as in [Section 8.1](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_1)) that analyzes both datasets simultaneously with the same binary inclusion indicators but different non-zero coefficients for the source and target models. TL is even more challenging when the two hospitals have different covariates and thus X0≠X1. Unless there is some common structure between the two domains, this may be beyond the scope of TL.

Deep learning is one of the most common applications of TL because these models have many parameters and thus require large training data. Consider the feed-forward neural network in (8.19) with the same domains, but potentially different parameters (weights and biases), for the source and target. A two-stage approach might first compute a point estimate, say MAP estimates (see (1.39)) for the parameters using the source data, denoted W\~ (and similar for the biases), and then use these estimates as priors W3k∼iidNormal(W\~3k,τ32), W2kl∼iidNormal(W\~2kl,τ22), and W1jl∼iidNormal(W\~1jl,τ12). This reduces the problem to specifying the variances τl2 or their priors. One approach is to freeze certain layers by setting some of the τl2\=0. For example, fitting τ12\=τ22\=0 sets W1jl\=W\~1jl and W2kl\=W\~2kl, leaving only the output-layer parameters W3k to be fit using the target data. This assumption that the latent structure of the regression model is shared across tasks reduces the TL problem to the linear regression model above.

Freezing layers is a drastic simplification, but it is unlikely that the latent structure is identical in the source and target, and so it may be preferable to specify priors for the variances. The prior should have some mass near zero to retain the information from the source data, but it is challenging to determine an appropriate prior for deep learning because of the large number of uninterpretable parameters. \[[1](./19-ref01.md#refbib1)\] propose a penalized complexity 288\. prior ([Section 2.4.5](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_4_5)) that treats the source model as the base model and places an exponential prior on the KL divergence between the target and source models. However, this is computationally intensive, and so it may be preferable to simply select the prior based on cross-validation or some other measure of fit.

Transfer learning is an emerging field with novel methods developing for the challenging cases of transferring information between regressions with different domains and tasks. In this chapter we have only scratched the surface. We refer to \[[149](./19-ref01.md#refbib149)\] for a comprehensive review.

### 8.6.5 Equation learning

Thus far we have used ML methods to estimate statistical quantities including the mean function in a regression model. In this context, ML models are often described as black boxes that provide excellent predictions but do not contribute to scientific understanding of the relationship between variables. However, ML methods are increasingly being used in science and engineering to study, and even uncover, the mathematical equations that govern physical and biological systems, thus bringing new scientific insights.

A classic example is an engineer designing a wind turbine to maximize efficiency. The mathematical model (i.e., differential equations based on physical laws) might depend on some independent variables, **X**, such as the height of the turbine and length of the blades, and some unknown parameters, θ, such as average wind speed in the region or the strength of one of the turbine's components. Given these values, the efficiency of the turbine is a deterministic function g(X,θ) that follows from a set of trusted differential equations. The engineer may use this model to predict performance in a new setting, estimate the parameters θ that cannot be directly measured, or find the design values **X** that optimize performance.

In some cases, data is collected to learn about unknown parameters θ or to evaluate and correct for an imperfect model _g_. A Bayesian analysis is appealing for this task because uncertainty in the parameters or the true physical model can be propagated to the final prediction, but standard MCMC methods are prohibitive because it would require thousands of evaluations of _g_. Say _n_ observations are collected with (Xi,Yi). A model for these data is \[[90](./19-ref01.md#refbib90)\]

Yi\=g(Xi,θ)+d(Xi)+ϵi(8.36)

where _g_ is the mathematical model output, _d_ is the discrepancy/bias between the model and the data and ϵi∼iidNormal(0,σ2) is measurement error. The unknowns to be learned from the data are the model parameters θ, the discrepancy function _d_ and the error variance _σ_2. Prior distributions for θ and _σ_2 are ideally based on scientific knowledge, but can also be uninformative. The discrepancy term _d_ is a function and this is modeled using nonparametric regression, e.g., a Gaussian process prior.

If the model _g_ were easy to compute, then Bayesian inference could proceed as usual with, say, MCMC to estimate the unknown parameters. If a Metropolis update is used for θ, then each iteration would propose a candidate for θ and then evaluate the model g(Xi,θ) for all _n_ observations to compute the acceptance probability. However, if each evaluation of _g_ takes hours, then this algorithm is prohibitively expensive.

An alternative is to approximate _g_ using machine learning. This is often called a statistical emulator or surrogate model of a mathematical model \[[67](./19-ref01.md#refbib67)\]. To train this model, the analyst would select _m_ training cases (X\~1,θ\~1),...,(X\~m,θ\~m) and evaluate the model at these _m_ values, Zj\=g(X\~j,θ \~j). The statistical model is

Zj\=g\~(X\~j,θ\~j)+εj(8.37)

289\. where g\~ is an approximation for _g_ and _ε_ _j_ is approximation error. Since it is presumed that the mathematical model is a complex function of its inputs, a nonparametric regression model such as Gaussian process regression is selected for the function g\~. The idea is that it may take a long time to produce the _m_ training observations and fit the machine learning model to estimate g\~, but once this work is completed, it is easy to evaluate g\~ for a new set of inputs. An advantage of a fully Bayesian analysis involving emulators is that uncertainty about the emulator's accuracy can be measured and propagated to the final output.

The emulator can be used for Bayesian optimization. That is, if the objective is to find the input **X** that maximizes the function _g_, that can be done quickly by instead maximizing the surrogate function, i.e.,

Xopt\=arg maxX g( X,θ)≈arg maxX g\~(X,θ).(8.38)

While evaluating g\~ is fast, optimization is complicated by the fact that g\~ is a statistical prediction and thus is a random function, requiring advanced methods from stochastic optimization \[[73](./19-ref01.md#refbib73)\].

The emulator can also be included in the model for the observations to learn about unknown parameter and model error. The model in (8.36) then becomes

Yi\=g\~(Xi,θ)+d( Xi)+ϵi.(8.39)

With the fast statistical prediction g\~ replacing the cumbersome mathematical model solution _g_, standard Bayesian computational tools such as MCMC can be used to estimate the parameters θ and discrepancy function _d_.

**Equation learning**: Methods that emulate the _g_ are useful for improving computational efficiency, and modeling the discrepancy term _d_ can shed some light on model performance. However, an emerging area of research \[[20](./19-ref01.md#refbib20), [24](./19-ref01.md#refbib24), [139](./19-ref01.md#refbib139)\] is to replace specifying _g_ based on physical laws and instead learn the underlying equations that dictate _g_. This method then replaces mathematical modeling based on physical or biological laws with machine learning based on training data and statistical analysis.

To understand this approach, consider the problem of mathematically modeling for the spread of an infectious disease briefly discussed in Chapter 7.1\. A standard model in epidemiology is the SIR model, where the number of susceptible, infected and recovered individuals evolve over time through the equations

dS(t)dt\=−βS(t)I(t), dI(t)dt\=βS(t)I(t)−γI(t), and dR(t)dt\=γI(t).

The model has two parameters, θ\=(β,γ). Say the output of interest is g(t;θ)\=I(t); then a reasonable analysis is to use the observed number of infected individuals at time _t_, Y(t), to fit the model Y(t)\=g(t,θ)+d(t)+ε(t) and learn the parameters θ while accounting for model error _d_ and measurement error _ε_.

If the mathematical model ([8.6.5](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_6_5)) is approximately correct, then learning the parameters θ and making forecasts from the model is an effective approach, even for scenarios beyond the range of data. However, if these equations are incorrect, which is almost certain for a process as complicated as the spread of an infectious disease, then no amount of data can correct for this model misspecification. An alternative is to model the derivatives using ML, e.g.,

dS(t)dt\=gS{S(t),I(t),R(t)}(8.40)

where _g_ _S_, and similarly _g_ _I_ and _g_ _R_ for derivatives of the infected and recovered classes, is modeled using a nonparametric regression (Chapter 8.3) such as GAMs or deep learning 290\. \[[101](./19-ref01.md#refbib101), [102](./19-ref01.md#refbib102), [109](./19-ref01.md#refbib109), [111](./19-ref01.md#refbib111), [157](./19-ref01.md#refbib157), [158](./19-ref01.md#refbib158)\]. This approach has the advantages of being able to learn equations from the data without strict assumptions. However, as with any statistical model, prediction outside the range of the training data is questionable.

Another option is symbolic regression, where the goal is to uncover the parametric form of the equations along with the unknown parameters. Symbolic regression assumes the differential equations can be constructed using basic operations of the state variables, such as polynomials, products and trigonometric functions. A simple model that would be effective for the SIR model is to assume the second-order polynomial model

gS{S(t),I(t),R(t)}\=b0+S(t)b1+I(t)b2+R(t)b3(8.41)

+S(t)2b4+I(t)2b5+R(t)2b6(8.42)

+S(t)I(t)b7+S(t)R(t)b8+I(t)R(t)b9.(8.43)

This simple model includes the true process with b7\=−β and bj\=0 for all j≠7, so Bayesian variable selection methods such as stochastic search variable selection (Chapter 6.2) may be able to select the true model. Tree-based approaches similar to Bayesian adaptive regression trees (Chapter 8.3.3) can also be used \[[84](./19-ref01.md#refbib84)\]. Of course, this approach relies on including the necessary basic operations to be considered, which cannot be guaranteed in a real application.

If observations are available at times t1,...,tn, then the fitted model could be

S(ti+1)−S(ti)ti+1−ti\=gS{S(ti),I(ti),R(ti)}+εi(8.44)

and similar for the other two classes. The prior for gS(S,I,R) could be centered on the parametric model, e.g., a Gaussian process with mean −βSI, and standard deviation with mass near zero such as a Cauchy prior and correlation that decays with distance in (S,I,R). Further modeling could add biological constraints, such as S(t)+I(t)+R(t) being fixed at the population size and gS(S,I,R)\=0 if I\=0 since with no infected individuals there can be no new cases and thus no change in the number of susceptible individuals. The modeling opportunities are endless, making this a promising area of future research.
