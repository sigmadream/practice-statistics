## 7.1 Overview of hierarchical modeling

Thus far we have introduced Bayesian ideas in the context of standard statistical models. However, one of the primary benefits of Bayesian methodology is flexibility to handle non-standard cases with irregularities such as missing values, censored data, variables measured with error, multiple data sources each with distinct biases and errors, subpopulations with different properties, etc. Incorporating all of these features in an analysis may seem daunting, but can often be accomplished by breaking the large model into manageable layers and combining the layers in a hierarchical model. Hierarchical modeling (also known as a multilevel modeling) is thus an essential model-building tool.

To see how building a model for complex data can be simplified by thinking hierarchically, say our objective is to specify the joint distribution of three variables _X, Y_ and _Z_. Directly fitting a multivariate joint distribution can be challenging, especially if the three variables have different supports. However, any trivariate distribution can be written

f(x,y,z)\=f(x)f(y|x)f(z|x,y).(7.1)

By ordering the three variables and specifying the univariate marginal distribution of _X_ and then the univariate conditional distributions for Y|X and Z|X,Y, the multivariate problem is reduced to three univariate problems. Because the variables are ordered and each conditional distribution depends only on the previous variables in the ordering, the resulting joint distribution is guaranteed to be valid. Also, since any multivariate distribution can be decomposed this way, there is no loss of flexibility by taking this approach.

212\. The trivariate model is represented as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) in [Figure 7.1a](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_1). A DAG (also called a Bayesian network) represents the model as a graph with each observation and parameter as a node (i.e., the points that define the graph) and edges (i.e., connections between the nodes) to denote conditional dependence. To define a valid stochastic model the graph must be directed and acyclic. A directed graph associates each edge with a direction; an arrow from _X_ to _Y_ indicates that the hierarchical model is defined by modeling the conditional distribution of _Y_ as a function of _X_. The absence of an arrow from _X_ to _Z_ in [Figure 7.1b](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_1) conveys the choice that conditioned on _Y, Z_ does not depend on _X_, i.e., f(z|x,y)\=f(z|y); in contrast, [Figure 7.1a](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_1) is the DAG for the model with conditional dependence between _X_ and _Z_ given _Y_. The graph must also be acyclic, meaning that it is impossible to follow directed edges from a node through the graph and return to the original node. These two conditions rule out building models such as p(x,y,z)\=p(x|y,z)p(y|z)p(z|y), which may not be a valid joint distribution.

Hierarchical models can take many forms, but a general way to build a model is through creating a data layer, a process layer and a prior layer. Model building should begin with the _process layer_ that contains the underlying scientific processes of interest and the unknown parameters. Building this layer is ideally done in consultation with domain experts. Once this layer is defined, the statistical objectives can be articulated, for example, to estimate a particular parameter or test a specific hypothesis. Ideally these objectives dictate the data to be collected for the analysis. The _data layer_ relates (via the likelihood function) the data to the process and encodes bias and error in the data collection procedure, which requires knowledge of how the data were collected. Finally the _prior layer_ quantifies uncertainty about the model parameters at the onset of the analysis.

Building a model hierarchically is convenient, but not fundamentally different than models we have considered previously. In fact, we have already encountered many hierarchical models such as the random effects models in [Section 4.4](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_4). This means that the computational methods to approximate the posterior described in [Chapter 3](./11-chapter3.md) apply to hierarchical models, as do the graphical and numerical methods used to summarize the posterior and check fit. Below we discuss several categories of hierarchical models and the application of Bayesian methods in each case. We then illustrate these concepts with several case studies in Chapter 7.2.

### 7.1.1 213\. Mathematical/statistical models

In many scientific disciplines, the process of interest is described mathematically as a set of differential equations. Differential-equation models use the current state of all variables and the physical or biological laws to determine the dynamics of the system. Mathematical models are fundamental to many applications, including the

* track of a hurricane through the Atlantic ocean,
* strength of a bridge under a given design,
* formation and spread of cancer cells,
* population dynamics of an endangered species.

Ideally, it can be assumed that the mathematical model is a reasonable representation of the system, but is imperfect due to discretization or simplification, and there is uncertainty because the initial and current states of the system and model parameters are not known precisely.

Consider modeling the spread of a wildland fire \[[49](./19-ref01.md#refbib49)\]. The mathematical/differential equations model would describe the dispersion of the fire as a function of the topology, terrain, meteorology, etc. There are many reasons forecasts from the model will have error. Even if the equations perfectly describe the system's dynamics, which is unlikely, in practice they would need to be discretized because of computational limitations or the spatial resolution of model inputs such as satellite imagery. Also, the equations are contingent on the current state of the system including the location and intensity of the fire, the condition of the surrounding vegetation and the current wind speed and direction. The model also depends on unknown parameters, such as the likelihood of spread into a high-elevation pine-dominant forest. Neither the current state nor model parameters can be known exactly.

Statistical analyses that account for model bias, unknowns and error are called uncertainty quantification (UQ; \[[143](./19-ref01.md#refbib143)\]). A UQ analysis can improve predictions by calibrating mathematical models with real-world data, communicate uncertainty to stakeholders to improve decision making, improve efficiency by optimizing future data collection for model refinement and formally test for differences between future scenarios while accounting for all sources of uncertainty. A Bayesian approach \[[90](./19-ref01.md#refbib90)\] is natural for these tasks because it is able to seamlessly incorporate prior knowledge when available and account for multiple sources of uncertainty.

As an introduction, consider the simple disease progression model below to illustrate the model-building process. Let _S_ _t_ and _I_ _t_ be the number of susceptible and infected individuals in a population, respectively, at time _t_. Scientific understanding of the disease is used to model disease propagation. The change of _S_ _t_ and _I_ _t_ over time can be described by differential equations \[[23](./19-ref01.md#refbib23)\]. Since our data are discretized to daily counts, we consider a difference equation. Many compartmental models have been proposed including the SIER model, but in consultation with an epidemiologist, we might select the simple Reed–Frost model \[[3](./19-ref01.md#refbib3)\]:

Process layer:It+1|St,It∼Binomial\[St,1−(1−q)It\]St+1\=St−It+1

where it is assumed that all infected individuals are removed from the population before the next time step and _q_ is the probability of a non-infected person coming into contact with and contracting the disease from an infected individual.

The epidemiological process-layer model expresses the disease dynamics up to a few unknown state variables, _I_1 and _S_1, and an unknown parameter, _q_. To estimate these 214\. unknowns, the number of cases at time _t_, denoted _Y_ _t_, is collected. The data layer models our ability to measure the process _I_ _t_. For example, after discussing the data collection procedure with domain experts, we might assume there are no false positives (uninfected people counted as infected) but potentially false negatives (uncounted infected individuals) and thus

Data layer: Yt|It∼Binomial(It,p)(7.2)

where _p_ is the probability of detecting an infected individual. The Bayesian model is completed using priors for the unknown state variables and parameters

Prior layer:I1∼Poisson(λ1),S1∼Poisson (λ2)p,q∼beta(a,b).

[Figure 7.2](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_2) plots the DAG corresponding to this model.

The Reed-Frost model is very simple and unlikely to provide a perfect representation of the system's dynamics. If diagnostics call the fit of the model into question, a remedy is to select a more sophisticated model, such as the SIER model mentioned above. However, we can almost never be certain that the chosen model has complete fidelity to the true dynamics; thus we should allow for this bias in the hierarchical model. Discrepancy terms can enter either in the data or process layers. Here we add the discrepancy term, dt\>0, to the data layer

Data layer with discrepancy: Yt|It,dt∼Binomial(Itdt,p).(7.3)

In this parameterization, dt\=1.1 means that the number of infected individuals is 10% higher at time _t_ in reality than in the current state of the model. Of course, if the discrepancy terms are allowed to vary freely across _t_ then it will absorb all the signal in the data and true number of infected individuals cannot be estimated. Identifiability concerns can be lessened 215\. by taking log(dt)\=∑k\=0Kαktk as a low-order polynomial function of _t_ and estimating the discrepancy parameters _α_ _k_ in the Bayesian hierarchical model.

The Reed-Frost model is simple, and an analysis of these data using Bayesian methods can utilize standard MCMC methods. However, more complex mathematical models that are defined as the solution to a set of differential equations pose computational challenges because evaluating the model even a single time can take several hours. [Section 8.6.5](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_6_5) shows how machine learning tools can be used to improve computation and thus facilitate a Bayesian analysis of complex mathematical models. This section also discusses Bayesian machine learning tools to learn the equations that govern the process from data rather than specifying models based on domain knowledge.

### 7.1.2 State-space models

State-space models (SSMs) are a class of hierarchical models that are used to study the evolution of processes over time and make short-term forecasts. The process layer defines the evolution of latent (unobserved) states that are related to the observations in the data layer. Below we discuss two examples of SSMs: the Dynamic Linear Model (DLM) and the Hidden Markov Model (HMM). Both models are described assuming time is discretized, so that the process is defined only at times t∈{1,…,n}, and the latent space variables follow a Markov process where the distributions of the states are independent of their history given the previous value. Extensions to continuous time and richer temporal dynamics are available, but more complex. The nature of the latent states distinguishes the two models with the DLM modeling them as continuous random variables whereas in HMM models the latent states as discrete random variables.

**Dynamic linear models**: In the DLM \[[163](./19-ref01.md#refbib163)\], the _q_ latent states at time _t_, Xt\=(Xt1,...,Xtq), evolve following the linear model

Xt\=AXt−1+ut

where **A** (q×q) determines the evolution of the latent states and ut∼iid Normal(0,Σu). The latent variables are related to the _p_ response variables, Yt\=(Yt1,...,Ytp), as

Yt\=BXt+vt

where **B** (p×q) links the latent states with the outcome and vt∼iid Normal(0,Σv).

As a concrete example, say the responses are _p_ stock prices that are thought to be driven by _q_ latent economic factors. As another example, say the outcomes are a patient's vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, etc.) and the latent factors are facets of heart function. In both cases, it is assumed that the responses are correlated over time and with each other, and most of this correlation can be explained by variation in the latent factors. An advantage of modeling dependence between the outcomes using the latent variables is the efficiency gain that comes if a small number of latent factors (q<p) can explain the dependence.

If data are available through time _n_, sampling from the posterior prediction distribution (PPD) of Yn+1 is straightforward using MCMC. Let the values for iteration _s_ be denoted with a superscript, e.g., Xt(s). Then a sample from the PPD is generated as Xt+1(s)∼Normal(A(s)Xt(s),Σu(s)) and then Yt+1(s)∼Normal(B(s)Xt+1(s),Σv(s)). The distribution of the samples Yt+1(1),...,Yt+1(S) account for uncertainty in the latent states as well as the parameters (**A**, **B**, etc). While MCMC provides a straightforward method for short-term prediction, it can be slow because sampling requires updating the latent process Xt for all _n_ training observations. More efficient methods are available, including the Kalman filter \[[32](./19-ref01.md#refbib32)\].

216\. Label switching \[[83](./19-ref01.md#refbib83)\] poses a challenge when fitting and interpreting SSMs (both DLMs and HMMs). Unless there is some prior knowledge to distinguish the latent factors, changing the labels of the model has no effect on fit. As an example, say q\=2 and Xt\=(Xt1,Xt2) is changed to Xt\=(Xt2,Xt1) and the corresponding rows of **A**, **B** and Σu are swapped; then the posterior is not changed. One way to resolve the label-switching problem is to insert known or artificial constraints to ensure the latent states are identified. In the casino example below, the distribution of one of the two latent states is known, and this information resolves label switching. If nothing is known about the latent states, then elements of **A**, **B** or Σu can be fixed. A common constraint is that **A** is lower-triangular with positive diagonal elements. Another challenge is selecting the number of latent states; model-fit criteria such as DIC, WAIC or cross validation can be used.

**Hidden Markov models:** HMMs \[[174](./19-ref01.md#refbib174)\] have a similar structure and interpretation as DLMs except for the key difference that the latent process Xt∈{1,...,q} is discete with only _q_ levels. This adds the interpretation that the latent state represents different modes of behaviour. For example, in animal movement modeling where Yt is the speed and direction of an animal at time _t_, the latent state may have q\=3 levels: Xt\=1 indicates the animal is resting, Xt\=2 that they are hunting and Xt\=3 that they are in transit. Clearly the distribution of the response will differ based on the current state. The objectives of an HMM analysis include identifying the latent states and understanding the temporal process in each state.

The latent state _X_ _t_ is assumed to be a Markov process; that is, given all previous states X1,...,Xt−1, _X_ _t_ depends only on Xt−1. Under this assumption, its distribution is completely determined by the q×q transition probability matrix with elements

Pij\=Prob(Xt\=j|Xt−1\=i).

Given the previous state is Xt−1\=i, the PMF of the current state is given by the _q_ probabilities Pi\=(Pi1,...,Piq). The conjugate prior for a vector of probabilities is a Dirichlet prior ([Section 2.1](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1)) Pi∼Dirichlet(ai1,...,aiq). Alternatively, if there are external covariates that are thought to affect the transition probabilities, then Pi can be modeled using a multinomial logistic regression model ([Section 4.3.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_3_2)).

The parameters in the response distribution are determined by the current state. If the data have support on the real line, then the simplest model is

Yt|Xt\=k∼indepNormal(μk,Σ)

where μk\=(μk1,...,μkp)T and Σ is the p×p covariance matrix. This model can be extended in many ways, including a state-specific covariance, covariates in the mean with state-specific regression coefficients, dependence in the response over time (called a regime-switching model), or a generalized linear model with state-specific coefficients for non-Gaussian data. If the number of states is small, then it is reasonable to use uninformative priors for the state-specific parameters; they can also be treated as random effects if the number of latent states is large.

**Dishonest casino example:** The “dishonest casino” data are from the R package aphid \[[164](./19-ref01.md#refbib164)\]. The data consist of n\=300 rolls of a die. The assumption is that there are q\=2 latent states: one is a fair die with equal probability on all six outcomes, and the other is a rigged die with unequal (and unknown) probabilities. The dealer switches in the rigged die at times during the game, and the goal is to compute the posterior probability that the rigged die is being used at a given time. The data are plotted in [Figure 7.3](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_3) (top). The sample proportions of the six outcomes are 0.13, 0.14, 0.16, 0.12, 0.14 and 0.30, so there is a substantially higher proportion of sixes than expected under a fair die.217\.

### 7.1.3 Multilevel statistical models

In many applications, physical models of the system of interest are unavailable and are replaced with purely statistical models built on simple relationships, such as linear trends and correlations. Hierarchical modeling is also useful for building rich statistical models. The general idea of building a hierarchical model through the data, process and prior layers remains valid, but with a statistical instead of physical view of the process layer. For example, a purely statistical model to replace the Reed-Frost process in the infectious disease analysis above is It+1x|St,It∼Binomial(St,qt) with logit(qt)\=β0+β1It. This process layer lacks the physical interpretation of the Reed-Frost model, but is a valid statistical model that may perform well in practice.

A general idea for building a multilevel model is to split the data into homogeneous groups and then pool information across the groups via the prior distribution. The random slopes model for the jaw bone density data (plotted in [Figure 4.8](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_8)) discussed in [Section 4.4](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_4) is an example of a hierarchical model built this way. The model is

Data layer:Yij|βi∼Normal(Xjβi,σ2)Process layer:βi|μ,Σ∼Normal(μ,Σ)Prior layer:μ∼Normal (0,c2I2),Σ∼ InvWishart(ν,Ω)

for i\=1,...,n\=20 and j\=1,...,m\=4. Each of the 20 patients gets their own simple linear regression model, and these regressions are combined in the process layer that specifies the distribution of the regression coefficients across individuals in the patient population.

This model is visualized as a DAG in [Figure 7.4](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_4). The DAG shows how information moves through the hierarchical model. For example, how does the data from patient 1 help us predict the bone density for patient 2's next visit? To traverse the DAG from Y1 to Y2 requires going through the population parameters μ and Σ. That is, Y1 informs the model about β1 which shapes the random effects distribution that enters the model for β2 and thus Y2. If we only had data from patient 2, then we would likely resort to an uninformative prior for β2 and with only a few observations for patient 2 the posterior would be unstable. 219\. However, the hierarchical model allows us to borrow strength across patients to stabilize the results.

Each group is linked upward from corresponding beta parameters beta one through beta n in the process layer. These beta parameters all receive arrows from shared hyperparameters mu and Sigma. MCMC is a natural choice for fitting hierarchical models; just as hierarchical models build complexity by layering simple conditional distributions, MCMC samples from the complex posterior distribution by sequentially updating parameters from simple full conditional distributions. In fact, displaying the hierarchical model as a DAG not only helps us understand the model but it also aids in coding the MCMC sampler because the full conditional distribution for a parameter depends only on terms with an arrow to or from the parameter's node in the DAG. From [Figure 7.4](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_4) it is clear that the full conditional distribution for β2 depends only on the data-layer terms for Y21,...,Y2m|β2 and the process-layer term for β2|μ,Σ. If we view the model only through these terms then it is immediately clear that the full conditional distribution of β2 is exactly the full conditional distribution of the regression coefficients in standard Bayesian linear regression ([Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2)).

**When to stop adding layers?** The hierarchical models in this section have three levels: data, process and prior. However, it is likely the values that define the prior layer will not be known exactly, and it is therefore tempting to add a fourth (and a fifth, etc.) layer to explain this uncertainty. A general rule of thumb is to stop adding layers when there is no replication to estimate parameters. For example, referring to the DAG in [Figure 7.4](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_4), it is reasonable to add a layer to estimate the random effect mean μ and covariance Σ because there are repeated random effects β1,...,βn that can be leveraged to estimate these parameters. However, adding an additional layer to estimate prior mean Ω of the random effect covariance Σ would not be reasonable because there is only one covariance Σ in the model, and even if we knew Σ exactly, we would not be able to estimate its distribution from a single sample.

### 7.1.4 220\. Hierarchical models for missing and censored data

Missing data and censoring are complicating factors in many analyses. A Bayesian analysis seamlessly handles these irregularities by treating the missing or censored variables in the same way as unknown parameters. As with parameters, this requires assigning prior distributions for the missing values. For missing or censored responses, their distribution is the same as the observed responses and thus no additional assumptions are needed, except that the data are missing/censored at random. Handling missing or censored covariate values, e.g., the covariates in a linear regression, does require a new prior distribution for the missing values because distributional assumptions are not required for covariates in standard analyses. Assuming reasonable distributions can be identified and modeling assumptions are met, a Bayesian analysis with missing data proceeds as usual and has the benefit of accounting for uncertainty in the missing values when summarizing the posterior distribution of the parameters or predictions of interest.

**Missing data:** The easiest resolution to the missing-data problem is to simply discard observations with missing data and proceed with a complete-case linear regression. However, this can dramatically reduce the size of the dataset and discard useful information in the partially-observed responses. Another simple approach is to impute the missing values using a regression with the other variables as covariates. A drawback of single imputation is that it does account for uncertainty in the missing observations, and thus the resulting posterior inference is questionable. Multiple imputation techniques are also available \[[134](./19-ref01.md#refbib134)\] (and are often motivated using Bayesian ideas).

A Bayesian analysis using a hierarchical model is a natural way to handle missing data. The Bayesian approach handles missing data in the same way as unknown parameters; we represent our uncertainty about them by treating them as random variables in a hierarchical Bayesian model. As with an unknown parameter, posterior inference on an unknown missing covariate requires assigning it a prior distribution. As an example, consider the multiple linear regression model in Chapter 4.2,

Yi|Xi∼Normal(Xiβ,σ2) where Xi∼Normal(μ,Σ)(7.4)

where μ is the mean vector of length _p_ and Σ is the p×p covariance matrix of the covariates. In the absence of subject-matter prior information, the hyperparameters μ and Σ are given priors and estimated as part of the Bayesian analysis, resembling a random-effects model. In this way, the complete cases inform the model about the distribution of the covariates across observations (via μ and Σ), and this information is used to impute the missing values.

Of course, this approach requires a reasonable model for the covariate distribution. This is challenging when _p_ is large and/or the covariates are non-Gaussian. For example, if the covariates are a mix of continuous and binary variables, sufficiently capturing their joint distribution is difficult. A simple approach is to model the _p_ covariates with independent priors. This is inefficient but at least supplies a reasonable approximation in many situations. More sophisticated modeling, such as the methods outlined in [Chapter 8](./16-chapter8.md), may improve this aspect of the analysis. A crucial (and often unverifiable) assumption is that there are no systematic biases in the missing data; i.e., they are missing completely at random. If the probability that Xij is missing depends on _Y_ _i_, then assuming the Xij are missing at random would likely result in a biased estimator for _β_ _j_.

Assuming that the covariate model is correct, a hierarchical Bayesian analysis appropriately accounts for uncertainty in the missing values. A Gibbs sampler would update each parameter (β, _σ_, etc.) and then cycle through the missing observations (_Y_ _i_, Xij) and update them from their full conditional distributions, treating them exactly the same as the model parameters. Therefore, each sample of the regression coefficient β is updated using a complete set of covariates, but the imputed covariates vary across iterations following 221\. their posterior distribution. The missing values are thus effectively treated as nuisance parameters, and the analysis produces both the posterior predictive distribution of the missing values but more importantly the posterior distribution of the regression coefficients marginally over the uncertainty in the missing observations, as desired.

**Marathon example with missing data:** In this analysis of the 2016 Boston Marathon data from [Section 2.1.7](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1_7), we build a linear regression model to predict the speed (minutes per mile) of the final mile (mile 26) of the top female runners as a function of their speeds in the first 25 miles. Let _Y_ _i_ be the speed of mile 26 for runner i\=1,...,n\=149 and Xij be the speed for runner _i_ in mile j\=1,...,p\=25. Approximately 8% of the speed measurements are missing: the number of missing observations ranges from 0 to 19 across runners, and the percentage of missing observations ranges from 0% to 55% (miles 6 and 7) across miles ([Figure 7.5a](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_5)). Discarding observations with at least one missing Xij would reduce the sample size from 149 runners to 58\. Given that most of the missing values are for miles 6 and 7 and these covariates are likely not important predictors of the response, it would be wasteful to discard all of these observations from the analysis.

**Covariate measurement error:** Error-in-variables models, also called measurement-error models \[[66](./19-ref01.md#refbib66)\], are used to handle covariates that are not missing, but are measured with error. For example, say controlled experiments show that an environmental contaminant measured in a person's blood has error standard deviation _τ_. Then a regression of a person's health status, measured by _Y_ _i_, onto their level of exposure to the contaminant, _X_ _i_, would suffer from covariate measurement error.

If the variance of the covariate measurement error is known and a distribution of the error can be assumed (which are strong assumptions), then a Bayesian hierarchical model can address this error. A joint model is

Yi|β,Xi∗∼Normal(β0+β1Xi∗,σ2) and Xi|Xi∗∼Normal(Xi∗,τ2),

where Xi∗ is the true value of the covariate and has prior Xi∗∼Normal(μ,σX2). In MCMC, the true value handled like missing data, i.e., is imputed for each observation at each iteration, and all other parameters (β and _σ_2) are updated as in regular linear regression.

**Censored data:** A censored observation is known partially but not exactly. Typically a censored observation is known to have occurred in an interval, say Yi∈Ii\=(Li,Ui). Special cases of interval censoring include: if Ii\=(−∞,Ui), then it is left-censored; if Ii\=(Li,∞), then it is right-censored; if Ii\=(−∞,∞), then it is missing; and if Li\=Ui, then it is uncensored. The canonical example of censored data is survival analysis (e.g., \[[82](./19-ref01.md#refbib82)\]), where _Y_ _i_ represents the time from patient _i_ entering the study to an event occurring (e.g., developing a disease). Right-censoring results from patients being lost to follow-up or events occurring only after trial completion. In this case, a patient who experienced an event during the trial is not censored and Li\=Ui\=Yi, and a patient known to have survived until _L_ _i_ but who was then lost to follow-up is right-censored with Ui\=∞.

A convenient model for right-censored data is Yi|λ∼iid Exponential(λ) with conjugate prior λ∼Gamma(a,b). The contribution of observation _i_ to the likelihood is the PDF λexp(−λYi) if it is uncensored and the survival time Prob(Yi\>Li|λ)\=exp(−λiLi) if it is right censored at _L_ _i_. If we denote δi\=0 and Ti\=Li if observation _i_ is censored and δi\=1 and Ti\=Yi otherwise, then

λ|δ1,...,δn,T1,...,Tn∼Gamma(∑i\=1nδi+a,∑i\=1nTi+b).

Under this model inference does not require MCMC sampling. However, the exponential model is usually too restrictive, and adding covariates, e.g., log(λi)\=Xiβ breaks conjugacy. Therefore, more flexible models are needed.

An attractive model to include convariates is the accelerated failure time model \[[25](./19-ref01.md#refbib25)\] that regresses the log time to event, Zi\=log(Yi), onto covariates, Zi\=Xiβ+εi. Under this model, if covariate _j_ increases by one, then the median (or any other quantile) time until event in multiplied by exp(βj). If there is no censoring and εi∼iid Normal(0,σ2), then this reduces to standard multiple regression as in Chapter 4.2\. Further, selecting conditionally conjugate priors for β and _σ_2 leads to Gibbs sampling.

However, if _Z_ _i_ is censored to the interval \[Li,Ui\], then conjugacy is broken. Assuming normality for _ε_ _i_, _Z_ _i_ follows a truncated normal distribution Zi∼TruncatedNormal(μi,σ2,Li,Ui) with μi\=Xiβ and density function

1σ2πexp\[−(Zi−μi)22σ2\]1Φ\[(Ui−μi)/σ\]−Φ\[(Li−μi)/σ\](./7.5)

224\. where Φ is the standard normal CDF. Since β is included (via _μ_ _i_) inside the normal CDF, its posterior is not conjugate.

Conjugacy is restored by treating the censored value _Z_ _i_ using Bayesian imputation inside MCMC (see [Listing 3](./15-chapter7.md#list7_3) for a simple example). For each iteration, the censored value _Z_ _i_ is drawn from the truncated normal distribution Zi∼TruncatedNormal(μi,σ2,Li,Ui) which has support \[Li,Ui\] and thus retains the censoring information. After imputation, the responses are complete, and updates of the regression coefficients and variance can be performed using the same Gibbs sampling methods as in usually multiple linear regression. As with missing data, new samples of the censored observations are generated for each MCMC iteration, and so this Bayesian imputation approach is equivalent to fitting the model with the likelihood function composed of terms such as (7.5). Also, Bayesian multiple imputation ensures that uncertainty about the censored values is incorporated in the posterior distribution of the regression parameters. This method of imputation is very general, and can thus be applied to the flexible regression methods in [Chapter 8](./16-chapter8.md) to relax assumptions about normality and linearity.

## 7.2 Case studies

The remainder of this chapter is formatted as a sequence of case studies in hierarchical modeling. The three case studies each pose different challenges:

1. **Species distribution mapping via data fusion**: Combining information from multiple data streams while accounting for their bias and uncertainty
2. **Tyrannosaurid growth curves**: Pooling information across subpopulations (species) and quantifying uncertainty in non-linear models with a small number of observations
3. **Climate reconstruction using proxy data**: Accounting for bias and uncertainty in proxy data to reconstruct the temperature distribution in the recent past

In these analyses we demonstrate the flexibility of hierarchical modeling, and also illustrate complete Bayesian analyses including model and prior specification, model comparisons, and presentation of the results.

### 7.2.1 Species distribution mapping via data fusion

The data for this case study come from \[[119](./19-ref01.md#refbib119)\]. The objective is to map the spatial distribution of a small song bird, the brown-headed nuthatch (BHNU; _Sitta pusilla_), in the southeastern US. There are two data sources, each with different strengths. The first data source is the Breeding Birds Survey (BBS). BBS is a network of hundreds of routes surveyed by thousands of volunteers that has been active since 1966 \[[138](./19-ref01.md#refbib138)\]. Data are collected systematically by trained volunteers, and sites are visited annually to monitor for changes. However, even with this immense sampling effort, there are spatial and temporal gaps in BBS coverage. An emerging line of research is to supplement systematic survey data with massive citizen science data such as the Cornell Lab of Ornithology's eBird database \[[150](./19-ref01.md#refbib150)\], which consists of millions of data points from thousands of citizen scientists each year. These data are not collected by trained birders, but have far greater spatial and temporal coverage.

225\. 226\. For this analysis, the southeast US is partitioned into n\=741, 0.25×0.25 degree lat/lon cells, and we analyze data from 2012\. Let N1i be the number of BBS sampling occasions in cell _i_ and Y1i∈{0,1,...,N1i} be the number of occasions with a sighting of the BHNU. Many cells do not have a BBS route and thus N1i\=Yi1\=0. Similarly, for cell _i_ define N2i as the number of hours logged by eBird citizen scientists and Y2i as the number of BHNU eBird sightings. [Figure 7.6](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_6) maps the data. The BBS sampling effort is fairly uniform, whereas the eBird effort is more concentrated in populated areas. Both maps show more BHNU sightings in Alabama, Georgia and the Carolinas.

The true process of interest in cell _i_ is the abundance λi≥0, defined as the expected number of birds present in the region during one unit of surveying effort. For a cell that is not inhabited by the BHNU λi\=0. The data layer relates the process to the observed data, and requires careful consideration of the strengths of the two data sources. First we make the assumption that the BBS and eBird datasets are independent given _λ_ _i_. This seems reasonable as most eBird users are not following BBS updates. The BBS data are gathered by expert birders, and thus we assume that there are no false positives or negatives (although more flexible models are available and likely preferable; see \[[119](./19-ref01.md#refbib119)\] for example). If the number of birds present during a survey is distributed as Poisson(λi), then the probability that there is at least one bird present is 1−exp(−λi), and so we model the BBS data as Yi1|λi∼Binomial\[N1i,1−exp(−λi)\].

For the eBird data, we do allow for false positives and false negatives. The mean is N2iλ\~i with rate λ\~i\=θ1λi+θ2, where θ1\>0 controls the difference between observation rates by BBS observers and eBird observers and θ2\>0 is the eBird false positive rate, so that if the cell is truly uninhabited and λi\=0, then E(Y2i)\=N2iθ2. Y2i|λi,θ∼NegBinomial(qi,m)(7.6)

with probability qi\=m/(λ\~i+m) and size _m_. Combining these two contributions to the likelihood, the data layer is

Data layer:Y1i|λi,θ1,θ2∼Binomial\[N1i,1−exp(−λi)\]Y2i|λi,θ1,θ2∼NegBinomial(qi,m).

The latent process of interest is the abundance in each cell, _λ_ _i_. It is difficult to model all the _λ_ _i_ without prior knowledge because some cells do not have BBS data. In the absence of other prior knowledge, we may simply assume that nearby cells have similar abundance. This is a reasonable assumption if the underlying factors that drive abundance (climate, habitat, etc.) vary spatially and allows us to pool information locally to estimate abundance. Many spatial models can be used for model abundance \[[53](./19-ref01.md#refbib53)\], but here we use spline regression as in [Section 8.3](./16-chapter8.md#sec8_3). The log abundance (we use a log transformation to ensure _λ_ _i_ is non-negative) is a smooth function of the grid cell's spatial location, si\=(s1i,s2i). ### 7.2.2 Tyrannosaurid growth curves

We analyze the data from 20 fossils to estimate the growth curves of four tyrannosaurid species: Albertosaurus, Daspletosaurus, Gorgosaurus and Tyrannosaurus. The data are taken from Table 1 of \[[48](./19-ref01.md#refbib48)\] and plotted in [Figure 7.7](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_7). The objective is to establish the growth curve, i.e., expected body mass by age, for each species. The data exhibit non-linear 229\. relationships between age and mass, and there are commonalities between species. We therefore pursue a non-linear hierarchical model.

![Two panels compare dinosaur growth patterns. The left plot shows body mass versus age for Albertosaurus, Daspletosaurus, Gorgosaurus and Tyrannosaurus, each species following a distinct S shaped growth curve with Tyrannosaurus reaching the largest mass above 5000 kilograms. Figure 7.7 **Tyrannosaurid growth curve data**. The left panel gives scatter plots of the estimated age and body mass (kg) of 20 samples of four tyrannosaurid species; the right panel plots the same data after a log transformation of both variables. The original analysis of these data used non-linear least squares (fitted curves shown in the left panel of [Figure 7.7](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_7)). Quantifying uncertainty in this fit is challenging. The sampling distribution of the estimator does not have a closed form due to the non-linear mean structure, and with only a handful of observations to estimate roughly the same number of parameters, large-sample normal approximations are not valid, and resampling techniques such as the bootstrap may have insufficient data to approximate the sampling distribution. As shown below, a Bayesian analysis powered by MCMC fully quantifies posterior uncertainty.

Let Yij and Xij be the body mass and age, respectively, of sample _i_ from species j\=1,...,4. We model the data as

Yij\=fj(Xij)ϵij,(7.9)

where _f_ _j_ is the true growth curve for species _j_ and ϵij\>0 is multiplicative error with mean one. We use multiplicative error rather than additive error because variation in the population likely increases with mass/age. Assuming the errors are log-normal with log(ϵij)∼Normal(−σj2/2,σj2), then E(ϵij)\=1 as required, and the model becomes

log(Yij)∼Normal(log\[fj(Xij)\]−σj2/2,σj2),(7.10)

with E(Yij)\=fj(Xij), where σj2 controls the error variance for species _j_.

The data in [Figure 7.7](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_7) (left) clearly exhibit nonlinearity. However, after taking a log transformation of both mass and age, their relationship becomes fairly linear ([Figure 7.7](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_7), right). Therefore, one model we consider is the log-linear model

log\[fj(X)\]\=aj+bjlog(X)(7.11)

where _a_ _j_ and _b_ _j_ are the intercept and slope, respectively, for species _j_. On the original scale, the corresponding growth curve is fj(X)\=exp(aj)Xbj. If as expected _b_ _j_ is positive, then the growth curve increases indefinitely, which may not be realistic. Therefore, we compare the log-linear model with the logistic growth curve

fj(X)\=aj+bjexp\[dj(x−cj)\]1+exp\[dj(x−cj)\],(7.12)

where x\=log(X). This model has four parameters:

1. _a_ _j_ is the expected mass at age 0.
2. _b_ _j_ is the expected lifetime gain in mass.230\.
3. log(cj) is the age at which the species reaches half its expected gain.
4. dj\>0 determines the rate of increase with age.

The form of the curve is increasing (assuming bj\>0) and plateaus at aj+bj rather than continuing to increase with age. This is the same function fit by \[[48](./19-ref01.md#refbib48)\], except that we transform to log age.

In addition to comparing these two forms of growth curves, we also compare two priors. The first prior (“unpooled”) fits each species separately using uninformative priors. For the log-linear model the priors are aj,bj∼Normal(0,10) and σj2∼InvGamma(0.1,0.1), and for the logistic model the priors are log(aj),log(bj),cj,log(dj)∼Normal(0,10) and σj2∼InvGamma(0.1,0.1). The normal prior is replaced with the log-normal prior for aj,bj and _d_ _j_ to ensure these parameters are positive, and thus fj(X) is positive and increasing for all _X_. The second prior (“pooled”) is a Bayesian hierarchical model that borrows information across the four species. In the pooled analysis we assume the variance is the same for all species, σj2\=σ2, and has uninformative prior σ2∼InvGamma(0.1,0.1). For the log-linear model priors for the intercepts are aj∼Normal(μa,σa2), where μa∼Normal(0,10) and σa2∼InvGamma(0.1,0.1). The same hierarchical model is applied to the log(aj), log(bj), _c_ _j_ and log(dj) in the logistic model. The JAGS code for this model is given in [Listing 5](./15-chapter7.md#list7_5).

This hierarchical model treats the parameters across the four species as random effects, and learning about the random effects distribution (i.e., _μ_ _a_ and σa2) stabilizes the posterior by providing additional information via the priors. It is debatable whether these parameters are truly random effects, i.e., whether there is an infinite distribution of exchangeable species from which these four were randomly selected for the study. However, analyzing the data 231\. 232\. from these four species using a random effects model clearly improves the results (as shown below) by pooling information across species to reduce uncertainty.

We fit the model with log-linear and logistic growth curves, each separate by species (unpooled) and using a hierarchical model (pooled). _DIC_ (_p_ _D_) for the four fits are: 29 (25) for log-linear unpooled, −3 (9) for log-linear pooled, 64 (41) for logistic unpooled and −2 (12) for logistic pooled. The pooled models reduce model complexity (as measured by _p_ _D_), and this leads to smaller (better) _DIC_. _DIC_ for the log-linear and logistic growth curves are similar.

Visually, the log-linear fits in [Figure 7.10](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_10) appear to sufficiently model the growth curves. ### 7.2.3 Climate reconstruction

This analysis uses the data of \[[39](./19-ref01.md#refbib39)\] and a simplified version of the statistical approach of \[[27](./19-ref01.md#refbib27)\] to reconstruct past annual precipitation in Brisbane, AU. The analysis is challenging because it combines several data types in a hierarchical model, and each data type has a high proportion of missing values. The rainfall index (RFI) is measured (in mm) from 1889-2017 and plotted in [Figure 7.12](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_12). Proxy data, e.g., ice cores and tree rings, dating as far back as 1612 are used to reconstruct RFI prior to 1889\. Let _Y_ _t_ be the observed RFI 233\. Yt|Yt−1∼Normal(μ0+ϕ0(Yt−1−μ0),(1−ϕ02)σ02),

where _μ_0 is the mean, σ02 is the variance and _ϕ_0 controls the temporal dependence. The form of the mean and variance are such that the marginal (over the _Y_ _j_ for j≠t) mean and variances are E(Yt)\=μ0 and Var(Yt)\=σ02 for all _t_.

Unfortunately, precipitation is measured only for the past 129 years. To impute these missing observations, we use several proxy variables shown in [Figure 7.13](./15-chapter7.md#fig7_13). The proxy variables also have a high proportion of missing values; however, there is some overlap between the years with proxies and RFI measurements, and the correlation between RFI and the proxies is as high as 0.40 depending on the proxy. Therefore, the relationships between precipitation and the proxies can be exploited to impute RFI back to 1612\. A hierarchical Bayesian model is a natural way to handle this task because of the large number of parameters and missing data, and thus uncertainty, that can be propagated through to the final analysis of precipitation across time.
