In this chapter we briefly discuss some of the most important concepts of statistical theory as they relate to Bayesian methods. This book is primarily dedicated to the practical application of Bayesian statistical methods, and thus this chapter can be skipped on first read. However, even an applied statistician should be familiar with statistical theory at least at a high level to plan and defend their work.

As an example, consider the problem of estimating a normal mean, _θ_. An estimator is a function that takes the data as input and returns an estimate of the parameter of interest. An estimator of the parameter _θ_ is denoted θ^(Y). A natural estimator of a normal mean is the sample mean, θ^(Y)\=∑i\=1nYi/n. However, there are other estimators including the sample median or the trimmed mean. In [Section 2.1.3](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1_3) we discussed Bayesian estimators such as the posterior mean θ^(Y)\=E(θ|Y)\=∑i\=1nYi/(n+m). How does one justify that the Bayesian estimator is a better estimator than the sample mean? In what sense is an estimator optimal? Questions such as these motivate the development of statistical theory.

In addition to investigating the properties of specific estimators and determining the settings for which they are preferred, much of statistical theory is dedicated to developing general procedures for deriving estimators with good properties. General estimation procedures include maximum likelihood estimation, the method of moments, estimating equations, and of course, Bayesian methods. Comparisons of these statistical frameworks are often made using frequentist criteria. As we will see, even if the objective if to generate a procedure with good frequentist properties, Bayesian methods often perform well.

In [Section 9.1](./17-chapter9.md#sec9_1) we focus on Bayesian methods and optimally summarizing the posterior distribution. In [Sections 9.2](./17-chapter9.md#sec9_2) and [9.3](./17-chapter9.md#sec9_3) we study the frequentist properties of Bayesian methods using mathematical and computational tools, respectively.

## 9.1 Decision theory

One of the primary goals of collecting data is to make decisions about the scientific problem. The decision can be devising the “best” estimate of a population level summary or the parameter _θ_ based on the observed data D\=(y1,y2,…,yn). It can also be of 296\. interest to test a hypothesis about the parameter _θ_; for example if θ\=(θ1,θ2), we may be interested to test the null hypothesis H0:θ1≤θ2 based on the observed data _D_. All of these cases can be considered within the general notion of _Decision Theory Framework_ that consists of a loss function L(θ,d) which measures the price one would pay if _θ_ is the true (but unobserved) value of the parameter, and we take a decision _d_ based on the data _D_.

_Point Estimation:_ Suppose we would like to compare two estimators T1(D) and T2(D), which are some functions of the data _D_, to estimate a real-valued parameter _θ_, how would we compare their performances? Consider a loss function (known as squared error loss) given by L(θ,T)\=(T−θ)2. Clearly, we can't directly compare the values Lj\=L(θ,Tj(D) for j\=1,2 for two obvious reasons: (i) _L_ _j_ is random as it depends on the observed data _D_; and (ii) _L_ _j_'s depend on _θ_, which is not known in the first place. This is exactly where the solutions obtained by frequentist and Bayesian methods differ and has been the subject of controversy over many decades if not centuries!

Frequentist solutions to resolve the two issues have been the following: (i) as _L_ _j_'s are random, average out the loss function over all possible data that could have been observed, and consider the risk function given by R(θ,T)\=Eθ\[L(θ,T(D)\], where the expectation _E_ _θ_ is taken with respect to the sampling distribution of _D_ given _θ_, viz., f(D|θ). This clearly takes care of the first problem, and there's no controversy about this step. The difference (between frequentist and Bayesian methods) arises on how the second problem (ii) is resolved. Clearly, R(θ,Tj) 's of the two estimators _T_1 and _T_2 are still not comparable directly as these risks still depend on _θ_.

_The Minimax approach_: One popular approach in frequentist theory (known as the _minimax method_) has been to find an estimator that would minimize the maximum risk, Rmax(T)\=supθR(θ,T). E.g., if we have two competing estimators _T_1 and _T_2, we can choose the one with smaller Rmax(Tj) for j\=1,2. In theory, the minimax approach may sound reasonable, but in practice it can be often infeasible, e.g., Rmax(T) may not be finite: if Yi∼N(θ,θ2) and we use T\=Y¯, then R(θ,T)\=θ2/n and Rmax(T)\=∞ for any fixed sample size _n_. Moreover, there might be possibilities like R(θ,T1)<R(θ,T2) for all θ≠θ0 but Rmax(T1)\=R(θ0,T1)\=R(θ0,T2)\=Rmax(T2), thus making _T_1 and _T_2 not comparable, and yet _T_1 may have significantly smaller risk than that of _T_2, except perhaps at the point θ\=θ0, at which the maximum risk is attained by both estimators. These types of problems are not uncommon in practice and led frequentist methods to consider alternative solutions.

_Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimators_: The primary idea is to work with a smaller class of estimators by restricting the class of possible estimators to the class of so-called _unbiased estimators_ satisfying Eθ\[T(D)\]\=θ for all values of _θ_; it tries to find the estimator with minimum variance among the class of all unbiased estimators, which leads to so-called minimum variance unbiased estimators (MVUE). The problem with this approach is often that for simple problems, there might not exist any unbiased estimator for a fixed sample size _n_. E.g., if Yi∼Poisson(θ) and we want to estimate the population standard deviation θ, there is no (finite sample) unbiased estimator of θ and hence no MVUE!

Thus, both minimax and MVUE approaches are not practically feasible for a broader class of problems (especially when _θ_ itself is of higher dimensions). The way out of these theoretical problems has been to consider asymptotic methods as n→∞ and find the “best” estimator that has the smallest asymptotic risk, which leads to the popular _maximum likelihood estimator_ defined as θ^MLE\=argmaxθL(θ;D), has been shown to have the least possible asymptotic risk.

However, a finite sample solution to the point estimation has remained illusive and unsatisfactory from a frequentist point of view, and so the final alternative is to consider a 297\. Bayesian approach which eliminates the problem of non-comparable risk functions R(θ,T) by considering the average risk r(T)\=Eπ\[R(θ,T)\] where the expectation _E_ _π_ is taken with respect to a prior distribution π(θ), which can be viewed as a weighting function.

_Bayes risk:_ The Bayes risk of an estimator T\=T(D) corresponding to a loss function L(θ,T) is defined as

r(T)\=E\[L(θ,T(Y))\]\=∫∫L(θ,T(y))f(y|θ)π(θ)dy dθ(9.1)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution of the data and parameter, (Y,θ) given by f(y|θ)π(θ). Clearly, the Bayes risk for two estimators (using the same prior π(θ)) are directly comparable, and the Bayes estimator is the one that minimizes the Bayes risk among all estimators T\=T(D). For many standard loss functions, the Bayes estimators can be derived explicitly. E.g., for the squared error loss L(θ,T)\=||θ−T||2 which measures the squared Euclidean distance between _θ_ and _T_, the Bayes estimator is given by θ^B\=E\[θ|Y\], the posterior mean of _θ_ given the data _Y_.

The main point of controversy lies in the choice of the prior density function π(θ) as the Bayes estimators do depend on such a (subjective) choice. However, both frequentist and Bayes estimators also depend on the (subjective) choice of the sampling density f(y|θ) and also the loss function L(θ,T). It can be argued that there's no way out of such subjective choices in making a statistical inference or any other scientific method, and the completely objective inference is more of a myth than a real objective!

We provide a simple example of estimating a proportion _θ_ based on the observations Y\=(y1,…,yn) and derive the frequentist and Bayes estimators.

_Twin Birth Example:_ Suppose we would like to predict the chance of a twin birth for the next day given that no twin birth was observed over the last 30 days in given city (e.g., in Raleigh, NC). What is the best estimate of θ\=Pr\[Twin birth\] given the data D\=(y1\=0,y2\=0,…,yn\=0), where _y_ _i_ is a binary variable taking a value of 1 if there's a twin birth on day _i_ and the value of 0 otherwise.

For this example, as the childbirths can be regarded as independent events, we may assume Yi|θ∼iidBer(θ), for i\=1,…,n, where Ber(θ) denotes the Bernoulli distribution with parameter θ∈\[0,1\]. Thus, in this case, f(y|θ)\=θy(1−θ)1−y for y∈{0,1}, and the likelihood function is given by L(θ;D)\=θS(1−θ)n−S, where S\=∑i\=1nyi denotes total number of twin births in a sample of _n_ observations.

Notice that for the twin birth example, we have S\=0, and hence it follows that the “best” frequentist estimate (MVUE or MLE) of _θ_ is given by θ^\=S/n\=0. The difficulty lies with the fact that the estimated standard error of the estimator is given by θ^(1−θ^)/n\=0. So, we are almost absolutely certain that the chance of a twin birth for next day is zero!! But is this really reasonable?

How about using the available background information based on historical data? A simple “Google search” will reveal that the chance of a twin birth is only about 1 in 30, and could we not possibly use such ‘prior’ information? E.g., we could use the prior θ∼Beta(1,29) as a prior distribution, which assures E\[θ\]\=1/30, and the posterior distribution is θ|S∼Beta(1+S,30+n−S), and the Bayes estimator is θ^\=1/60≈0.017, which is small but positive.

Before studying the frequentist properties of Bayesian estimators, we discuss how to optimally summarize the posterior distribution. An appealing feature of a Bayesian analysis is that it produces the full posterior distribution of each parameter. However, in many cases a point estimate (i.e., a single-number estimator) is desired. Common one-number 298\. summaries of the posterior used as point estimators are the posterior mean, median or mode. Rather than arbitrarily choosing one of these posterior summaries as the estimator, Bayesian decision theory provides a formal way to select the optimal Bayesian point estimator.

Defining the optimal point estimator requires defining the measure of estimation accuracy that is to be optimized. If _θ_ is the true value of the parameter and θ^(Y) is the estimator, then the loss function is defined as l(θ,θ^(Y)). For example, we might choose squared error loss, l(θ,θ^(Y))\=\[θ−θ^(Y)\]2, or absolute loss l(θ,θ^(Y))\=|θ−θ^(Y)|.

The loss function depends on the true value of the parameter, and therefore cannot be evaluated in a real data analysis. From the Bayesian perspective, given the data, uncertainty about the fixed but unknown parameter _θ_ is quantified by its posterior distribution. In this view, _θ_, and thus l(θ,θ^(Y)), are random variables. The Bayesian risk is the expected (with respect to the posterior distribution of _θ_) loss, R(θ^(Y))\=E\[l(θ,θ^(Y))|Y\]. The _Bayes' rule_ is the estimator θ^(Y) that minimizes Bayesian risk.

For example, assume squared error loss and define the posterior mean as θ¯\=E(θ|Y). The Bayesian risk is

R(θ^(Y))\=E\[l(θ,θ^(Y))|Y\]\=E{\[θ−θ^(Y)\]2|Y}\=E{\[(θ−θ¯)−(θ^(Y)−θ¯)\]2|Y}\=E{(θ−θ¯)2|Y}−2E{(θ−θ¯)(θ^(Y )−θ¯)|Y}+E{\[θ^(Y)−θ¯\]2|Y}\=Var(θ|Y)−2(θ^(Y)−θ¯)E(θ−θ¯|Y)+\[θ^(Y)−θ¯\]2\=Var(θ|Y)+\[θ^(Y)−θ¯\]2.

In the last equation, E(θ−θ¯|Y)\=E(θ)−θ¯\=0 by definition. The estimator θ^(Y) does not affect the posterior variance Var(θ|Y), and so under squared error loss, the posterior mean θ^(Y)\=θ¯ minimizes Bayesian risk and is the Bayes rule estimator. This justifies the use of the posterior mean to summarize the posterior in cases where squared error loss is reasonable.

Different loss functions give different Bayes rule estimators. Absolute loss gives the posterior median as the Bayes rule estimator. More generally, if over-estimation and under-estimation are weighted differently, a useful loss function is the asymmetric check-loss function

l(θ,θ^(Y))\={(1−τ)(θ^(Y)−θ)if θ^(Y)\>θτ(θ−θ^(Y))if θ^(Y )<θ(9.2)

for τ∈(0,1). In this loss function, if _τ_ is close to zero, then over-estimation (top row) is penalized more than under-estimation (bottom row). The Bayes rule for this loss function is the τth posterior quantile. If _θ_ is a discrete random variable, then the MAP estimator θ^(Y)\=arg maxθf(θ| Y) is the Bayes rule under zero-one loss l(θ,θ^(Y))\=I\[θ≠θ^(Y)\].

Decision theory can also formalize Bayesian hypothesis testing. Let H∈{0,1} denote the true state, with H\=0 if the null hypothesis is true and H\=1 if the alternative hypothesis is true. A Bayesian analysis produces posterior probabilities Prob(H\=h|Y) for h\={0,1} (see [Section 5.1](./13-chapter5.md#sec5_1)). The decision to be made is which hypothesis to select. Let d(Y)\=0 if we select the null hypothesis and d(Y)\=1 if we select the alternative hypothesis.

To determine the Bayes rule for d(Y) we must specify the loss incurred if we select the wrong hypothesis. If we assign a Type I error the loss _λ_1 and a Type II error the loss _λ_2, 299\. the loss function can be written

l(H,d(Y))\={0if H\=d(Y)λ1if H\=0 and d(Y)\=1λ2if H\=1 and d(Y)\=0.(9.3)

The Bayesian risk is

R(d(Y))\={λ1Prob(H\=0|Y)if d(Y)\=1λ2Prob(H\=1|Y)if d(Y)\=0.(9.4)

Therefore, the Bayes rule is to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the alternative is true; i.e., select d(Y)\=1, if the posterior probability of the alternative hypothesis exceeds

Prob(H\=1|Y)\>λ1λ1+λ2.(9.5)

Note that unlike classical hypothesis testing, if we swap the roles of the hypotheses, the decision rule remains the same. Often the loss of a Type 1 error is assumed to be larger than a Type II error, e.g., λ1\=10λ2, so that the null hypothesis is rejected only if the posterior probability of the alternative hypothesis is near one.

These decision-theory results hold for prediction as well. For example, if predictions are evaluated using squared-error prediction loss, then the mean of the posterior predictive distribution is the Bayes rule. Also, if the response is binary, then the Bayes rule for classification is to predict Ypred\=1 if the posterior predictive probability Prob(Ypred\=1|Y)\>λ1/(λ1+λ2), where _λ_1 is the loss for a false positive and _λ_2 is the loss of a false negative. In this case, it might be reasonable to set λ1\=λ2 and predict Ypred\=1 if Prob(Ypred\=1|Y)\>0.5.

## 9.2 Frequentist properties

Comparison and evaluation of statistical procedures proposed for general use are commonly evaluated using frequentist criteria. A frequentist evaluation of an estimator focuses on its sampling distribution, i.e., the distribution of θ^(Y) over repeated samples of the data **Y**. The distribution of the data of course depends on the true value of _θ_, denoted in this discussion of frequentist properties as _θ_0. For example, if Yi∼iidNormal(θ0,σ2), then the sample mean θ^(Y)\=∑i\=1nYi/n is an estimator of _θ_, and its sampling distribution is θ^(Y)∼Normal(θ0,σ2/n).

There are many ways to compare the sampling distributions of two estimators. For example, we could study the bias

Bias\[θ^(Y)\]\=E\[θ^(Y)\]−θ0.(9.6)

If the bias is positive, this means that on average the estimator over-estimates the parameter and vice versa. With all else equal, an unbiased estimator with bias equal to zero is preferred. However, the bias only evaluates the center of the sampling distribution and ignores its spread. Therefore we also study the variance of the sampling distribution Var\[θ^(Y)\]. For two estimators that are unbiased, we prefer low variance because this means the sampling 300\. distribution is concentrated around the true parameter; on the other hand, a small variance can be undesirable for biased estimators ([Figure 9.1](./17-chapter9.md#fig9_1)). The most common method of comparison is mean squared error

MSE\[θ^(Y)\]\=E\[θ^(Y)−θ0\]2\=Bias\[θ^(Y)\]2+Var\[θ^(Y)\],(9.7)

which combines bias and variance into a single measure.

Evaluating estimators using bias, variance and MSE is complicated by the fact that these summaries depend on the true parameter value, _θ_0, and the sample size, _n_. For example, it may be that an estimator performs well if _θ_0 is close to zero and the sample is small, but performs poorly in other settings. Therefore it is important to evaluate the procedure over a range of settings. To deal with the sample size, we often consider asymptotic arguments with n→∞. A method is asymptotically unbiased if its bias converges to zero as the sample size goes to infinity. An estimator is said to be consistent if it converges in probability to the true value, i.e., the probability of the estimator being within _ϵ_ (for any _ϵ_) of the true value increases to one as the sample size increases to infinity. An asymptotically unbiased estimator is consistent if its variance decreases to zero with the sample size.

Frequentist evaluation of statistical methods extends to interval estimation and testing. For Bayesian credible intervals we evaluate their frequentist coverage probability, i.e., the probability that they include the true value when applied repeatedly to many datasets. Similarly, for a Bayesian testing procedure we compute the probability of making Type I and Type II errors when the test is applied to many random datasets.301\. 

### 9.2.1 Bias-variance tradeoff

For difficult problems such as analyses with many parameters or regression with correlated predictors, adding prior information can stabilize the statistical analysis by reducing the variance of the estimator. On the other hand, if the prior information is erroneous, then this can lead to bias. The balance between these two consequences of incorporating prior information can be formalized by studying the bias/variance trade-off. Recall that the mean squared error is the sum of the variance and the squared bias,

MSE\[θ^(Y)\]\=Bias\[θ^(Y )\]2+Var\[θ^(Y)\].(9.8)

Therefore it is possible for a biased estimator to have smaller MSE than an unbiased estimator if the reduction in variance is large enough to offset the squared bias.

The optimal estimator as given by the bias-variance tradeoff often depends on the true value of the parameter and the sample size. As a concrete example, consider the simple case with Y1,...,Yn∼iid Normal(θ,σ2). For mathematical simplicity we assume _σ_ is fixed. We compare two estimators:

1. θ^1(Y)\=1n∑i\=1nYi\=Y¯
2. θ^2(Y)\=1n+m∑i\=1nYi\=cY¯

where c\=n/(n+m)∈\[0,1\]. The first estimator is the usual sample mean (i.e., the MLE or posterior mean under Jeffreys prior) and the second is the posterior mean assuming prior θ∼Normal(0,σ2/m).

### 9.2.2 Asymptotics

In [Section 3.1.3](./11-chapter3.md#sec3_1_3) we discussed the Bayesian central limit theorem that states that under general conditions the posterior converges to a normal distribution as the sample size increases. It can also be shown that under these conditions, and assuming the prior includes the true value, that the sampling distribution of the posterior mean θ^B\=E(θ|Y) converges (as n→∞) to the sampling distribution of the maximum likelihood estimator

θ^B∼Normal(θ0,Σ^MLE)(9.11)

where θ0 is the true value and Σ^MLE is defined in [Section 3.1.3](./11-chapter3.md#sec3_1_3). Therefore, the posterior mean is asymptotically unbiased. Further, it follows from classic results for maximum likelihood estimators that its variance decreases to zero as the sample size increases, and thus the posterior mean is a consistent estimator for θ under essentially the same conditions as the maximum likelihood estimator.

An asymptotic property that is uniquely Bayesian is posterior consistency. For a given dataset **Y**, the posterior probability that _θ_ is within _ϵ_ of the true value is

Prob(||θ−θ0||<ϵ|Y).(9.12)

A Bayesian procedure is said to possess posterior consistency if this probability is assured to converge to one as the sample size increases ([Figure 9.2](./17-chapter9.md#fig9_2)). [Appendix A.3](./18-appA.md#secA_3) provides a proof that the posterior distribution is consistent for parameters with discrete support under very general conditions on the likelihood and prior, and posterior consistency has been established for most finite-dimensional problems and many Bayesian nonparametric methods. For a more thorough discussion of posterior consistency see \[[62](./19-ref01.md#refbib62)\].

## 9.3 Simulation studies

For simple models such as the normal mean example in [Section 9.2.1](./17-chapter9.md#sec9_2_1) it is possible to derive the frequentist properties of an estimator using algebra. However, for more complicated models, a purely mathematical study is impossible. Especially in these complicated settings, it is important to understand the operating characteristics of a statistical procedure, and a simulation study is a general way to carry out this evaluation.

Just as MCMC is used to approximate complicated posterior distributions, simulation studies use Monte Carlo sampling to approximate complicated sampling distributions. To approximate, say, the mean squared error of an estimator, we generate _S_ independent and identically distributed datasets given the true parameter value _θ_0 and sample size _n_, and 303\. then compute the estimator for each simulated dataset. If we denote the _S_ simulated datasets as Y1,...,YS, then θ^(Y1),...,θ^(YS) are _S_ draws from the sampling distribution of the estimator θ^(Y). The mean squared error is then approximated as

MSE\[θ^(Y)\]≈1S∑s\=1S\[θ^(Ys)−θ0\]2.(9.13)

Other summaries of the sampling distribution such as bias and coverage are computed similarly. Of course, this is only a Monte Carlo estimate of the true MSE, and a different simulation experiment will give a different estimate. Therefore, the approximation should be accompanied by a standard error, sMSE/S, where sMSE is the sample standard deviation of the _S_ squared errors, \[θ^(Y1)−θ0\]2,...,\[θ^( YS)−θ0\]2.

A typical simulation study will generate data from a few values of _θ_0 and _n_ to understand when the method performs well and when it does not. Note that if θ^(Y) is a Bayesian estimator, say the posterior mean, then computing each θ^(Ys) may require MCMC and thus Bayesian simulation experiments may need many applications of MCMC and can be time consuming (running the _S_ chains in parallel is obviously helpful). For a thorough description of simulation studies, see \[[21](./19-ref01.md#refbib21)\] ([Chapter 9](./17-chapter9.md)).

**Variational Bayesian simulation study**: This simulation study examines the frequentist properties and a (naive) variational Bayesian (VB) analysis of a linear model. The data-generating model is the same multiple linear regression as the Bayesian LASSO simulation. For this simulation, we set n\=1000, p\=500 and σ02\=1. The covariates are drawn from a multivariate normal distribution with mean zero, variance one and Cor(Xij,Xik)\=0.9|j−k| to give strong collinearity. The model is not sparse with true parameters drawn (differently for each dataset) βj0∼Normal(0,0.052). We generate and analyze 100 datasets from this model.

The simulated data are analyzed using the conjugate prior βj∼iidNormal(0,τ2) with τ2\=10 and error variance fixed at the true value. With this model, the posterior has a closed form,

β|Y∼Normal{(R+gIp)−1Rβ^,σ2(R+gI)−1},

where g\=σ2/τ2, R\=XTX has diagonal elements Rj\=∑i\=1nXij2 and β^\=(XTX)−1XTY. Of course, we do not need a VB approximation in this case, but we implement a naive VB approximation to illustrate how one might evaluate the properties of an approximation in more challenging settings. If we select the multivariate normal posterior distribution for β with unknown mean and covariance matrix, then the VB posterior would be the exact posterior. However, we use a mean-field approximation

βj|Y∼indepNormal(mj,uj).

It can be shown that the posterior mean estimators, _m_ _j_, are the exact posterior mean, but the posterior variances are uj\=σ2/(Rj+g), which are generally smaller than the exact posterior variance.

Since the point estimates are the same for both the exact and VB posteriors, we compare methods using only the average variance and coverage (defined as in [Table 9.2](./17-chapter9.md#tbl9_2)). The average posterior variance is 0.019 for the exact posterior compared to 0.001 for the VB approximation. This leads to poor coverage, 39%, for the VB method compared to 95% for the exact posterior. Therefore, in this case with strong collinearity, the mean-field approximation does not give reliable frequentist uncertainty quantification and thus a better approximation to the posterior (i.e., a richer parametric model for the posterior) should be considered.
