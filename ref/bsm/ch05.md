Statistical hypothesis testing is a key ingredient of the scientific method. Hypothesis testing provides a rigorous comparison of experimental conditions or populations. Thus far, we have performed tests informally. For example, in [Figure 1.10](./09-chapter1.md#fig1_10) we compared two interval hypotheses for a binomial proportion _θ_. The null hypothesis was H1:θ<0.5, and the alternative hypothesis was H2:θ\>0.5. We used the posterior probability of each hypothesis as a measure of evidence for each, but we did not discuss how to use these probabilities to arrive at a decision. As another example, for multiple regression in [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2) we computed 95% intervals for regression coefficient _β_ _j_ and reached conclusions based on whether the interval included zero. A more formal test would set the null hypothesis to be that H1:βj\=0, i.e., a point hypothesis versus H2:βj≠0. However, under a continuous prior distribution such as the Gaussian distribution, the posterior probability of a single point such as βj\=0 is always zero, and thus new methods are needed to formalized this hypothesis test.

In this chapter, we introduce fundamental Bayesian hypothesis testing concepts such as decision theory and the Bayes factors. We also cover more advanced topics including multiple, A/B and sequential testing, and sample size calculations.

## 5.1 Bayes factors

Bayes factors \[[87](./19-ref01.md#refbib87)\] provide a formal summary of the evidence that the data support one statistical model over another. In hypotheses testing, the two models correspond to the null hypothesis H1 and the alternative hypothesis H2; therefore, below we use the terms hypotheses and models exchangeably. Although not necessary for the computation of Bayes factors, most testing problems can be framed so that both hypotheses are nested in a common model and distinguished by the model parameters. The full model might be Yi|μ∼iidnormal(μ,σ2), with H1 defined by μ≤0 and H2 defined by μ\>0.

In a Bayesian analysis, the unknown parameters are treated as random variables. If the hypotheses are stated as functions of the parameters, then they are also random variables; i.e., the posterior probability Prob(μ≤0|Y)\=Prob(H1|Y) is the posterior probability of the null hypothesis and Prob(μ\>0|Y)\=Prob(H2|Y). These posterior probabilities are the most intuitive summaries of uncertainty.

170\. A common mistake in a frequentist hypothesis testing is to confuse the p-value with the probability that the null hypothesis is true; from a frequentist perspective, the parameters and thus the hypotheses are fixed quantities and not random variables, and therefore it is not sensible to assign them probabilities. From the Bayesian perspective however giving the posterior probability of each hypothesis is a legitimate summary of uncertainty.

Posterior probabilities of hypotheses incorporate information from both the data and the prior. Bayes factors remove the effect of the prior and quantify the data's support of the hypotheses. The Bayes factor (BF) of H2 relative to H1 is the ratio of posterior odds to prior odds,

BF\=Prob(H2|Y)/Prob(H1|Y)Prob(H2)/Prob(H1).(5.1)

If the prior probability of the two hypotheses are equal, then the BF is simply the posterior odds Prob(H2|Y)/Prob(H1|Y). In this case, rejecting H1 if BF\>c is equivalent to rejecting H1 if the posterior probability of the alternative hypothesis is high, Prob(H2|Y)\>c/(c+1).

Hypothesis tests are usually designed to be conservative so that the null model is rejected in favor of the alternative only if the data strongly support this model. A rule of thumb \[[87](./19-ref01.md#refbib87)\] is that BF\>10 provides strong evidence of the alternative hypothesis compared to the null hypothesis, and BF\>100 is decisive evidence.

Bayesian decision theory provides a way to use posterior probabilities of hypotheses to make decisions. We defer a full discussion of decision theory to Chapter 9.1, but introduce the concepts needed for hypothesis testing here. Decision theory requires specifying the loss incurred for each type of error. For hypothesis testing there are two types of losses: a Type I error rejects the null hypothesis when it is in fact true, and a Type II error fails to reject the null hypothesis when it is in fact false. Define the loss of a Type _I_ error as _λ_ _I_. To be conservative, we might assume the loss of a Type I error is _c_ times the loss of a Type II error, so λI\=cλII with _c_ set to a large value, say c\=10 or c\=100.

The risk of a particular decision is defined as the expected value of the loss under the decision, which is the product of the probability of the decision being wrong and the loss associated with this error. The risk for the decision to reject the null hypothesis is

R2\=Prob(H1|Y)λI\=Prob(H1|Y)cλII,

and the risk for the decision to not reject the null hypothesis is

R1\=Prob(H2|Y)λII.

Risk is minimized by rejecting the null hypothesis if R2/R1<1, i.e.,

Prob(H2|Y)Prob(H1| Y)\>c,

or equivalently rejecting the null hypothesis if the posterior probability of the alternative hypothesis exceeds Prob(H2|Y)\>c/(1+c). To be concrete, if we assume a Type I error is c\=9 times as costly as a Type II error, then we reject the null if the posterior probability of the alternative exceeds 0.9\. If the prior probabilities of the hypotheses are equal, then the posterior odds are the Bayes factors, and we should reject if BF\>c.

This formulation using decision theory shows that labelling hypotheses as “null” versus “alternative” has no special role in Bayesian testing. That is, the results for the test of H1:θ<0 versus H2:θ\>0 with relative loss _c_ are identical to the test of H1:θ\>0 versus H2:θ<0 with relative loss 1/c. This symmetry is in contrast with classical testing, where the p-value is computing as if the null hypothesis is true and so changing the labels of the hypotheses can change the results.

171\. Computing BFs for problems with many parameters can be challenging. In general, model selection can be framed as treating the model as an unknown random variable H∈{H1,H2} with prior probability Prob(H\=Hj)\=qj. Conditioned on model _j_, i.e., H\=Hj, the remainder of the Bayesian model is

Y∼f(Y|θ;Hj) and θ∼π(θ|Hj).(5.2)

The BF requires the marginal posterior of the model integrating over uncertainty in the parameters,

Prob(H\=Hj|Y)\=∫p(θ,H\=Hj|Y)dθ.(5.3)

Unfortunately, marginalizing over the parameters (as in _m_ in Table 1.4) is rarely possible. Also, the marginal distribution of H is not defined for improper priors. Therefore, _BFs cannot be used with improper priors_. Even with proper priors, BFs can be very sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters as shown by the examples below. Partial or fractional Bayes factors \[[118](./19-ref01.md#refbib118)\] adapt the Bayes factor for improper priors. These methods have two stages. Part of the data's information is analyzed in the first stage, and the posterior from the first stage is used as a proper prior for the second-stage analysis of the remaining data and computing the Bayes factor.

MCMC provides a means of approximating BFs in special cases. The BF for nested models defined by intervals of the parameters is straightforward to compute using MCMC. For example, in the model above with Yi|μ∼iidNormal(μ,σ2) and μ∼Normal(0,102), and models H1 defined by μ≤0 and H2 defined by μ\>0, the posterior probability of Model 1 (2) can be approximated by generating samples from the posterior of the full model and recording the proportion of the samples for which _μ_ is negative (positive). [Section 6.2](./14-chapter6.md#sec6_2) provides a more general computational strategy for computing model probabilities.

MCMC can also be used for point-null hypotheses. If the hypotheses to be tested are H1:μ\=0 and H2:μ≠0, then the Bayes factor can be approximated by using MCMC by fitting the model Yi|μ∼iidnormal(μ,σ2) with μ\=γδ and prior distributions γ∼Bernoulli(0.5) and δ∼Normal(0,102). MCMC samples with δ\=0 correspond to H1, and samples with δ\=1 correspond to H2, therefore the posterior probability that δ\=1 approximates the posterior probability of H2 which can be used to compute the Bayes factor. [Section 6.2](./14-chapter6.md#sec6_2) expands on this idea in the context of multiple regression.

A final note on Bayes factors is that they resemble the likelihood ratio statistic, which is commonly used in frequentist hypothesis testing. Assuming equal priors, the Bayes factor is

BF\=∫f(Y|θ;H2)π(θ|H2)dθ∫f(Y|θ;H1)π(θ|H1)dθ,(5.4)

i.e., the ratio of marginal distribution of the data under the two models. This resembles the likelihood ratio from classical hypothesis testing,

LR\=f(Y|θ^2)f(Y |θ^1),(5.5)

where θ^j is the MLE under hypothesis j\=1,2. Therefore, both measures compare hypotheses based on the ratio of their likelihood, but the BF integrates over posterior uncertainty in the parameters, whereas the likelihood ratio statistic plugs in point estimates.

**Beta-binomial example**: To build intuition about BFs, we begin with the univariate binomial model and test whether the success probability equals 0.5\. Let Y|θ∼Binomial(n,θ), and consider two hypotheses:

H1:θ\=0.5 versus H2:θ≠0.5 and θ∼Beta(a,b).(5.6)

172\. The null hypothesis has no unknown parameters, and for H2 the parameter _θ_ can be integrated out giving the beta-binomial model for _Y_ (see [Appendix A.1](./18-appA.md#secA_1)). Therefore, these hypotheses about _θ_ correspond to two different models for the data

H1:Y∼Binomial(n,0.5) versus H2:Y∼Beta-Binomial(n,a,b).(5.7)

Assuming priors Prob(Hj)\=qj and observed data Y\=y,

BF(y)\=fBB(y;n,a,b)fB(y;n,0.5)(5.8)

where fBB and _f_ _B_ are the beta-binomial and binomial PMFs, respectively.

[Figure 5.1](./13-chapter5.md#fig5_1) plots BF(y) for n\=20 and different hyperparameters _a_ and _b_. Assuming the uniform prior (a\=b\=1) or Jeffreys prior (a\=b\=0.5), the BF exceeds 10 for y<5 or y\>15 successes, and exceeds 100 for y<4 or y\>16 successes; these scenarios give strong and decisive evidence, respectively, against the null hypothesis that θ\=0.5 in favor of the alternative that θ≠0.5. The BF is less than 10 for all possible _y_ for the strong prior centered on 0.5 (a\=b\=50). Under this prior the two hypotheses are similar, and it is difficult to distinguish between them. Finally, under the strong prior that _θ_ is close to one (a\=50 and b\=1), the BF exceeds 10 only for y\>16 successes; in this case _y_ near zero is even less likely under the alternative than under the null that θ\=0.5.

**Normal-mean example**: Say there is a single observation Y|μ∼Normal(μ,1), and the objective is to test whether μ\=0. The competing hypotheses are

H1:μ\=0 versus H2:μ≠0 and μ∼Normal(0,τ2).(5.9)

173\. Given that we observe Y\=y, it can be shown that the BF of H2 relative to H1 is

BF(y)\=(1+τ2)−1/2exp\[y22(τ21+τ2)\].(5.10)

For fixed _τ_, BF(y) increases to infinity as _y_2 increases as expected because data far from zero contradict H1:μ\=0. However, for any fixed _y_, BF(y) converges to zero as the prior variance _τ_2 increases. Therefore, even if the observation is 10 standard deviation units above zero, the BF favors the null model with μ\=0 if the prior variance is sufficiently large. This is an example of Lindley's paradox \[[97](./19-ref01.md#refbib97)\] where Bayesian tests can perform poorly depending on the prior. For the normal means problem, this odd result occurs because with large _τ_ these data are unlikely under both models. For example, the probability of |Y|∈\[9,11\] is very small under the null hypothesis that μ\=0, but under the alternative hypothesis this probability converges to zero as _τ_ increases because the marginal distribution of _Y_ (averaging over _μ_) becomes increasingly diffuse.

BFs are not defined with improper priors, and this example suggests that they can have strange properties for uninformative priors. It has been argued \[[16](./19-ref01.md#refbib16)\] that the standard half-Cauchy prior μ∼t1(0,1) is preferred to the large-variance normal prior because this prior is diffuse without having a variance parameter to tune. In general, prior selection has more impact for hypothesis testing than estimation, making it imperative to report Bayes factors for multiple priors to illuminate this sensitivity.

Lindley's paradox is more pronounced for tests of point null hypotheses such as H1:μ\=0 than for one-sided tests such as

H1:μ≤0 versus H2:μ\>0.

To compute the BF for these hypotheses we simply fit the Bayesian model Y|μ∼Normal(μ,1) and μ∼Normal(0,τ2) and compute Prob(H2|Y)\=Prob(μ\>0|Y) using the results in [Section 2.1.3](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1_3). This test is stable as the prior variance increases because the BF converges to Φ(y)/\[1−Φ(y)\]\>0, where Φ is the standard normal CDF. Since 1−Φ(y) is the p-valuefor the classical one-sided z-test, the BF test with large variance is equivalent to the frequentist test.

Tests based on credible sets are another remedy to Lindley's paradox. That is, we simply fit a Bayesian model Y|μ∼Normal(μ,1) and μ∼Normal(0,τ2) as in [Section 4.1](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_1) and reject the null hypothesis that μ\=0 if the posterior credible set for _μ_ excludes zero. As the prior variance increases, this rule has the same frequentist operating characteristics as the classic two-sided z-test (or t-test with unknown error variance). Therefore, this approach is not sensitive to the prior and has appealing frequentist properties including controlling Type I error.

## 5.2 Multiple testing

Traditional experiments are designed to test a single hypothesis. However, modern data analyses often present many variables and thus many possible tests. Consider a genetic study where _m_ genetic markers are used as predictors of a patient's health outcome. We might fit the multiple regression (Chapter 4.2) Yi\=∑j\=1mXijβj+εi where Xij is the expression at marker _j_ for subject _i_ and _β_ _j_ is the effect of marker _j_ on the health outcome. This leads to _m_ potential tests,

H1j:βj\=0 versus H2j:βj≠0,

174\. for j∈{1,...,m}. For genetic studies, the number of tests _m_ is often in the thousands, and thus new testing procedures are required to avoid being overwhelmed with spurious results. As an extreme case, if m\=10,000 and in fact none of the markers have any effect on the health response, then a method that incorrectly rejects H1j with probability 0.05 will result in an average of 500 false positives, which is clearly unacceptable.

A global test is a potential remedy for this multiple testing problem. The global test replaces the _m_ individual tests with the single test of hypotheses

H1:βj\=0 for all j versus H2:βj≠0 for at least one j.

Since this reduces the problem to a single test comparing two models, standard methods can be used such as cross-validation or computing the posterior probability of H1 and rejecting H1 if its probability is small.

If we assume the global null hypothesis is false and return to multiple testing, the challenge is identifying the subset of the _m_ tests that should be rejected. To formalize this problem, we establish notation for our decision (_r_ _j_) and the true status (_δ_ _j_) of test _j_. Let rj\=1 if we reject H1j in favor of H2j and rj\=0 if we do not reject. Ideally, the decisions _r_ _j_ align with the true status of the hypotheses δj\=0 if H1j is true and δj\=1 if H2j is true. For a set of decisions r1,...,rm, the number of “discoveries” is ∑j\=1mrj. However, some of these discoveries may be false, i.e., rj\=1 but in reality δj\=0 or equivalently 1−δj\=1. The number of false discoveries is then ∑j\=1mrj(1−δj) and the false discovery proportion is

FDP\=∑j\=1mrj(1−δj)∑j\=1mrj.

A multiple testing procedure might want to control this proportion to be small, say FDP≈α\=0.1.

Unfortunately, FDP cannot be computed because we do not know the true status of the hypotheses, _δ_ _j_. The Bayesian false discovery rate \[[147](./19-ref01.md#refbib147)\] evaluates the posterior mean of _FDP_ averaging over the posterior distribution of the _δ_ _j_. The posterior mean is

BFDR\=∑j\=1mrj(1−qj)∑j\=1mrj

where _q_ _j_ is the posterior probability of H2j (as computed by MCMC for example). For any given set of decision r1,...,rm, we can compute BFDR and thus select the decision that gives the most discoveries with BFDR≈0.1.

To make this search practical for large problems, the decision rule can be simplified to thresholding the posterior probabilities. That is, we reject the null hypothesis H1j if the posterior probability of the alternative is high and let rj(t)\=1 if qj\>t and rj(t)\=0 otherwise. Then the BFDR is a function of _t_ (as in the right panel of [Figure 5.2](./13-chapter5.md#fig5_2)), and we can search for the _t_ so that

BFDR(t)\=∑j\=1mrj(t)(1−qj)∑j\=1mrj(t)≈α,

## 5.3 A/B testing and the Bayesian bandit

The term A/B testing was popularized by the technology sector, but the testing methods apply to many other settings. The task is to conduct an experiment to compare several experimental conditions and identify which gives the best performance. For example, a marketing company may run three versions of an advertising campaign to determine which is optimal. This task does not fit the classic frequentist hypothesis testing framework since there is no natural control group or null hypothesis. The Bayesian approach is intuitively 176\. Figure on this page. appealing because it returns the posterior probability that each condition is optimal and thus directly addresses the study objectives.

Let _n_ _j_ be the number of observations made under condition j∈{1,...,J} and Yij be the response for observation i∈{1,...,nj} in group _j_. A/B testing can be applied to continuous (time spent on a website), count (number of clicks) or binary (purchase or not) data. The most basic models for these data types are Gaussian, Poisson and Bernoulli, respectively. Assuming independence, for Poisson and Bernoulli observations we can aggregate across observations giving Yj\=∑i\=1njYij. The likelihoods for these models are

* Continuous response: Yij|θ∼indepNormal(θj,σj2),
* Count response: Yj|θ∼indepPoisson(njθj),
* Binary response: Yj|θ∼indepBinomial(nj,θj).

A default prior in this case in the objective Jeffreys prior (Chapter 4.2.2.1), but informative priors can also be used if this information is available.

The objective of the analysis is to compare _θ_ _j_ across the _J_ conditions and compute the posterior probability that each has the largest mean, i.e.,

pj\=Prob(θj\=max{θ1,...,θJ}|Y),

which can often be computed analytically for two groups and approximated using Monte Carlo sampling in other cases. The groups can also be weighted by cost. For example, if profit of a sale is _P_ and advertising campaign _j_ costs _C_ _j_ units per user and gives probability of purchase _θ_ _j_, then the value of campaign _j_ is Vj\=Pθj−Cj, and we might compute

pj\=Prob(Vj\=max{V1,...,VJ}|Y),

which is also straightforward to approximate using Monte Carlo sampling if _P_ and _C_ _j_ are known.

These two posterior distributions are plotted in [Figure 5.3](./13-chapter5.md#fig5_3). Although the retention rates are similar for both groups, there is a slight preference for Level 30 with (computed via Monte Carlo sampling from the posterior beta distributions above) Prob(θ1\>θ2|Y1,Y2)\=0.96. With such a large dataset, the results are not sensitive to the prior with Prob(θ1\>θ2|Y1,Y2) remaining 0.96 for a\=b equal 0.1, 1 or 10.

**Bayesian gambit**: A/B testing is used to identify the optimal strategy to be used in practice. Conducting an experiment and analyzing the data are crucial to decision making, but time spent experimenting is time lost implementing the optimal strategy. This is a basis of the so-called multi-armed bandit problem \[[142](./19-ref01.md#refbib142)\], where the objective is take actions sequentially to optimize overall results while accounting for uncertainty in the reward of the potential actions. Thompson sampling \[[136](./19-ref01.md#refbib136), [154](./19-ref01.md#refbib154)\] is a Bayesian approach that balances experimentation and optimization. Thompson sampling randomly assigns the strategy to use at a given time point based on posterior probabilities determined using all information collected up to the decision point.

Consider the following application. We are designing a mobile heath app that sends reminders to users to take an action. There are J\=3 treatment strategies under consideration: (1) text message, (2) email and (3) both text message and email. Let _θ_ _j_ be the probability that a user takes the desired action when given a reminder following strategy j∈{1,2,3}. An initial A/B test is conducted with nj\=100 observations in each group. The model is Yj|θj∼Binomial(nj,θj) and prior θj∼Beta(0.5,0.5). The posterior distributions of this test are plotted in [Figure 5.4](./13-chapter5.md#fig5_4) (top left); it appears that the text and email combination is preferred, but the probability it is optimal is only 0.68.177\. 178\.

## 5.4 Sample size and power calculations

Thus far we have considered methods to test hypotheses given an observed dataset, but have ignored decisions in data collection. Although other decisions can be considered (see \[[125](./19-ref01.md#refbib125)\] for a review), a critical design criterion is selecting the sample size, _n_. Selecting the sample size poses a trade-off between gathering sufficient information to reliably conduct inference with the expense of collecting data. In this section, we discuss methods to select the sample size that acknowledge this trade-off.

To fix ideas, consider the simple experiment where _n_ independent trials will be conducted, each with success probability _θ_. The data is then generated as Y|θ∼Binomial(n,θ). Below we refer to the prior distribution that will be used to analyze the data as the _analysis prior_, which for this example is taken to be the conjugate Jeffreys prior θ∼Beta(a,b) with a\=b\=0.5. The posterior is then θ|Y∼Beta(Y+a,n−Y+b). The Bayes factor, BF(Y), for testing the null hypotheses that θ\=θ0 versus the alternative that θ≠θ0 and follows prior θ∼Beta(a,b) is given in (5.8), and we reject the null hypothesis in favor of the alternative if BF(Y)\>c.

There is nothing in this analysis that requires frequentist notions of probability because it is conducted conditioned on an observed dataset. However, in the design stage before the data is collected, we must necessarily consider the data to be random and thus both Bayesian (random _θ_) and frequentist (random _Y_) notions of probability and uncertainty. To illustrate this, say the sample size _n_ is selected so that the posterior variance is sufficiently small. For the binomial model, the posterior variance is

V(Y)\=(Y+a)(n−Y+b)(n+a+b)2(n+a+b+1),

which depends on _Y_ and can thus not be computed at the design stage. To account for this uncertainty about _Y_, we could view _Y_ as random and select _n_ so the expected value (over _Y_) of V(Y) is sufficiently small.

Treating _Y_ as random to compute the mean of V(Y) requires specifying its distribution. It is reasonable to use the binomial model, but to complete the specification requires selecting _θ_ or its distribution, referred to as the _design prior_ \[[137](./19-ref01.md#refbib137)\]. The design prior need not be the same as the analysis prior, which is often set to be diffuse or even improper to reflect prior ignorance, and is often chosen to be a range of values that are plausible and reflect scientifically-meaningful differences. For example, if the study is designed to determine if _θ_ differs from 0.50, then the design prior might be θ∼Uniform(0.55,0.65) An advantage of the Bayesian approach to sample size calculation is the ability to incorporate results from preliminary data or other sources into the design prior distribution.

If the analysis will be a hypothesis test of the null hypothesis that θ\=θ0 versus the alternative that θ≠θ0, then the design is typically based on frequentist properties of the test including:

* The **Type-I error rate** is the probability of mistakenly rejecting the null hypothesis assuming data are generated under the null hypothesis with θ\=θ0 and a Bayesian test is performed on the observed data.
* 181\. The **power** is the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis assuming data are generated under the alternative hypothesis/design prior and a Bayesian test is performed on the observed data.

A Bayesian experimental design for this test selects the sample size, _n_, and other design factors including the Bayes-factor threshold to reject the null hypothesis, _c_, to control Type I error and/or power.

[Listing 5.2](./13-chapter5.md#list5_2) provides three ways to select _n_ for the beta-binomial example via Monte Carlo simulation. Many of these quantities could be computed mathematically without sampling for this simple case \[[34](./19-ref01.md#refbib34)\], but in general, Monte Carlo sampling provides a flexible and reliable way to approximate design quantities of interest \[[160](./19-ref01.md#refbib160)\]. The first approach selects _n_ so that the posterior standard deviation is sufficiently small. Many datasets are generated from the design prior, and for each dataset the posterior standard deviation is computing. [Figure 5.5](./13-chapter5.md#fig5_5) (left) plots the sampling distribution of the posterior standard deviation as a function of the sample size. If the objective is to have the posterior standard deviation no larger than 0.05, then the sample size should be at least n\=100.

The remaining two approaches select _n_ to control the power of the test of the null hypothesis that θ\=0.5 at 0.80\. The first test simply computes the posterior 95% credible interval and rejects the null if _θ_0 is outside the interval. The Type I error of this test is controlled between 0.049 and 0.066 depending on _n_, and n\=300 gives power exceeding 0.8 ([Figure 5.5](./13-chapter5.md#fig5_5), right). The final design rejects the null hypothesis if BF(Y)\>c. The threshold _c_ (which depends on _n_) is selected to fix the Type I error at 0.05 by simulating data from the null hypothesis and setting _c_ to the 0.95 quantile of the sampling distribution of BF(Y). With _c_ fixed, the power is computed and also results in a sample size of n\=300 to control power at 0.80.182\.
