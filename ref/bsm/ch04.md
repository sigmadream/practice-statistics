## 4.1 122\. Analysis of normal means

### 4.1.1 One-sample/paired analysis

In a one-sample study, the _n_ observations are modeled as Yi|μ,σ2∼iidNormal(μ,σ2) and the objective is to determine if μ\=0. This model is often applied to experiments where each unit _i_ actually has two measurements taken under different conditions and _Y_ _i_ is the difference between the measurements. For example, say student _i_'s math scores before and after a tutorial session are Z0i and Z1i, respectively, then testing if the (population) mean of Yi\=Z1i−Z0i is zero is a way to evaluate the effectiveness of the tutorial sessions.

A Bayesian analysis specifies priors for _μ_ and _σ_2 and then summarizes the marginal posterior of _μ_ accounting for uncertainty in _σ_2. In this chapter we use the Jeffreys prior, but conjugate normal/inverse gamma priors can also be used. In most cases it is sufficient to plot the posterior density p(μ|Y) and report the posterior mean and 95% interval. We will also address the problem using a formal hypothesis test. In this chapter we will compute the posterior probabilities of the one-sided hypotheses H1:μ≤0 and H2:μ\>0; in [Chapter 6](./14-chapter6.md) we test the point hypothesis H1:μ\=0 versus H2:μ≠0.

**Known variance**: Conditional on _σ_2, _μ_ has Jeffreys prior π(μ)∝1. The posterior is μ|Y∼Normal(Y¯,σ2n), and thus the 100(1−α)% posterior credible interval for _μ_ is

Y¯±zα/2σn,(4.1)

where _z_ _τ_ is the _τ_ quantile of the standard normal distribution (i.e., the normal distribution with mean zero and variance one). This exactly matches the classic 100(1−α)% confidence interval. While the credible interval and confidence interval are numerically identical in this case, they are interpreted differently. The Bayesian credible interval quantifies uncertainty about _μ_ given this dataset, **Y**, whereas the confidence interval quantifies the anticipated variation in Y¯ if we were to repeat the experiment.

For the test of null hypothesis H1:μ≤0 versus the alternative hypothesis H2:μ\>0, the posterior probability of the null hypothesis is

Prob(H1|Y)\=Prob(μ<0|Y)\=Φ(−Z)(4.2)

where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and Z\=nY¯/σ and thus matches exactly with a frequentist p-value. By definition, Φ(zτ)\=τ, and so the decision rule to reject H1 in favor of H2 if the posterior probability of H1 is less than _α_ is equivalent to rejecting H1 if −Z<zα, or equivalently if Z\>z1−α (−zα\=z1−α due to the symmetry of the standard normal PDF). Therefore, the decision rule to reject H1 in favor of H2 at significance level _α_ if Z\>z1−α is identical to the classic one-sided z-test. However, unlike the classical test, we can quantify our uncertainty using the posterior probability that the hypothesis H1 (or H2) is true since we have computed Prob(H1|Y).

**Unknown variance**: As shown in [Section 2.4](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_4), the Jeffreys prior for (μ,σ2) is

π(μ,σ2)∝(1σ2)3/2.(4.3)

[Appendix A.3](./18-appA.md#secA_3) shows that the marginal posterior of _μ_ integrating over _σ_2 is

μ|Y∼tn(Y¯,σ^2/n),(4.4)

123\. where σ^2\=∑i\=1n(Yi−Y¯)2/n’ i.e., the posterior is Student's t-distribution with location Y¯, variance parameter σ^2/n and _n_ degrees of freedom. Posterior inference such as credible sets or the posterior probability that _μ_ is positive follow from the quantiles of Student's t-distribution. The credible set is slightly different than the frequentist confidence interval because the degrees of freedom in the classic t-test is n−1, whereas the degrees of freedom in the posterior is _n_; this is the effect of the prior on _σ_2.

In classical statistics, when _σ_2 is unknown, the Z-test based on the normal distribution is replaced with the t-test based on Student's t-distribution. Similarly, the posterior distribution of the mean changes from a normal distribution given _σ_2 to Student's t-distribution when uncertainty about the variance is considered. [Figure 4.1](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_1) compares the Gaussian and t density functions. The density functions are virtually identical for n\=25, but for n\=5 the t-distribution has heavier tails than the Gaussian distribution; this is the effect of accounting for uncertainty in _σ_2.

μ|Y∼tn−1(Y¯,σ^2/n).(4.5)

With this prior, the posterior distribution matches the frequentist sampling distribution, and thus credible intervals match confidence intervals, etc.

### 4.1.2 124\. Comparison of two normal means

The two-sample test compares the mean in two groups. For example, an experiment might take the blood pressure of a random sample of _n_1 mail carriers that walk their route and _n_2 mail carriers that drive their route to determine if these groups have different mean blood pressure. Let Yi∼iidNormal(μ,σ2) for i\=1,...,n1 and Yi∼iidNormal(μ+δ,σ2) for i\=n1+1,...,n1+n2\=n, so that _δ_ is the difference in means and the parameter of interest. Denote the sample mean of the _n_ _j_ observations in group j\=1,2 as Y¯j and the group-specific variance estimators as s^12\=∑i\=1n1(Yi−Y¯1)2/n1 and s^22\=∑i\=n1+1n1+n2(Yi−Y¯2)2/n2.

**Conditional distribution with the variance fixed**: Conditional on the variance and flat prior π(μ,δ)∝1 it can be shown that the posterior of the difference in means is

δ|Y∼Normal(Y¯2−Y¯1,σ2\[1n1+1n2\]).(4.6)

As in the one-sample case, posterior intervals and probabilities of hypotheses can be computed using the quantiles of the normal distribution. As in the one-sample case, the credible set and rejection rule match the classic confidence interval and one-sided z-test numerically but have different interpretations.

**Unknown variance**: The Jeffreys prior for (μ,δ,σ2) is ([Section 2.4](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_4))

π(μ,δ,σ2)∝1(σ2)2.(4.7)

[Appendix A.3](./18-appA.md#secA_3) shows (as a special case of multiple linear regression, see [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2)) the marginal posterior distribution of _δ_ integrating over both _μ_ and _σ_2 is

δ|Y∼tn\[Y¯2−Y¯1,σ^2(1n1+1n2)\],(4.8)

where σ^2\=(n1s^12+n2s^22)/n is a pooled variance estimator. As with the one-sample model, the difference between the posterior for the known-variance versus unknown-variance cases is that an estimate of _σ_2 is inserted in the posterior, and the Gaussian distribution is replaced with Student's t-distribution with _n_ degrees of freedom. As in the one-sample case, using the alternative Jeffreys prior π(μ,σ2)∝1/σ2 gives posterior distribution that matches the frequentist sampling distribution,

δ|Y∼tn−2\[Y¯2−Y¯1,σ^2(1n1+1n2)\].(4.9)

In the Bayesian analysis we did not “plug in” an estimate of _σ_2. Rather, by accounting for its uncertainty and marginalizing over _μ_ and _σ_2 the posterior for _δ_ happens to have a natural estimator of _σ_2 in _δ_'s scale. [Listing 4.1](./12-chapter4.md#list4_1) implements this method.

## 4.2 126\. Linear regression

The multiple linear regression model with response (also called the dependent variable, outcome or label) _Y_ _i_ and covariates (also called independent variables, predictors, features or inputs) Xi1,...,Xip is

Yi\=∑j\=1pXijβj+εi,(4.11)

where β\=(β1,...,βp)T are the regression coefficients and the errors (also called the residuals) are εi∼iid Normal(0,σ2). We assume throughout that Xi1\=1 for all _i_ so that _β_1 is the intercept (i.e., the mean if all other covariates are zero).

The regression model assumes the response _Y_ _i_ is continuous, but can accommodate either continuous or categorical variables as covariates. Categorical predictors are typically included as binary dummy variables and can include the mean comparisons and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) models as special cases. With only the intercept term (p\=1) and _β_1 relabeled as _μ_, the linear regression model simplifies to the one-sample mean model in [Section 4.1.1](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_1_1). The two-sample mean model in [Section 4.1.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_1_2) is a special case with p\=2, Xi2 set to the binary dummy variable equal to one if observation _i_ is from the second group and zero otherwise, and the parameters relabelled as β1\=μ, and β2\=δ. Similarly, a categorical variable with _K_ levels can be included using an ANOVA framework with a different mean for each level by setting with Xi1\=1 and including K−1 dummy variables Xi2,...,XiK with Xik equal 1 if observation _i_ is from group _k_ and zero otherwise.

The coefficient _β_ _j_ is the slope associated with the jth covariate. For the remainder of this subsection we will assume that all p−1 covariates (excluding the intercept term) have been standardized to have mean zero and variance one so the prior can be specified without considering the scales of the covariates. That is, if the original covariate _j_, X\~ij, had sample mean X¯j and standard deviation s^j, then we set Xij\=(X\~ij−X¯j)/s^j. After standardization, the slope _β_ _j_ is interpreted as the change in the mean response corresponding to an increase of one standard deviation unit (s^j) in the original covariate. Similarly, βj/s^j is the expected increase in the mean response associated with an increase of one in the original covariate. The model actually has p+1 parameters (_p_ regression coefficients and variance _σ_2), but we temporarily use _p_ as the number of regression parameters and not the total number of parameters in the model.

A key concept when selecting a regression model and interpreting results is collinearity. Collinearity generally refers to correlation between the covariates Xij. Recall the interpretation of _β_ _j_ is the effect of increasing Xij with all other covariates held fixed, but this is hard to envision if Xij is strongly correlated with other covariates. Consider the extreme case with temperature and heat index as covariates. Interpreting the slope for temperature requires envisioning the heat index held fixed while temperature increases, which is counter-intuitive.

When the _n_ observations of one of the covariates is exactly a linear combinations of the _n_ values of the other covariates, then the covariates are said to be linearly dependent. If covariates are not linearly dependent, then they are said to be linearly independent. If the number of predictors exceeds the number of observations, p\>n, then the covariates will automatically be linearly dependent. As discussed below, the methods that can be applied differ based on whether the covariates are linearly independent.

### 4.2.1 127\. Classical approaches

Non-Bayesian estimators of β range from classical least squares for simple problems to modern penalized regression methods for more complex settings. The Bayesian approaches described in the following sections often give identical point estimators to penalized regression estimators with the prior distribution corresponding to the penalty term. Of course, even in cases with identical point estimators the interpretation of uncertainty is different between the Bayesian and classical approaches.

The classical estimator of β is the least squares estimator

β^LS\=argminβ∑i\=1n(Yi−∑j\=1pXijβj)2.(4.12)

This estimator is only valid when the covariates are linearly independent. The sampling distribution of β^LS follows a normal distribution if _σ_2 is known and a Student's t-distribution when _σ_2 must be estimated, as is typical.

The likelihood function for the linear model with _n_ observations is the product of _n_ Gaussian PDFs with means ∑j\=1pXijβj and variance _σ_2,

f(Y|β,σ2)\=∏i\=1n12πσexp\[−12σ2(Yi−∑j\=1pXijβj)2\].(4.13)

Since maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood, the maximum likelihood estimator is identical to the least squares estimator, i.e., β^MLE\=β^LS.

Penalized regression is often used for challenging problems with strong collinearity or a large number of covariates. The ridge regression estimator \[[79](./19-ref01.md#refbib79)\] is

β^R\=argminβ∑i\=1n(Yi−∑j\=1pXijβj)2+λ∑j\=2pβj2.(4.14)

The addition of the penalty term λ∑j\=2pβj2 encourages solutions with estimates near zero and discourages/penalizes implausibly large estimates, thus adding stability. In fact, this (and the LASSO below) solution is valid even if p\>n and/or the covariates are linearly dependent. The tuning parameter _λ_ determines how strongly the estimator is shrunk towards zero and can be chosen via cross validation.

While ridge regression adds stability, the elements of β^R are never exactly zero, and thus all covariates, even those with small effect estimates, are included in the predictive model. The famous LASSO \[[155](./19-ref01.md#refbib155)\] penalized regression estimator simultaneously shrinks estimates to zero and gives some estimates with β^j set exactly to zero, and thus performs variable selection simultaneously with estimation. The estimator is

β^LASSO\=argminβ∑i\=1n(Yi−∑j\=1pXijβj)2+λ∑j\=2p|βj|,(4.15)

where again the tuning parameter _λ_ can be chosen using cross validation. The LASSO estimator differs from the ridge estimator only in that the squared penalty is replaced by the absolute penalty; however, the discontinuity of the absolute value at the origin gives solutions with exact zeros and thus the properties of the LASSO regressions are quite different than ridge regression.

### 4.2.2 128\. Prior distributions for Bayesian linear regression

The model assumptions and likelihood in a Bayesian linear regression are identical to classical linear regression, that is, Yi\=∑j\=1pXijβj+εi where εi∼iid Normal(0,σ2). A Bayesian analysis requires prior distributions for the regression coefficients _β_ _j_ and variance _σ_2. This section provides several options and discusses the scenarios that favor each option. We first introduce the improper Jeffreys prior that, analogous to least squares in classical statistics, is a default choice for regression with a small number of covariates. This is generalized to the Gaussian priors which is useful in the presence of collinearity or for incorporating prior information. Finally we discuss continuous shrinkage priors that are useful when there are many covariates and the prior belief is that many are irrelevant with regression coefficients near zero.

#### 4.2.2.1 Jeffreys prior

Conditioned on _σ_2, the Jeffreys prior is π(β)∝1. This improper prior leads to a proper posterior under the same conditions required for the least squares estimator to be valid, namely that the covariates are linearly independent. The posterior of β is multivariate normal with mean β^LS and covariance that depends on _σ_2 and the covariates Xij. In fact, the posterior distribution of β is identical to the sampling distribution of the least squares estimator. Therefore, the posterior credible intervals from this model will numerically match the confidence intervals from a least squares analysis with known error variance.

With _σ_2 unknown, the Jeffreys prior is π(β,σ2)∝(σ2)−p/2−1 ([Section 2.4](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_4)). Under this prior with n\>p and linearly independent covariates, the marginal posterior of β averaging over _σ_2 follows the _p_\-dimensional Student's t-distribution with location β^LS and covariance matrix that depends on σ^2 and the covariates. As described in [Section 4.2.3](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2_3), the estimator of _σ_2 and the degrees of freedom differ slightly from the least-squares procedure, but these differences are small if _n_ is much larger than _p_, and thus the credible intervals approximate the least-squares confidence intervals in most cases. Alternatively, the product of independent Jeffreys prior π(β,σ2)∝1/σ2 gives

β|Y∼tn−p(β^LS,Σ^)(4.16)

where Σ^ is the estimated covariance of the sampling distribution of β^LS used in least-squares analysis (see (4.23) for details). That is, under this prior, the posterior distribution of β matches the sampling distribution of β^LS.

#### 4.2.2.2 Gaussian prior

For high-dimensional cases with more predictors than observations, the improper Jeffreys prior does not lead to a valid posterior distribution. Impropriety can be remedied using a proper prior, which always leads to a proper posterior. Even in less extreme situations with moderate _p_, a proper prior can stabilize the posterior and give better results than the improper prior.

The conjugate prior (conditioned on _σ_2) for β is the multivariate normal distribution. The most common approach assumes prior independence for the elements of β with β1|σ2∼Normal(μ1,σ2ω2) for the intercept and βj|σ2∼iidNormal(μj,σ2τ2) for the slopes with j\>1. We include _σ_2 in the prior variance to account for the scale of the response, although this is not required. This prior can be informative if _ω_ and _τ_ are small and the _μ_ _j_ are selected based on prior knowledge. However, typically the prior is uninformative with μj\=0 for all _j_ and _ω_ and _τ_ set to be large values or given uninformative prior distributions. Since μj\=0 is common, we assume the prior mean is zero unless otherwise noted.

129\. Under this prior with 1/ω2\=0 and λ\=1/τ2, the posterior-mean (and MAP) estimator is the ridge regression estimator \[[79](./19-ref01.md#refbib79)\]

E(β|Y)\=βR\=argminβ∑i\=1n(Yi−∑j\=1pXijβj)2+λ∑j\=2pβj2.(4.17)

Ridge regression is often used to stabilize least squares problems when the number of predictors is large and/or the covariates are collinear. In ridge regression, the tuning parameter _λ_ can be selected based on cross-validation. In a fully Bayesian analysis, _τ_2 can either be fixed to a large value to give an uninformative prior, or given a conditionally conjugate inverse gamma prior as in [Listing 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#list4_2) to allow the data to determine how much to shrink the coefficients towards zero. If _τ_2 (and thus λ\=1/τ2) is given a prior, then the intercept term _β_1 should be given a different variance because it plays a different role than the other regression coefficients.

#### 4.2.2.3 Double exponential prior

In regressions with many predictors it is often assumed that most of the _p_ predictors have little effect on the response. The Gaussian prior can reflect this prior belief with a small prior variance, but a small prior variance has a negative side effect; it shrinks the posterior mean of the important regression coefficients toward zero and and induces bias. An alternative \[[120](./19-ref01.md#refbib120)\] is to use double exponential priors for the _β_ _j_ that have ([Figure 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_2)) a peak at zero to reflect the prior belief that most predictors have no effect on the response, but a heavy tail to reflect the prior belief that there are a few predictors with strong effects. [Listing 4.4](./12-chapter4.md#list4_4) provides JAGS code to implement this model (although the R package BLR \[[41](./19-ref01.md#refbib41)\] is likely faster). This notion of prior distribution with concentration at zero is extended to other distributions in Chapter 8.1.

### 4.2.3 Linear regression in matrix notation

The linear regression model is conveniently written in matrix notation. While matrix notation is not necessary to implement Bayesian linear regression, for Gaussian prior distributions this compact notation leads to a closed-form expression for the posterior distribution and thus eliminates the need for MCMC. Let Y\=(Y1,...,Yn)T be the response vector of length _n_ and the design matrix **X** be the n×p matrix with the first column equal to the vector of ones for the intercept and column _j_ having elements X1j,...,Xnj. The linear regression model is then

Y∼Normal(Xβ,σ2In)(4.19)

131\. 132\. where In is the n×n identity matrix with ones on the diagonal (so all responses have variance _σ_2) and zeros off the diagonal (so the responses are uncorrelated).

**Classical approaches**: The usual least squares estimator in matrix notation is

β^LS\=(XTX)−1XTY.(4.20)

Note that the least squares estimator is unique only if XTX is full rank (i.e., p<n and none of **X**'s columns are redundant) and (XTX)−1, and thus the estimator is undefined if XTX is not full rank. Assuming **X** is full rank and _σ_2 is known, the sampling distribution is β^LS∼Normal(β0,σ2(XTX)−1), where β0 is the true value. Typically _σ_2 is estimated based on the residual variance, leading to the Student's t-distribution for the sampling distribution of β^LS that is used to construct frequentist confidence intervals and p-values.

The ridge regression estimator can also be written in closed form

β^R\=(XTX+λD)−1XTY(4.21)

where **D** is the p×p diagonal matrix with zero in the first diagonal element for the intercept and one in the remaining diagonal elements. Given _λ_, the sampling distribution of β^R is also a Student's t-distribution that can be used for uncertainty quantification. Unfortunately, the LASSO estimator β^LASSO does not have a closed form, and thus a sophisticated numerical optimization routine is required for computation, and the sampling distribution is complex.

**Jeffreys prior**: Conditioned on _σ_2, the Jeffreys prior is π(β)∝1. This improper prior only leads to a proper posterior if XTX has full rank, which is the same condition needed for the least squares estimator to be unique. Assuming this condition is met, the posterior is

β|Y,σ2∼Normal\[β^LS,σ2(XTX )−1\].(4.22)

The posterior mean is the least squares solution and the posterior covariance matrix is the covariance matrix of the sampling distribution of the least squares estimator. Therefore, the posterior credible intervals from this model will numerically match the confidence intervals from a least squares analysis with known error variance.

133\. With _σ_2 unknown, the Jeffreys prior is π(β,σ2)∝(σ2)−p/2−1 ([Section 2.4](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_4)). Assuming XTX has full rank, [Appendix A.3](./18-appA.md#secA_3) shows that β|Y∼tn\[β^LS,σ^2(XTX)−1\], where σ^2\=(Y−Xβ^LS)T(Y−Xβ^LS)/n. That is, the marginal posterior of β follows the _p_\-dimensional Student's t-distribution with location β^LS, scale matrix σ^2(XTX)−1, and _n_ degrees of freedom. Under the alternative Jeffreys prior π(β,σ2)∝1/σ2 that is often preferred for moderate to large _p_, the posterior is

β|Y∼tn−p\[β^LS,σ^2(XTX)−1\].(4.23)

A property of the multivariate t distribution is that the marginal distribution of each element is univariate t so that under the alternative Jeffreys prior

βj|Y∼tn−p(β^j,sj2)(4.24)

where β^j is the jth element of β^LS and sj2 is the jth diagonal element of σ^2(XTX)−1 (the degrees of freedom is _n_ under the full Jeffreys prior). Therefore, the joint distribution of all the regression coefficients as well as the marginal distribution of each regression coefficient belong to a known family of distributions, and can thus be computed without MCMC sampling. [Listing 4.5](./12-chapter4.md#list4_5) provides R code to compute the posterior mean and 95% intervals.

**Gaussian priors**: For high-dimensional cases with more predictors than observations the improper Jeffreys prior does not lead to a valid posterior distribution, and thus a proper prior is required. The conjugate prior (conditioned on _σ_2) for β is β|σ2∼Normal(μ,σ2Ω). As above, we include _σ_2 in the prior variance to account for the scale of the response. Typically the prior is centered around zero, and so from here on we set μ\=0. This prior, combined with the likelihood Y|β,σ2∼Normal(X β,σ2In), gives posterior

β|Y,σ2∼Normal\[(XTX+Ω−1)−1X TY,σ2(XTX+Ω−1)−1\],(4.25)

which is a proper distribution as long as the prior is proper (Ω is positive definite). As with the Jeffreys prior, if _σ_2 is unknown and has a conjugate inverse gamma prior, the the marginal posterior of β follows a Student's t-distribution.

There are several choices for the prior covariance matrix Ω. The independent prior in [Section 4.2.2.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2_2_2) is obtained by taking Ω as the diagonal matrix with diagonal elements {ω2,τ2,...,τ2}. The variance component _τ_2 can either be fixed at a large value or given a prior distribution, such as the _R_2 prior of \[[172](./19-ref01.md#refbib172)\] and \[[168](./19-ref01.md#refbib168)\]. Alternatively, the form of the posterior simplifies under Zellner's g-prior \[[171](./19-ref01.md#refbib171)\] β|σ2∼Normal\[0,σ2g(XTX)−1\] for g\>0. The conditional posterior is then

β|Y,σ2∼Normal\[cβ^LS,cσ2(XTX )−1\],(4.26)

where c\=1/(g+1)∈(0,1) is the shrinkage factor. This speeds up computation because β^LS and (XTX)−1 can be computed once outside the MCMC sampler. The shrinkage factor _c_ determines how strongly the posterior mean and covariance are shrunk towards zero. A common choice is g\=1/n and thus c\=n/(n+1). Since Fisher's information matrix for the Gaussian distribution is the inverse covariance matrix, the prior contributes 1/nth the information as the likelihood, and so this prior is called the unit information prior \[[88](./19-ref01.md#refbib88)\].

### 4.2.4 Linear regression prediction

One use of linear regression is to make a prediction for a new set of covariates, Xpred\=(X1pred,...,Xppred). Given the model parameters, the distribution of the new response is 134\. To properly account for parametric uncertainty, we should use the posterior predictive distribution ([Section 1.5](./09-chapter1.md#sec1_5)) that averages over the uncertainty in β and _σ_2. MCMC provides a means to sample from the PPD by making a sample from the predictive distribution for each of the s\=1,...,S MCMC samples of the parameters, Y(s)|β(s),σ2(s)∼Normal(∑j\=1pXjpredβj(s),σ2(s)), and using the _S_ draws Y(1),...,Y(S) to approximate the PPD. The PPD is then summarized the same way as other posterior distributions, such as by the posterior mean and 95% interval. Similar approaches can be used to analyze missing data as in [Section 7.1.4](./15-chapter7.md#sec7_1_4).

## 4.3 Generalized linear models

Multiple linear regression assumes that the response variable is Gaussian and thus the mean response can be any real number. Many analyses do not conform to this assumption. For example, in [Section 4.3.1](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_3_1) we analyze binary responses with support {0,1} and in [Section 4.3.3](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_3_3) we analyze count data with support {0,1,2,...}. Clearly these data are not Gaussian, and their mean cannot be any real number because the mean must be between zero and one for binary data and positive for count data. The generalized linear model (GLM) extends linear regression concepts to handle these non-Gaussian outcomes. There is a deep theory of GLMs (exponential families, canonical links, etc; see \[[103](./19-ref01.md#refbib103)\]), but here we focus only on casting the GLM in the Bayesian framework through a few examples.

136\. Figure on this page. 137\. The basic steps in selecting a GLM are (1) determine the support of the response and select an appropriate parametric family and (2) link the covariates to the parameters that define this family. As an example, consider the Gaussian linear regression model in [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2). If the support of the response is (−∞,∞), a natural parametric family is the Gaussian distribution. Of course, there are other families with this support, and the fit of the Gaussian family to the data should be verified empirically. Once the Gaussian family has been selected, the covariates must be linked to one or both of the parameters, i.e., the mean or the variance. Let the linear combination of the covariates be

ηi\=∑j\=1pXijβj.(4.27)

The linear predictor _η_ _i_ can take any value in (−∞,∞) depending on Xij. Therefore, to link the covariates with the mean, we can simply set E(Yi)\=ηi as in standard linear regression. To complete the standard model we elect not to link the covariates with the variance, and simply set Var(Yi)\=σ2 for all _i_.

The function that links the linear predictor with a parameter is called the link function. Say that the parameter in the likelihood for the response is _θ_ _i_ (e.g., E(Yi)\=θi or Var(Yi)\=θi); then the link function _g_ is

g(θi)\=ηi.(4.28)

The link function must be an invertible function that is well-defined for all permissible values of the parameter. For example, in the Gaussian case the link function for the mean is the identity function g(x)\=x so that the mean can be any real number. To link the covariates to the variance we must ensure that the variance is positive, and so natural-log function g(x)\=log(x) is more appropriate. Link functions are not unique, and must be selected by the user. For example, the link function for the mean could be replaced by g(x)\=x3, and the link function for the variance could be replaced with g(x)\=log10(x).

Bayesian fitting of a GLM requires selecting the prior and computing the posterior distribution. The priors for the regression coefficients discussed for Gaussian data in [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2) can be applied for GLMs. If the number of observations is much larger than the number of covariates, uninformative normal prior distributions can be selected for the _β_ _j_. Shrinkage prior distributions βj|τ2∼Normal(0,τ2) with prior distribution assigned to _τ_2 are more stable with a large number of covariates. The prior for _τ_2 can be the conditionally conjugate inverse gamma distribution or the more flexible generalized beta prime prior in \[[168](./19-ref01.md#refbib168)\] (via the R package r2d2glmm) that leads to a beta distribution for the model _R_2 as in [Section 4.2.2.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2_2_2). The posterior distributions for GLMs are usually too complicated to derive the posterior in closed-form and prove that, say, a particular prior distribution leads to a Student's t-posterior distribution with interpretable mean and covariance. However, much of the intuition developed with Gaussian linear models carries over to GLMs.

With non-Gaussian responses the full conditional distributions for the _β_ _j_ are usually not conjugate, and Metropolis sampling must be used. Maximum likelihood estimates can be used as initial values, and the corresponding standard errors can suggest appropriate candidate distributions. In the examples in this chapter we use JAGS to carry out the MCMC sampling. R also has dedicated packages for Bayesian GLMs, such as the MCMClogit package for logistic regression ([Section 4.3.1](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_3_1)) that are likely faster than JAGS. With flat priors and a large sample it is even more efficient to invoke the Bayesian central limit theorem ([Section 3.1.3](./11-chapter3.md#sec3_1_3)) and approximate the posterior as Gaussian (e.g., using the glm function in R) and avoid MCMC altogether.138\. 

### 4.3.1 139\. Binary outcome

Binary outcomes Yi∈{0,1} occur frequently when the result of an experiment is recorded only as an indicator of a success or failure. For example, in [Section 1.2](./09-chapter1.md#sec1_2) the response was the binary indicator that a test for HIV was positive. Binary variables must follow a Bernoulli distribution. The Bernoulli distribution has one parameter, the success probability Prob(Yi\=1)\=qi∈\[0,1\]. Since the parameter is a probability, the link function must have input range \[0,1\] and output range \[−∞,∞\]. Below we discuss two such link functions: logistic and probit.

**Logistic regression**: The logistic link function is

g(q)\=logit(q)\=log(q1−q).(4.29)

This link function converts the event probability _q_ first to the odds of the event, q/(1−q)\>0, and then to the log odds, which can be any real number. The logistic regression model is written

Yi∼indepBernoulli(qi) and logit(qi)\=ηi\=∑j\=1JXijβj.(4.30)

The inverse logistic (expit) function is g−1(x)\=exp(x)/\[1+exp(x)\] and so the model can also be expressed as Yi∼indepBernoulli(exp(ηi)/\[1+exp(ηi)\]). JAGS code for this model is in [Listing 4.8a](./12-chapter4.md#list4_8).

Because the log odds of the event that Yi\=1 are linear in the covariates, _β_ _j_ is interpreted as the increase in the log odds corresponding to an increase of one in _X_ _j_ with all other covariates held fixed. Similarly, with all other covariates held fixed, increasing _X_ _j_ by one multiplies the odds by exp(βj). Therefore if βj\=2.3, increasing _X_ _j_ by one multiplies the odds by ten, and if βj\=−2.3, increasing _X_ _j_ by one divides the odds by ten. This interpretation is convenient for communicating the results and specifying priors. If a change of one in the covariate is deemed a large change, then a standard normal prior may have sufficient spread to represent an uninformative prior. The prior for the regression coefficients can also be evaluated on the induced prior for the success probabilities, _q_ _i_. [Figure 4.4](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_4) shows that prior standard deviation 10 gives a prior for _q_ _i_ that has most of its mass on zero and one, which may be unrealistic in many problems. This is an example of prior predictive checks, which will be discussed in [Section 6.5](./14-chapter6.md#sec6_5)

**Probit regression**: There are many possible link functions from \[0,1\] to \[−∞,∞\]. In fact, the quantile function (inverse CDF) of any continuous random variable with support \[−∞,∞\] would suffice. The link function in logistic regression is the quantile function of the logistic distribution. In probit regression, the link function is the Gaussian quantile function

Yi∼indepBernoulli(qi) and qi\=Φ(ηi),(4.31)

where Φ is the standard normal CDF ([Listing 4.8b](./12-chapter4.md#list4_8)).

Unfortunately, the regression coefficients _β_ _j_ in probit regression do not have a nice interpretation such as their log-odds interpretation in logistic regression. However, probit regression is useful because it leads to Gibbs sampling and can be used to model dependence between binary variables. Probit regression is equivalent to specifying latent variables Zi∼indepNormal(ηi,σ2) and assuming that observing Yi\=1 indicates that _Z_ _i_ exceeds the threshold _z_. For example, _Z_ _i_ might represent a patient's blood pressure, and _Y_ _i_ is the corresponding binary indicator that the patient has high blood pressure (defined as exceeding 140\. _z_). In this example, the probability that the patient has high blood pressure is

Prob(Yi\=1)\=Prob(Zi\>z)\=Prob\[(Zi−ηi)/σ\>(z−ηi)/σ\]\=1−Φ\[(z−ηi)/σ\]\=Φ\[(ηi−z)/σ\]\=Φ\[(∑j\=1JXijβj−z)/σ\].

Since we never observe the latent variables _Z_ _i_, we cannot estimate the threshold _z_ or the variance _σ_2. The threshold _z_ is coupled with the intercept because adding a constant to the threshold and subtracting the same constant from the intercept does not affect the event probabilities _q_ _i_. Therefore, the threshold is typically set to z\=0. Similarly, multiplying by _σ_ and dividing the slopes _β_ _j_ by the same constant do not affect the event probabilities, and so the variance is typically set to σ2\=1. This gives the usual probit regression model qi\=Φ(ηi).

In this formulation, the regression coefficients β have conjugate full conditional distributions conditioned on the latent _Z_ _i_. This leads to Gibbs sampling if the _Z_ _i_ are imputed at each MCMC iteration \[[5](./19-ref01.md#refbib5)\]. Also, dependence between binary outcomes _Y_ _i_ can be induced by a multivariate normal model for the latent variables _Z_ _i_.

### 4.3.2 Categorical outcomes

Logistic and probit regression can be generalized to model categorical data with more than two possible outcomes. Categorical data might arise if a survey asks customers which 141\. of R\=4 products they prefer, and their response is coded as Yi∈{1,...,R}, or how strongly they agree with a statement (e.g., “strongly”, “very strongly”, etc.), again coded as Yi∈{1,...,R}. Models differ depending on whether the response levels are ordered (strength of agreement) or unordered (product choice), but in either case the main objectives are to determine how covariates Xi affect the likelihood of each response and make predictions.

A natural model for unordered responses is the multinomial logistic regression model,

Prob(Yi\=r|β)\=exp(Xiβr)∑k\=1Rexp(Xiβk)(4.32)

for β\=(β1,...,βR). In this model, each of the _R_ levels has a different set of regression parameters, βr, that determine how the covariates influence the probability of response _r_. The linear predictors Xiβr are exponentiated to ensure the probabilities are positive and the sum in the denominator ensures that ∑r\=1RProb(Yi\=r|β)\=1, giving a valid PMF for all Xi and β.

This formulation maintains the log-odds interpretation that

log{Prob(Yi\=r|β)Prob(Yi\=s|β)}\=Xi(βr−βs).

As demonstrated by the log-odds above, only the differences between the regression coefficients are estimable. To avoid overparameterization, one of the _R_ responses, say r\=1, is set as the baseline with β1\=0. With this restriction, the log-odds of category _r_ compared to the baseline is log{Prob(Yi\=r|β)Prob(Yi\=1|β)}\=Xiβr, and thus βr is interpreted as in logistic regression with the caveat that all comparisons are with respect to the baseline response (often chosen as the most common response). In fact, if there are only R\=2 categories with Yi\=1 a failure and Yi\=2 a success, then the multinomial logistic regression model reduces to the standard logistic regression model for binary data.

Ordinal data where Yi\=r (agree) is in some sense between Yi\=r−1 (disagree) and Yi\=r+1 (strongly agree) can be modeled as a sequence of logistic regressions. The sequence of logistic regressions mimics the respondent first deciding whether to give the first response; if not they decide whether to give the second, etc. Mathematically, the first logistic regression is the probability of selecting the first category versus all others log{Prob(Yi\=1|β)/Prob(Yi\>1|β)}\=Xiβ1. The second logistic regression is fit only using data from observations with Yi\>1 and is log{Prob(Yi\=2|β)/Prob(Yi\>2|β)}\=Xiβ2. This is repeated until all R−1 decisions have been made giving parameters β1,...,βR−1. For both the ordered and unordered logistic regression models, any of the priors for standard logistic regression discussed in Chapter 4.3.1 can be applied, independently across the βr. If _R_ is large, then it may be beneficial to treat the _β_ _r_ as random effects following the methods in Chapter 4.4.

Probit regression can also be applied to categorical data. One approach for ordinal data is to assume that there is a latent continuous variable Zi|β∼Normal(Xiβ,1) for each observation, and the response is determined by this latent value. In this model, if there are _R_ categories, there are R+1 breakpoints L0<L1<...<LR with L0\=−∞ and LR\=∞, and Yi\=k if Lk−1<Zi<Lk. This is visualized in [Figure 4.5](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_5) where R\=4 and the shaded probability is Prob(Yi\=3|β)\=Prob(L2<Zi<L3|β).

![A Gaussian density curve is plotted over Z from about minus 5 to 5. Three vertical lines labelled L one L two and L three mark fixed Z values. The appeal of this model is that it connects categorical data analysis back to the well-understood Gaussian linear regression. For instance, say _Z_ _i_ is a continuous measure of a respondent's view on a topic; then β summarizes the covariate effects on this continuous measure. However, the _Z_ _i_ are not observed; rather the respondent is asked to rate the topic as strongly unfavorable, unfavorable, favorable or strongly favorable, and these responses are coded as Yi∈{1,...,4}.

142\. The probit regression model for categorical data is conducive to MCMC computing. They should be given priors so that Lk<Lk+1 for all _k_, and one of the cutpoints must be fixed, e.g., L1\=0, because increasing the intercept in β and increasing all the cutpoints by the same amount gives the same bin probabilities (i.e., in [Figure 4.5](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_5) the x-axis would be shifted but the area of the shaded region would not be affected). ### 4.3.3 Count outcomes

Random variables with support Yi∈{0,1,2,...} often arise as the number of events that occur in a time interval or spatial region. For example, in [Section 2.1](./10-chapter2.md#sec2_1) we analyze the number of NFL concussions by season. In this chapter, we will focus on modeling the mean as a function of the covariates. The link between the linear predictor and the mean must ensure that E(Yi)\=λi≥0. A natural link function is the log link,

log(λi)\=∑j\=1pXijβj.(4.33)

In this model, the mean is multiplied by exp(βj) if _X_ _j_ increases by one with all other covariates remaining fixed. Unlike binary data, specifying the mean does not completely determine the likelihood for count data. We discuss below two families of distributions for the likelihood function: Poisson and negative binomial.

143\. **Poisson regression**: The Poisson regression model is

Yi|λi∼indepPoisson(λi) where λi\=exp(∑j\=1pXijβj).(4.34)

As with logistic regression, the regression coefficients β1,...,βp can be given the priors discussed in [Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2), and Metropolis sampling can be used to explore the posterior ([Listing 4.8c](./12-chapter4.md#list4_8)). A critical assumption of the Poisson model is that the mean and variance are equal, that is,

E(Yi)\=Var(Yi)\=λi.(4.35)

The distribution of a count is over-dispersed (under-dispersed) if its variance is greater than (less than) its mean. If over-dispersion is present, then the Poisson model is inappropriate and another model should be considered so that the posterior accurately reflects all sources of variability.

**Negative binomial regression**: One approach to accommodating over-dispersion is to incorporate gamma random variables ei∼iidGamma(m,m) with mean 1 and variance 1/m. Then

Yi|ei,λi,m∼indepPoisson(λiei).(4.36)

Marginally over _e_ _i_, _Y_ _i_ follows the negative binomial distribution,

Yi|λi,m∼NegBinomial(qi,m)(4.37)

with probability qi\=m/(λi+m) and size _m_. The size _m_ need not be an integer, but if it is and we envision a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials each with success probability _q_ _i_, then _Y_ _i_ can be interpreted as the number of failures that occur before the mth success. More importantly for handling over-dispersion is that E(Yi)\=λi and Var(Yi)\=λi+λi2/m\>λi. The parameter _m_ controls over-dispersion. If _m_ is large, then ei≈1, and the model reduces to the Poisson regression model with Var(Yi)≈E(Yi); if _m_ is close to zero, then _e_ _i_ has large variance, and thus Var(Yi)\>E(Yi). The over-dispersion parameter can be given a gamma prior, as in [Listing 4.8d](./12-chapter4.md#list4_8).

**Zero-inflated Poisson**: Another common deviation from the Poisson distribution is an excess of zeros. For example, say that _Y_ _i_ is the number of fish caught by visitor _i_ to a state park. It may be that most of the _Y_ _i_ are zero because only a small proportion (say _p_) of the visitors fished, but the distribution of _Y_ _i_ for those that did fish is Poisson with mean _λ_. The probability of an observation being zero is then 1−p for the non-fishers plus _p_ times the Poisson probability at zero for the fishers. The PMF corresponding to this scenario is

f(y|p,λ)\={(1−p)+pfP(0|λ)if y\=0pfP(y|λ)if y\>0(4.38)

where _f_ _P_ is the Poisson PMF. This is equivalent to the two-stage model

Yi|Zi,λ∼Poisson(Ziλ) and Zi∼ Bernoulli(p)

where _Z_ _i_ is the latent indicator that visitor _i_ fished and thus the mean of _Y_ _i_ is zero for non-fishers with Zi\=0. In this scenario, we do not observe the _Z_ _i_, but this two-stage model gives the equivalent model to (4.38) but uses only standard distributions. As a result, the model can be coded in JAGS with covariates included in the mass at zero and the Poisson rate as in [Listing 4.8e](./12-chapter4.md#list4_8).144\.

## 4.4 Random effects

The standard linear regression model assumes all observations are independent. This assumption is tenuous if data are collected in groups. For example, [Figure 4.8](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_8) plots the jaw bone density measurements of n\=20 children measured over the course of m\=4 visits. Analyzing data from this repeated measures design requires accounting for likely correlation between subsequent observations on the same child. This section introduces the concept of a random effect to account for this and other settings with potential dependence between observations. The random effects methods introduced here are a preview of hierarchical modeling ([Section 7.1](./15-chapter7.md#sec7_1)), which is one of the most powerful applications of the Bayesian framework.

[Return to text.⏎](chapter4)

These 20 children represent only a random sample from a much larger population, but we can use these samples to make an inference about the larger population. If we let Yij be the jth measurement for patient _i_, then (ignoring age as in the top right panel of [Figure 4.8](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_8)) the one-way random effects model is

Yij|αi∼indepNormal(αi,σ2) where αi∼iidNormal(μ,τ2).(4.41)

The random effect _α_ _i_ is the true mean for patient _i_, and the observations for patient _i_ vary around _α_ _i_ with variance _σ_2. The _α_ _i_ are called random effects because if we repeated the experiment with a new sample of 20 children, the _α_ _i_ would change. In this model, the population of patient-specific means is assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean _μ_ and variance _τ_2.

For the one-way random effects model, marginally over the random effects, the mean is E(Yij)\=μ for all observations, and the variance of the response is the sum of the random effect and error variances, Var(Yij)\=τ2+σ2. The proportion of the variance attributed to the random effects is

R2\=Var(αi)Var(Yij)\=τ2τ2+σ2.

Marginalizing over the random effects induces correlation between observations from the same group. The marginal covariance is

Cov(Yij,Yuv)\={τ2+σ2i\=u and j\=vτ2i\=u and j≠v0i≠u.(4.42)

Since two observations from the same patient share a common random effect, they have covariance _τ_2 and thus correlation τ2/(τ2+σ2). Observations from different patients have no common source of variability and are thus uncorrelated.

The generalized beta-prime (GBP) distribution has been proposed as a generalization of the half-Cauchy prior \[[10](./19-ref01.md#refbib10), [91](./19-ref01.md#refbib91), [168](./19-ref01.md#refbib168), [172](./19-ref01.md#refbib172)\]. In Chapter 4.2, the beta-prime (BP) prior τ2∼BP(a,b) was used in linear regression to induce a Beta(a,b) prior on the model _R_2. With small _a_ and _b_ this gives an uninformative prior for the overall model fit. As discussed below, the BP prior for random effects variance has a similarly appealing interpretation.

The GBP distribution extends the BP distribution by including a power parameter, _c_, and scale parameter, _d_. If X∼BP(a,b) then

X∗\=dX1/c∼GBP(a,b,c,d)

with c\=d\=1 reducing to the beta-prime distribution X∗∼BP(a,b). The flexibility of the power parameter allows the variance and standard deviation parameterizations to stay in the same family because τ2∼GBP(a,b,c,d) is equivalent to τ∼GPD(a,b,2c,d1/2). The Half-Cauchy prior is a special case with a\=b\=1/2 and c\=1; that is, τ∼HC(σ) is equivalent to τ2∼GBP(1/2,1/2,1,σ). However, as shown in [Figure 4.9](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_9) the BP prior, and thus the GBP prior, includes a wider range of shapes. For example, the PDF at zero can be zero like the inverse gamma, finite like the half-Cauchy or infinite.

**Marginal models**: Inducing correlation by conditioning on random effects is equivalent to a marginal model ([Section 4.6](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_6)) that does not include random effects but directly specifies correlation between observations in the same group. For example, the one-way random effects model

Yij|αi∼indepNormal(αi,σ2) where αi∼iidNormal(μ,τ2)(4.44)

is equivalent to the marginal model

Yi∼Normal(μ,Σ),(4.45)

where Yi\=(Yi1,...,Yim)T is the data vector for group _i_, μ\=(μ,...,μ)T is the mean vector, and Σ is the covariance matrix with τ2+σ2 on the diagonals and _τ_2 elsewhere. An advantage 153\. of the marginal approach is that we no longer have to estimate the random effects _α_ _i_; a disadvantage is that for large data sets and complex correlation structure the mean vector and especially the covariance matrix can be large, which slows computation.

In the hierarchical representation, the elements of Yi are independent and identically distributed given _α_ _i_; in the marginal model the _m_ observations from group _i_ are no longer independent, but they remain exchangeable; i.e., their distribution is invariant to permuting their order. The concept of exchangeability plays a fundamental role in constructing hierarchical models. The representation theorem by Bruno de Finetti states that any infinite sequence of exchangeable variables can be written as independent and identically distributed conditioned on some latent distribution. Therefore, this important type of dependent data can be modeled using a simpler hierarchical model.

Bayesian random effects models can also describe structured (non-exchangeable) correlation as in Chapter 4.6\. For example, observations made over space and time may exhibit correlation that decays with distance in space or lag in time.

## 4.5 Generalized linear mixed models

Multiple linear regression ([Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2)) estimates the relationships between variables under the assumption of independence between observations. The regression coefficients (β) are the parameters of interest and apply to all observations in the analysis and for prediction for other observations from the same population. In this sense, these parameters are _fixed effects_. Random effects ([Section 4.4](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_4)) allow for different parameters (_α_ _i_) in different subpopulations and can be used to capture dependence. Fixed and random effects can of course be combined in common model, which is then referred to as a linear mixed model. In this chapter we introduce the linear mixed model for Gaussian data and the generalized linear mixed model for non-Gaussian data.

As we will see, the same algorithms (e.g., Gibbs sampling) can be used for mixed models as for the other models we have considered. In fact, computationally there is no need to distinguish between fixed and random effects (which can lead to confusion, \[[76](./19-ref01.md#refbib76)\]). Conceptually, however, fixed and random effects are distinct because fixed effects describe the population, and a random effect describes an individual from the population. A common source of confusion is that fixed effects are treated as random variables in a Bayesian analysis. However, as with all parameters, the prior and posterior distributions for fixed effects reflect subjective uncertainty about the true values of these fixed but unknown parameters, and not that they are a random sample from a larger population.

**Linear mixed model**: The linear mixed model extends the multiple regression model ([Section 4.2](./12-chapter4.md#sec4_2)) by adding random effects to account for clustering of the observations. The linear mixed model has Gaussian responses and both fixed and random effects. Returning to the bone density example in [Figure 4.8](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_8), the response Yij is the bone density for child i∈{1,...,20} at visit j∈{1,...,4}. There is an increasing trend in age, and so we may add a fixed effect for the child's age at visit _j_, Xij. There is also clearly clustering/dependence between the four observations for each child, so we include a random effect for child, giving a linear mixed model with the one-way random effect,

Yij|αi∼indepNormal(μ+Xijβ+αi,σ2) where αi∼iidNormal(0,τ2),(4.46)

where _μ_ is the intercept and _β_ is the fixed age trend. This model has the mean of the random-effect distribution equal zero, which is equivalent to removing _μ_ from the mean of Yij and assuming the mean of _α_ _k_ is _μ_.

154\. In this model, the age trend is fixed in that it applies to all children in this dataset and to all children in the population not included in the sample. The random effects _α_ _i_ each apply to only one child. If the objective of the study is to learn about the population, then the primary interest is not in the random effects for the children in the random sample, but rather to generalized to the distribution across the population, parameterized by _τ_2.

A more general formulation of the linear mixed model with one type of random effect is

Yi|β,α∼indepNormal(∑l\=1pXilβl+∑k\=1KZikαk,σ2)(4.47)

where αk∼iidNormal(0,τ2). The fixed component ∑l\=1pXilβl is identical to multiple linear regression. The random component is now determined by the Zik, which are often binary cluster indicators. The bone density model fits this format with Y1,...,Y80 being the observations for the 20 children and 4 visits in a single vector and Zik\=1 if observation _i_ comes from child _k_ and Zik\=0 otherwise. Writing the model this way allows it to be expressed in matrix notation, extending (4.19) by including the n×K matrix **Z** to give Y|β,α∼Normal(Xβ+Zα,σ2In).

The Bayesian linear mixed model is completed with priors for β, _τ_2 and _σ_2. Priors from previous sections can be adapted for the linear mixed model; e.g., the priors βj∼iidNormal(0,c2) and σ2,τ2∼iid InvGamma(a,b) for large _c_ and small _a_ and _b_ are conjugate and uninformative. The inverse gamma distributions can be replaced with Half-Cauchy or beta-prime distributions (Chapter 4.4). As discussed in \[[168](./19-ref01.md#refbib168)\], the beta-prime prior can induce a beta distribution on the overall model fit,

R2\=Var(∑j\=1pXijβj+∑k\=1KZikαk)Var(Yi).

If the covariates are standardized and βj∼Normal{0,q1σ2W/(p−1)} and τ2\=q2σ2W with q1+q2\=1, then the prior W∼BP(a,b) gives the prior R2∼Beta(a,b). The proportions (q1,q2) determine the relative contribution of the fixed and random effects and can be fixed or given a Dirichlet prior.

**Two-way random effects**: The model above assumes a linear time trend, which may be overly restrictive. A more flexible model includes a separate parameter for the mean response at each of the four time points, α21,...,α24,

Yij|αi∼indepNormal(μ+Xijβ+α1i+α2j,σ2)

where as before the child random effects are modeled as α1i∼iidNormal(0,τ12). Treating age as a random effect is questionable, as the four ages under consideration are not a random sample from a larger population of ages. One approach is to treat the α2j as fixed effects with uninformative prior, effectively including four dummy variables for visit number as covariates. This model is overparameterized as there are six parameters (_μ, β, α_21, …, αj4) to explain the population mean at the four ages, and so at least two parameters must be removed.

An alternative is to model the age indicators as α2j∼iidNormal(0,τ22). In this model, the α2j represent deviations from the linear trend, and the Gaussian priors add stability to avoid overfitting; i.e., small _τ_2 shrinks α2j≈0, and the time trend is approximately linear with slope _β_. This example illustrates how random effects can be used as a convenient modeling device in Bayesian models that do not have the classical interpretation of a random effects models \[[77](./19-ref01.md#refbib77)\].

155\. Random effects can also be used to capture complex dependence structure between observations. For the two-way random effects model with α2j∼iidNormal(0,τ22), the covariance is

Cov(Yij,Yuv)\={σ2+τ12+τ22i\=u and j\=vτ12i\=u and j≠vτ22i≠u and j\=v0i≠u and j≠v.(4.48)

Therefore, the random effects induce correlation τ12/(σ2+τ12+τ22) between observations for the same child at different visits and correlation τ22/(σ2+τ12+τ22) between observations for the different children at the same visit time.

**Random slopes model**: The previous models for the bone growth data allow each child to have a different intercept but assume the same trajectory across time for all children. However, [Figure 4.8](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_8) indicates that the rate of increase over time varies by child. Therefore we could model the child-specific slopes as random effects

Yij|αi∼indepNormal(αi1+αi2Xj,σ2) where αi∼iidNormal(β,Ω),(4.49)

and the random intercept and slope for patient _i_ is αi\=(αi1,αi2)T. The mean vector β includes the population mean intercept and slope, and is thus a fixed effect. The 2×2 population covariance matrix Ω determines the variation of the random effects over the population. To complete the Bayesian model we specify prior σ2∼InvGamma(0.1,0.1), β∼Normal(0,1002I2), and Ω∼InvWishart(3.1,I2/3.1). **Generalized linear mixed models**: Generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) include random effects to the GLMs of Chapter 4.3 in the natural way. Recall that for GLMs the mean (or other features of the distribution) response E(Yi) is related to the linear predictor 157\. _η_ _i_ via link function _g_ as g{E(Yi)}\=ηi. For instance, in logistic regression the binary response variable _Y_ _i_ was regression onto covariates Xi using the logistic link function logit{E(Yi)}\=ηi\=∑l\=1pXilβl. This link function ensures that the mean response is between zero and one for _η_ _i_.

The GLMM adds random effects to the linear predictor. For example, consider the one-way GLMM model where Yij is the ith observation in group _j_. The linear predictor g{E(Yij)}\=ηij includes both the fixed effect covariate Xij and random effect _α_ _j_

ηij\=μ+Xijβ+αj

with random effects distribution αj∼iidNormal(0,τ2). As with GLMs, different link function, are appropriate for different response types; e.g., if Yij is binary then we might use a logistic link function or if Yij is a count we might use a Poisson regression with log link function. Also, the linear mixed model is a special case of the GLMM with identity link function and Gaussian assumption for the response distribution.

The motivation for and interpretation of the random effects is the same for GLMMs as for Gaussian linear mixed models. That is, the distribution of random effects is interpreted as representing a variation across a population of sampling units. The statistical interest is either in the population parameter _τ_2, or sometimes in identifying extreme units in the sample with large or small _α_ _j_.

Random effects are also used to account for dependence between observations in the same cluster. Unlike in the Gaussian case, the correlation cannot be computed analytically, but the insight that shared random effects leads to dependence remains. To see this, consider the logistic model for binary data without fixed effects so logit{Prob(Yij\=1|αj)}\=αj. The mean is Prob(Yij\=1)\=0.5 marginally over the random effect for all _τ_. ## 4.6 Linear models with correlated data

Chapter 4.4, specifically (4.42), demonstrates that including random effects can capture dependence between observations. In this approach, we included random effects to the model, assumed conditional independence between the observations given the random effects, and 159\. Figure/Table on this page. 160\. studied the correlation induced by marginalizing over the random effects. Alternatively, correlation between observations can be modeled directly without including random effects. For example, rather than including random effects to capture dependence, the covariance model in (4.48) could be used directly, and equivalently, to model dependence. For data with a natural ordering such as spatial or temporal data, modeling the marginal correlation directly as a function of distance in space or time is often the more intuitive approach. As with random effects, there are many correlation models, and finding a reasonable one is important to obtain valid inference and make accurate predictions.

The Bayesian linear model with correlated errors is

Y|β∼Normal(Xβ,Σ).(4.52)

The mean structure **Xβ** is interpreted the same as in a linear model with independent errors; i.e., with all other covariates held fixed, increasing a covariate by one increases the mean by the corresponding element in β; however, the posterior of β can be dramatically affected by the choice of correlation (e.g., \[[78](./19-ref01.md#refbib78)\]). A Bayesian analysis of correlated data hinges on correctly specifying the correlation structure to capture, say, spatial or temporal correlation. Say observation _i_ is taken at spatial location (longitude/latitude) si. Then we might assume that the correlation between observations at locations si and sj decays with distance between locations, dij\=||si−sj||, as

Cor(Yi,Yj)\=C(dij)\=exp(−ϕdij)

where ϕ\>0 determines the rate of decay and is a parameter to be estimated. Then the (i,j) element of Σ is σ2C(dij), so _σ_2 is the variance of **Y**.

Rather than explicitly modeling the correlation between each pair of observations, the correlation is determined by the correlation function, _C_, which inputs a distance and outputs a correlation. The exponential correlation function is just one simple example, e.g., the squared exponential correlation function is C(d)\=exp(−ϕd2), which is popular in machine learning (Chapter 8.3.2). The correlation function is typically selected from a list of valid correlation functions that ensure than when used to populate Σ, Σ is guaranteed to by symmetric and positive definite. \[[38](./19-ref01.md#refbib38)\] provides a list of common spatial and temporal correlation functions.

Given the correlation structure and priors for the correlation parameters (_ϕ_ in the previous example), standard Bayesian computational tools can be used to summarize the posterior. Correlation parameters usually will not have conjugate priors, and so Metropolis–Hastings sampling is used. An advantage of the Bayesian approach for correlated data is that using MCMC sampling, we can account for uncertainty in the correlation parameters for prediction or inference on other parameters, whereas maximum likelihood analysis often uses plug-in estimates of the correlation parameters and thus underestimates uncertainty.

The assumption of independent residuals is questionable because neighboring states may be correlated. Spatial correlation may stem from guns being brought across state borders or from missing covariates (e.g., attitudes about and use of guns) that vary spatially. Research has shown that accounting for residual dependence can have a dramatic effect on regression coefficient estimates \[[78](./19-ref01.md#refbib78)\].

We decompose the residual covariance Cov\[(ε1,...,εn)T\]\=Σ as

Σ\=τ2S+σ2In,(4.54)

where τ2S is the spatial covariance and σ2In is the non-spatial covariance. There are many spatial correlation models (e.g., \[[11](./19-ref01.md#refbib11)\]) that allow the correlation between two states to decay with the distance between the states. For example, a common model is to assume the correlation between states decays exponentially with the distance between them. However, quantifying the distance between irregularly shaped states is challenging, and so we model spatial dependence using adjacencies. Let Aij\=1 if states _i_ and _j_ share a border and Aij\=0 if i\=j or the states are not neighbors. The spatial covariance follows the conditionally autoregressive model S\=(M−ρA)−1, where _A_ is the adjacency matrix with (i,j) element Aij and _M_ is the diagonal matrix with the ith diagonal element equal to the number of states that neighbor state _i_. The parameter ρ∈(0,1) is not the correlation between adjacent sites, but determines the strength of spatial dependence with ρ\=0 corresponding to independence.

162\. The posterior mean (standard deviation) of the spatial dependence parameter _ρ_ is 0.38 (0.25), and so the residual spatial dependence in these data is not strong. However, the posterior of the regression coefficient of interest in [Figure 4.12](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_12) is noticeably wider for the spatial model than the non-spatial model. The posterior probability that the coefficient is negative lowers from 0.96 under the non-spatial model to 0.93 for the spatial model. Therefore, while accounting for residual dependence did not qualitatively change the results, this example illustrates that the chosen model for the residuals can affect the posterior of the regression coefficients.

**Jaw bone density example**: A possible correlation structure for the longitudinal data in [Figure 4.8](./12-chapter4.md#fig4_8) (top left) is to assume that correlation decays with the time between visits. A first-order autoregression correlation structure is Cor(Yij,Yik)\=ρ|j−k|. Denoting the vector of _m_ observations for patient _i_ as Yi\=(Yi1,...,Yim)T, the m×m covariance matrix Σ has (j,k) element equal to σ2ρ|j−k|. The random slope model for the mean in matrix notation is E(Yi|αi)\=Xαi, where **X** is the m×2 matrix with the first column equal to the vector of ones for the intercept and the second column equal to the ages X1,...,Xm for the slope. The likelihood is then

Yi|αi∼indepNormal (Xαi,Σ)(4.55)

with random effects distribution αi∼iid Normal(β,Ω). The correlation parameter is given prior ρ∼Uniform(0,1) and all other priors are the same as other fits. JAGS code is given in [Listing 4.15](./12-chapter4.md#list4_15).

This model with random intercept, random slope and autoregressive correlation structure is now very complex.
